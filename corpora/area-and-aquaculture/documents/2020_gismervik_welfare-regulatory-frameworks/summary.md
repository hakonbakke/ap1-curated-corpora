### Background
Gismervik and colleagues compare Norwegian legal health and welfare frameworks for broiler chickens and farmed salmon. The stated aim is to improve salmon regulation and to show how unintended gaps arise when laws are written at different times. This is animal-health and welfare law. It is not a paper about where farms should sit.

The Animal Welfare Act (2010, replacing the 1974 animal-protection law) covers fish and terrestrial animals equally and gives animals intrinsic value independent of utility. Farmed fish, unlike other productions, also have the Aquaculture Act, whose aim is stated as promoting industry profitability inside the limits of sustainability. Norway produced 66 552 000 broilers in 2018 and over 872 631 000 farmed fish.

### Methods
The authors review the Food Act, the Animal Welfare Act, and the Aquaculture Act, the ministries and agencies that govern them, and 36 regulations based on those laws. They contrast daily care, mortality recording, welfare courses, slaughterhouse control of high-density production, breeding text, and light and noise rules. Cleaner fish are out of scope. A full slaughterhouse-rule comparison is also out of scope.

### Main findings
Chicken producers face two laws that the authors describe as governed by the same ministry family and supervised by the Food Safety Authority. Salmon producers must also follow the Aquaculture Act and deal with several ministries and agencies (Food Safety Authority, Directorate of Fisheries, county municipalities) with different aims. The Aquaculture Act has no direct health and welfare text except that farms can be ordered to move if environment and fish health require it. County municipalities are described as allocating licences (new sites, size changes) after consulting other bodies. That is institutional background, not an area-allocation analysis.

Salmon regulation is described as more complex, with potentially conflicting aims and less positive welfare phrasing. Daily securing of animals and mortality recording are less strict than for chicken. Chicken rules define mortality (including euthanasia) and ask for cause of death where possible. Salmon “increased mortality” is “significantly more than normal.” There is no specified governmental slaughterhouse control of high-density salmon production comparable to the broiler rules above 33 kg per m2. Welfare courses for chicken require approval and name infection prevention. Salmon courses must be repeated at least every fifth year. Neither rule requires senior company leaders to take a course.

The Animal Welfare Act has no dispensation paragraph. The Food Act does. The authors say future farmed-fish frameworks should avoid unintended downgrading of fish health and welfare. They recommend aligning courses, daily mortality practice, slaughterhouse control, welfare programmes, and light and noise rules, and they note that salmon decisions on infrastructure are often made centrally in large companies. Closing pages say welfare measures could inform licences, production changes, and localisation. That is a recommendation, not a siting study.

### Uncertainty and limitations
This is a legal comparison, not a study of welfare outcomes on farms or of how licences are decided. The snapshot reflects rules around 2019-2020. The incoming EU animal-health law is described as focused on disease prevention and as not covering welfare.

### Relevance to this corpus
Adjacent to area, not core. The same Aquaculture Act and county licence role that gate sites also omit welfare as a statutory aim. The paper does not analyse municipal coastal plans, locality clearance, or farm density.
