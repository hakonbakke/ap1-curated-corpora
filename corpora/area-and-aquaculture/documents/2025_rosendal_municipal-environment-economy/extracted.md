environment beat the economy?

A B S T R A C T
Salmon aquaculture belongs to Norway’s second largest and most profitable export industry after oil and gas 
production, but the industry is also riddled with environmental challenges that may restrict further growth. This 
article examines the positions of coastal municipalities on aquaculture expansions, being key actors in autho­
rizing new areas for salmon farming. A reasonable assumption is that municipalities with significant environ­
mental problems may be negative towards further growth. Yet, a long and varied scholarship on environmental 
politics shows that economic interests tend to trump concerns raised by environmental problems. We thus seek to 
investigate how environmental problems and dependency on aquaculture industry clusters affect Norwegian 
municipal positions on aquaculture expansion in their area planning. Selecting ten municipalities for in depth 
studies, we apply a qualitative comparative analysis approach to examine the proposition that municipalities 
hosting strong aquaculture industry clusters (AC) will be positive to expansion, regardless of the state of the 
environment. Our examinations largely support this proposition but also reveals nuances and deviant cases. We 
find that the effect of AC on positions is weaker in municipalities hosting large, externally based corporations 
than in those hosting smaller and locally entrenched companies. Furthermore, the effect of AC appears to be 
weak in larger and more economically diverse municipalities compared to smaller municipalities.
1. Introduction
Salmon aquaculture belongs to Norway’s second largest and most 
profitable export industry after oil and gas production, and accounts for 
more than 50 % of the global Atlantic salmon supply. Expansion of 
Norway’s ‘blue economy’ and particularly salmon farming industry has 
become a political vision for economic growth in the post fossil-fuel era 
(Reinertsen and Asdal, 2019; White Paper (Meld. St. 5), 2019-2020; 
NFD, 2021). Yet, lofty growth goals have also been accompanied by 
widespread concerns for the environmental problems that have surged 
because of more intensive salmon farming (White Paper (Meld. St. 16), 
2014–2015; White Paper (Meld. St. 5), 2019-2020; NFD, 2021; Nor­
wegian Veterinary Institute, 2021; Sommerset et al., 2022; NOU, 2023). 
Environmental problems related to salmon aquaculture include the 
transfer of parasitic salmon lice from salmon farms to migrating wild 
salmon juveniles—posing a threat to wild salmon populations and other 
salmonids like trout and char (Larsen and Vormedal, 2020; Nilsen et al., 
2021; Vollset et al., 2023)—as well as farm salmon escapees from open- 
net sea farms, which may enter rivers to spawn and lead to genetic 
introgression and maladaptation in wild populations (Diserud et al., 
2022). Furthermore, on-site pollution from waste matter and effluents 
may have significant negative ecological impacts (Grefsrud et al., 2022; 
Kutti et al., 2022; Legrand et al., 2022). Over the past decade, it has 
therefore become widely acknowledged that aquaculture industry 
expansion must be “environmentally sustainable” (White Paper (Meld. 
St. 16), 2014–2015; White Paper (Meld. St. 5), 2019-2020; NOU, 2023).
Growth in salmon production volumes is primarily regulated at the 
national level through the “Traffic Light” management system (TLS). 
The TLS assigns a high, moderate, or low risk of wild salmon mortality 
due to louse-infestations to different production areas (PAs). It allows 
companies located in low-risk PAs to increase production capacity on a 
biannual basis (Hersoug, 2022; Vollset et al., 2021). However, national 
growth permits must also be granted a localization permit from the 
County Authority—that is, the approval of a physical farm site at which 
salmon production can be expanded or carried out. While the County 
Authority represents the coordinating agency in localization decisions,

which also involve a range of sectoral authorities, municipalities are 
granted responsibility for coastal area planning and development 
through Norway’s Planning and Building Act (PBA). Municipalities are 
thus authorized to restrict or expand specific coastal areas for salmon 
farming activities in the land-use section of their municipal master plans 
(Hersoug and Johnsen, 2012). This local authority makes the positions 
of coastal municipalities on whether to authorize or hinder the estab­
lishment of new areas for salmon aquaculture an interesting case for 
studying trade-offs between environmental problems and industry 
growth.
There are 145 out of 276 Norwegian coastal municipalities that host 
salmonid farming and related supply-chain activities according to the 
Aquaculture Registry (2024).1 Coastal municipalities often benefit 
economically from hosting aquaculture businesses: Both the industry 
and the Aquaculture Fund2 provide substantial revenues and local 
employment, including from farming, supply-chain services and activ­
ities, and ripple effects from technology innovation and equipment 
manufacturing, processing, and sales (Bergesen and Tveterås, 2019). We 
conceptualize and define such local aquaculture industry and related 
businesses as “aquaculture industry clusters” (AC). To the extent that 
ACs provide sizeable economic benefits for coastal municipalities they 
may be inclined to support salmon farming expansions and thus seek to 
authorize new areas for aquaculture in their municipal master 
plans—even when environmental problems persist. On the other hand, 
there is also broad political agreement that aquaculture growth must be 
environmentally sustainable, and that growth should be restricted when 
environmental problems such as high sea louse levels are prominent 
(White Paper (Meld. St. 16), 2014–2015; White Paper (Meld. St. 5), 
2019-2020; NOU, 2023). Thus, the weight of environmental problems 
may also influence municipal positions on expansions of salmon farming 
in their areas. Against this backdrop, we seek to investigate the 
following research question: How do environmental problems and 
aquaculture industry clusters, separately and in combination, affect 
Norwegian municipal positions on aquaculture expansion?
Our study addresses attitudes to aquaculture at the municipal level. 
Previous research has examined the attitudes of the local communities 
and stakeholder groups towards salmon aquaculture production 
(Bjørkan and Eilertsen, 2020; Rybråten et al., 2018; Lindland et al., 
2019) and compared the attitudes of residents to that of general publics 
(Krøvel et al., 2019). Recent studies find evidence that social acceptance 
depends on environmental sustainability, trust in industry as well as the 
governance system, and how revenues are distributed (Eriksen and 
Mikkelsen, 2024; Olsen et al., 2024; Misund et al., 2023). To comple­
ment these studies, we provide a systematic study of how coastal mu­
nicipalities position themselves towards local aquaculture expansions in 
the context of growing environmental challenges. Earlier studies of 
municipalities have focused on general legal and management frame­
works and various forms of interest conflicts related to coastal planning 
and area-use (Fauchald, 2020; Hersoug and Johnsen, 2012; Hersoug, 
2013; Hauge and Stokke, 2021; Hersoug et al., 2021; Mikkelsen et al., 
2019; Aanesen and Mikkelsen, 2019; Hersoug et al., 2019; Myklebust, 
2018; Sønvisen and Vik, 2021). However, these have paid little attention 
to environment-economy trade-offs at the local level. Instead, the bulk of 
research on the conditions for environmentally sustainable growth has 
tended to focus on the national level, or to take a top–down perspective 
(Vormedal, 2017; Greaker et al., 2020; Larsen and Vormedal, 2020; 
Aarstad et al., 2023).
Here, we study the positions of Norwegian coastal municipalities on 
local expansion of areas for salmon farming, which remains an under­
explored area of research. Based on pre-screening to ensure variation in 
positions (see below) we select 10 coastal municipalities for empirical 
examination and investigate what factors may account for variation in 
their positions. In so doing, we aim to advance knowledge on how the 
municipal level perceives and handles potential trade-offs between 
economic development and environmental problems, drawing on liter­
ature that have studied social attitudes to aquaculture more generally 
(Hynes et al., 2018; Kaltenborn et al., 2017; Krøvel et al., 2019; Eriksen 
and Mikkelsen, 2024). Considering the important role of municipalities, 
examining their positions on aquaculture expansion more specifically 
from an environmental sustainability perspective, this study offers 
relevant results to scholars, policy makers, practitioners, and other 
stakeholders.
We start with presenting an analytical framework for examining and 
assessing municipal positions on aquaculture expansion, followed by a 
thorough explanation of our methodology for making these assessments. 
We proceed to examine how municipalities stand on aquaculture 
expansion and explain variation and change in positions. We discuss 
under what conditions environmental challenges may trump local eco­
nomic dependency on AC.
2. Analytical framework
Our focus of explanation is municipal positions on aquaculture 
expansion. ‘Positions’ are assessed according to two indicators: First, 
based on research interviews, we refer to the position expressed by 
mainly administrative representatives of the municipalities on whether 
they seek to authorize or hinder further expansions. Second, we assess 
the land-use elements of municipal master plans (including proposals for 
master plan revisions) to establish whether the municipality seeks to 
authorize new coastal areas for aquaculture. Decisions on land use 
cannot be used alone as an indicator mainly because the timing of 
municipal decisions on area-use for aquaculture can vary greatly. Often, 
they manifest in municipal “area plans” that can span 12-year periods, 
which make such decisions hard to compare. Focusing on positions 
expressed by local administrative representatives thus allows for more 
valid comparison of municipal stances even while they are at different 
stages of their area-planning processes. Focusing on area-planning al­
lows us to isolate and establish the stance of municipal actors.
Many factors may influence municipal positions on aquaculture 
expansion, including economic, political, institutional and social factors. 
In this study we focus on two main explanatory variables: i) local envi­
ronmental problems) and ii) the strength of aquaculture industry clusters 
(AC) in municipalities. These factors resemble a classic goal conflict 
between two legitimate concerns.
Local environmental problems may as noted include a range of 
challenges from aquaculture, such as toxic and feed residues, impacts on 
the seabed, benthic fauna and vicinity, farm- and wild-fish interactions, 
impacts on wild-species spawning grounds, shrimp fields, salmon fjords 
and protected areas, and coastal water condition. Some of these prob­
lems are regulated based on various forms of environmental impact 
assessments (Agnalt et al., 2024), while salmon lice are regulated by 
maximum thresholds for the average number of sea lice per fish at each 
site.3 Additionally, to allow for further growth, the cumulative impact of 
salmon lice within production areas must have a low impact on wild 
salmon mortality, according to the Traffic Light System.4 In the meth­
odology section we apply data from various types of environmental 
assessments to develop five indicators to establish the state of (marine) 
environmental problems.
The national government pursues an evidence and knowledge-based 
management of aquaculture to ensure that any further growth is envi­
ronmentally sustainable (White Paper (Meld. St. 16), 2014–2015; White 
Paper (Meld. St. 5), 2019-2020; NOU, 2023). Thus, to the extent that

municipalities access and use information about negative environmental 
problems in their areas, it is reasonable to expect that such knowledge 
can lead municipalities to refrain from allowing further expansion in 
area planning. This implies that municipalities experiencing significant 
environmental problems from aquaculture are not likely to favour expansion, 
or vice versa.
Our second major explanatory variable is the strength of aquaculture 
industry clusters (AC) in the municipality, which may be conceptualized 
as degree of economic dependency on salmon farming and related 
business activities (Afewerki et al., 2023; Asche et al., 2016). We use the 
following criteria to assess the strength of ACs: locally dominant com­
panies, income from the Aquaculture Fund (AF), and localization of 
headquarters. A strong AC equals local presence of companies, high 
income from AF and localization of headquarters in the community.
These three criteria are based on the following: First, Fairfield (2015)
suggests that companies with a dominant position in local communities 
often provide employment and revenues for local institutions, and often 
fund various local needs, such as schools, playgrounds, and cultural 
activities. The related cluster of aquaculture businesses is also likely to 
have ripple effects beyond direct jobs in salmon farming, including in 
technology innovation and development, processing and sales, and 
other supply services.
Second, municipalities have substantial incomes from the Aquacul­
ture Fund, which re-distribute national revenues from industry growth 
and from taxes on production of salmonids to the municipal sector. In 
2022 transfers from the government’s Aquaculture Fund to municipal­
ities amounted to £250 million (3 billion NOK5).
Finally, localization of headquarters is also likely to be linked to the 
local identity (Eisenhauer et al., 2000) and make the community a more 
attractive place to live and work, providing local tax revenues and act as 
a buffer against urbanisation and local de-population (Fairfield, 2015; 
Asche et al., 2016). These factors provide a strong AC related to the 
threat of relocation to other regions if the local framework conditions do 
not favour expansions (Fairfield, 2015). These factors taken together 
could imply that municipalities hosting strong aquaculture industry clusters 
(AC) are likely to be positive to expansion.
Indeed, the long scholarship on environmental politics suggests that 
economic interests often tend to trump environmental concerns (Jansen, 
1989; Underdal, 2000; Lafferty, 2012; Andresen et al., 2017). Studies of 
local and national attitudes to aquaculture also indicate that economic 
and employment factors tend to have stronger impacts than environ­
mental concerns at the local level (Hynes et al., 2018; Kaltenborn et al., 
2017; Krøvel et al., 2019). Our main proposition is therefore that:
P: municipalities hosting strong aquaculture industry clusters (ACs) are 
likely to be positive to expansion, regardless of the environmental problems 
related to aquaculture.
Through our case studies, we examine this proposition, eventual 
deviations from the proposition, and under what conditions such de­
viations may occur.
3. Methodology
3.1. Data collection
We employ a qualitative, case-study approach. The research draws 
upon a range of written sources, including peer-reviewed literature, 
white papers, policy, and legislative documents. We have also conducted 
semi-structured interviews within ten municipalities, with a total of 39 
municipal representatives (three to five within each municipality). The 
interviewees included mayors, municipal executives, and administrative 
representatives of municipalities.6 The in-depth interviews were con­
ducted on-line (teams-meeting), followed a common interview guide 
(Appendix 1) and lasted between 45 and 90 min. All interviews were 
transcribed and subjected to systematic analysis, where the content was 
organized according to key themes and coding words. This approach is 
according to a semi-structured model, with a prepared set of themes, 
issues and questions that are covered during the interview, while still 
leaving time to adjust and follow up questions (Kvale, 1994). As 
requested by our respondents, we have made the list of interviewees 
confidential. Our in-depth interviews are well suited for exposing and 
exploring nuances in positions on whether to increase expansion of 
aquaculture in the municipality, and for deriving explanations for the 
positions (Yin, 2003; King et al., 1994). We acknowledge that the 
interview-based, case-study method may be less suited for generaliza­
tion to other municipalities and countries than what might have been 
accomplished by a statistical survey. On the other hand, the key inter­
view approach allows us to uncover explanatory factors and mecha­
nisms that have wider relevance for understanding municipal positions 
on aquaculture expansion. Also, this method is added to by our selection 
of indicators (see below). These are datasets that have not been much 
used or tapped into for this kind of study before. While our assessment of 
these data sets may be open to discussion, we use methodological 
triangulation (here: a combination of qualitative interviews to access 
data and assessing quantitative data material) to discuss the strength 
and weaknesses of the trends indicated by our empirical material.
We selected ten municipalities from Norway’s five largest aquacul­
ture counties based on variation in positions on aquaculture expansion. 
To help identify a variation of suitable municipalities, the selection was 
carried out following a scoping review in these five counties. This means 
that we had indications at the outset which municipalities were positive, 
and which were negative to expansion, helping us achieve variation 
within and across counties. The variation in municipalities covers eight 
of the 13 production areas (PAs, see Map in Appendix 2 and Table 2). 
Further, our selection covers salmon farming PAs that have been given 
red, yellow, and green traffic lights7 (corresponding to high, moderate, 
and low salmon-louse infestation risks) under the TLS. Some munici­
palities are located on the outskirts of fjords, some in the innermost 
areas of fjords, and some are close to iconic populations of wild salmon. 
Finally, there is significant variation in terms of the size of the aqua­
culture activities between the coastal municipalities selected. (See 
Fig. 1.)
We seek to empirically establish knowledge about the extent of each 
municipality’s environmental problems through a combination of 
sources. The environmental problems are assessed based on five in­
dicators: the two first ones are related to the traffic light system i) 
whether the municipality is based in a red, yellow or green PAs in the 
traffic light system (TLS), ii) whether the municipality was attributed 
high, moderate or low risk of louse-induced wild salmon mortality by 
the TLS Expert Group. As the TLS evaluations encompass broad cate­
gorizations across extensive areas and multiple municipalities, which 
may face varying challenges with sea lice due to natural factors such as 
sea currents and salinity, our study aims to incorporate additional in­
dicators to provide a more detailed assessment of each municipality’s 
environmental status. Considering the availability of site-level data, we 
have selected two additional indicators related to sea lice, as sea lice 
serve as the most important indicator in the existing management 
framework. These two indicators include: i) the proportion of sites 
within each municipality exceeding the sea lice threshold mandated by 
regulations, and ii) the frequency of delousing actions undertaken 
within the municipality. These indicators aim to elucidate the severity of

Furthermore, we establish the strength of ACs from the three criteria 
set out above by way of scores on indicators: i) salmon farming com­

and employment, ii) presence of salmon farming-related supply-chain 
industries (ripple effects), iii) size of revenues and taxes distributed by 
the Aquaculture Fund, iv) the local ties of the aquaculture company 
(local headquarters). A fourth indicator (local ties of aquaculture com­
panies) is added to aid the examination of whether these ties might add 
to and impact the sense of local identity linked to salmon farming. 
Drawing in the opposite direction, however, would be the presence of 
other economic interests and concerns (fisheries, tourism, Sami in­
terests). We add this as a fifth indicator that could signify less de­
pendency on AC, as the municipality would have other sources of 
revenues and employment, and/or opposing interests. The scores on the 
Aquaculture Fund are drawn from documents from the Directorate of 
Fisheries9 and the remaining scores are based on information from the 
interviews (Appendix 1) and corroborated by official documents. This 
means that we apply quantitative measures, corroborated with docu­
mented data, in addition to the methodological triangulation achieved 
by interviewees, relating how ACs are perceived by the municipality. In 
line with our methodological approach (see next section), the total 
scores are aggregated to binary values with either high or low AC de­
pendency: half, or more than half positive scores on the five indicators 
will result in high dependency and vice versa.

presented in Tables 2 and 3 in section four as we summarize empirical

3.2. Methodological approach to analysis of data material
As a first methodological step, we draw inspiration from the quali­
tative comparative analysis for social-science case studies (Ragin, 2000). 
The qualitative comparative analysis method assigns scores to each 
observation on the presence or absence of certain conditions: Here, this 
means scores on the indicators for environmental problems and the in­
dicators for AC-dependency summed up and treated as binary variables.
This amounts to four possible types of combinations based on the 
combination of scores on the two independent variables (summed up in 
Table 1): 
• a high level of Environmental problems and a high level of De­
pendency on aquaculture industry clusters (ED);
• a high level of Environmental problems and a low level of de­
pendency on aquaculture industry clusters (Ed);
• a low level of environmental problems and a high level of De­
pendency on aquaculture industry clusters (eD);
• a low level of environmental problems and a low level of dependency 
on aquaculture industry clusters (ed).
Placing our ten cases in the resulting tables (Tables 1–4), we identify 
deviant cases and subject all to in-depth examination of municipality 
positions on aquaculture expansion: 
• According to our proposition we will find only cases of positive po­
sitions to expansion in boxes 1 (ED) and 3 (eD).
• In the first box (ED), we look for deviant cases and check whether 
high score on environmental problems may still constitute a condi­
tion for rejecting aquaculture expansion despite high dependency on 
AC.
• In the second box (Ed), a deviant case would be one with a positive 
position on expansion, directing us to seek for alternative factors to 
account for positive attitudes.
• Third (eD), in cases with low scores on environmental problems and 
high AC dependency, there might be fewer lessons to be drawn about 
the role of environmental issues, regardless of positions (accepting or 
rejecting expansion).
• Cases in the fourth box (ed) would likewise shed less light on the 
effect of environmental problems.
Accordingly, we begin our examination on municipal positions in 
cases belonging to box ED. We start out with an examination of ED 
municipalities with negative positions on aquaculture expansion, i.e. 
those that deviate from the expected pattern in our proposition. The 
remaining cases are all subject to in-depth investigation to check for 
additional insights about under what conditions cases may deviate from 
the predicted patterns.
4. Results: main tendencies in data material
Our ten cases display considerable variation – the majority of which 
are in line with our main proposition, but a few are not. Four munici­
palities receive high scores on both environmental problems and on AC 
dependency. Of these, three are positive to aquaculture expansion, in 
line with the proposition, while Osterøy alone is negative. The latter 
constitutes an interesting ‘deviant’ case by diverging from the expected 
pattern, based on our main proposition. In addition, Hareid was difficult 
to categorize in terms of AC dependency as the municipality has recently 
changed its position from being positive to being negative towards 
initiating aquaculture expansion. We hence include Hareid in the more 
detailed study based on the indicators for environmental problems and 
ACC dependency.
The three positive ED municipalities (Steigen, Solun, and Flatanger) 
will also receive attention. Here we examine whether other factors may 
nuance the matching patterns although being in line with our 
proposition.

problems, Hammerfest, Frøya and Smøla, also score high on AC de­
pendency (eD) and are positive to aquaculture expansion. As environ­
mental problems are low, these cases may not provide much insight 
regarding the impact of ensuing environmental concerns, but in-depth 
examination might nuance the picture. Two cases (Alta and Tromsø) 
score low on AC dependency, both are negative to expansion, and they

5. Discussion of empirical results
5.1. Deviant cases: highly AC-dependent municipalities that are negative 
to expansion
We found that Osterøy alone constitute a deviant case with high AC 
dependency and high environmental problems yet still being negative 
towards aquaculture expansion. Moreover, our interviews showed that 
Hareid had recently changed its position from positive to negative, 
which led us to include this municipality in the discussion of deviant 
cases. What may explain the divergences from our main proposition?
In the case of Hareid, the deviation seems strongly related to changes 
in their AC dependency. Hareid use to host several small-scale aqua­
culture companies. Until 2021, the municipality was positive towards 
starting aquaculture expansion and there were few conflicts of interest 
over coastal area-use. Hareid explained that they had had areas for 
aquaculture in the existing area plan, but that they then worked to 
prevent establishment of aquaculture in time for a new coastal zone plan 
to be in place. According to our interviewees, the municipal attitudes 
changed abruptly in 2021 with the entry of several large-scale aqua­
culture companies, which strongly advocated plans to establish major 
new aquaculture production facilities in popular coastal areas (Hareid, 
2021). Our interviewees explained how pressure from other types of 
industry actors and local people then led the municipality to decide to 
halt the initiation of aquaculture expansion in their area planning 
altogether. Yet, supply-chain aquaculture businesses still flourish in the 
municipality, including salmon slaughter and processing facilities, boat 
services (transport, sea lice cleaning and feed), which also cater to the 
shipping industry, and there are plans for farming of cleaner-fish (pro­
duced to eat salmon lice). This indicator could seem to point in the di­
rection of AC dependency, but as also stressed by our respondents, the 
local community receives no revenues from the Aquaculture Fund, nor 
any direct monetary benefits from the aquaculture industry as there are 
no salmon farming sites in operation in Hareid.
Environmental concerns seem to have climbed rapidly on municipal 
agendas in Hareid. Our interviewees point out that the private sector – 
including the tourism, fisheries, and shipyards industries – have recently 
come to realize that Hareid can flourish without direct ties to salmon 
farming. This has led the business groups to altogether stop pushing for 
any new aquaculture establishment in the municipality. Closeness to the 
city of Ålesund and the regional airport provide Hareid with a range of 
other industrial and employment opportunities that provide key sources 
of income. As regards environmental problems, the interviewees argued 
that Hareid have excellent natural conditions with favourable currents 
for aquaculture, despite the municipality’s location in a fjord situated in 
a yellow area of the TLS. The yellow TLS results add to environmental 
complaints by the tourist sector, which has frequently opposed any 
initiation of aquaculture expansions due to noise and other disturbances. 
Hareid doesn’t altogether fit the bill as a deviant case. It seems that it is 
the combination of relatively favourable natural conditions (albeit 
placed within a yellow PA area), the shift from local small-scale to large- 
scale external aquaculture companies, and a growing tourist sector that 
have instigated Hareid’s transition from positive to negative to intro­
duce aquaculture expansion in their area planning.
Osterøy resembles (the old) Hareid in several respects. It too is not 
solely dependent on the salmon-farming part of the AC, although 
aquaculture has played an important historical role in the local econ­
omy. Osterøy is situated in the innermost area of a fjord within a red 
production area of the TLS where there is high risk of salmon lice in­
festations, which contributes to give Osterøy a high score on environ­
mental 
problems.

has been consensus among key municipal actors that having large-scale 
farmed salmon is not compatible with Osterøy’s role as a possible host to 
this iconic wild salmon population. Instead, Osterøy municipality has 
decided to go for farmed trout. The shift from salmon to trout eliminates 
any genetic introgression problems for Vossalaks. However, farmed trout 
may also serve as hosts to sea lice, which threatens salmonids in general. 
As Osterøy is in favour of trout farming, its status as being negative to 
aquaculture expansion in general is challenged. We need a closer look.
Looking more carefully into the indicators for AC dependency, we 
note that Osterøy is highly dependent on the employment and on the 
revenues generated by AC (although they receive relatively low reve­
nues from the Aquaculture Fund), in this case stemming from trout 
farming, rather than farmed salmon, as this poses no risk of genetic 
intrusion. Nevertheless, and as actually pointed out by our interviewees 
in Alta municipality, Osterøy has become a role model and an inspiration 
to other municipalities due to its adamant stand against expanding 
salmon aquaculture and their demand that salmon farming must take 
place only in closed cages.10 Our data material indicates that this is due 
to the presence of strong and active environmental interests in this 
municipality. The employment and funds received from the aquaculture 
industry are still substantial and important to the local community, but 
trust in the salmon aquaculture industry appears to have diminished: 
Interviewees describe the local revenues received as small compared to 
the billions earned by the AC multinationals.
On closer scrutiny, Hareid and Osterøy appear to deviate less from 
our proposition than suggested by the results from the indicators. Hareid 
has over time become less dependent on the AC, while building revenues 
from other sectors, also through improved infrastructural ties to the city 
of Ålesund. Osterøy, on the other hand, has switched to another 
salmonid – trout farming, but still receives much needed revenues from 
AC investments and aquaculture ripple effects. In this case, it may be the 
presence of the iconic wild salmon Vossalaks and the related identity 
and strength of environmental groups advocating for only closed-caged 
salmon to protect wild species that explain the negative position of 
Osterøy municipality.

Solund, Flatanger and Steigen. These are all experiencing high scores on 
environmental problems, they are highly dependent on ACs – and they 
are still positive to aquaculture expansion.

environmental problems that might point against expanding aquacul­
ture production in their communities, but they are still adamant about 
allowing such expansion through their area planning. In the case of 
Solund, their positive stance on aquaculture expansion goes against the 
advice of the Country Governor, who wanted to stop further growth in

Flatanger, also with a small population of just above 1000, resembles 
Solund in having high AC dependency, and hosting both small local and 
large externally based companies. There are two large corporations, 
Salmar and Mowi, and one smaller, local company, Bjørøya AS. The 
municipality representatives argue that they must serve as advocates for 
the aquaculture industry, to counterbalance the advice from the County 
Governor to stop expanding areas for aquaculture. Serious problems 
with salmon lice as well as pancreas disease have recently caused 
reduced salmon production volumes in Flatanger. Nevertheless, like 
Solund, Flatanger municipality seeks to facilitate future expansion of 
salmon farming by establishing the more flexible “multi-use areas” in 
their area planning, which opens for future aquaculture expansion. 
Flatanger argues that the aquaculture industry is taking the salmon lice 
problem and other ecological challenges very seriously, trusting that the 
development of new technologies will soon provide solutions to the 
environmental problems.
While AC dependency seems to be central to the local identity in 
Flatanger, we find that it is primarily the small, local company Bjørøya 
that is involved in municipal matters. Bjørøya is providing the bulk of 
funding for local activities and infrastructure, such as schools and cul­
ture. According to our respondents, Bjørøya provides a greater share of 
benefits for residents compared to the much larger multinational cor­
porations, Mowi and Salmar. Our interviewees also point out that the AC 
has helped to bring in well-trained aquaculture personnel, thus building 
local knowledge in support of increased aquaculture growth.
Steigen, on their part, appears somewhat more concerned about the 
overall environmental impact of aquaculture expansion compared to 
Solund and Flatanger. Our interviewees were concerned that the largest 
salmon farming company headquartered in their community, Cermaq, 
had requested two separate, twin locations to rotate production, alleg­
edly to contribute to ecological sustainability. Currently, however, 
Cermaq is instead utilizing both areas to maximize production. Against 
this background, our Steigen interviewees worried that the central au­
thorities would ignore the negative environmental effects. This reso­
nates with the TLS Expert Group assessment (Table 2) that there is a high 
risk of salmon lice infestations in Steigen, even though the production 
area (PA9) received a green light by central authorities (the TLS does not 
regulate salmon lice at the level of sites). Pointing in a similar direction, 
Steigen received a high score on the percentage of sites exceeding sea 
lice limits. Interviewees argued that Steigen as a small municipality 
(population 2.657) lacks the necessary environmental competence to 
assess the cumulative environmental effects of aquaculture expansion. 
They also argued that a large-scale company such as Cermaq, albeit a 
locally based company in the municipality, is likely to be less attentive to 
local needs than would a smaller firm with local origins. Steigen was also 
negative to plan for “multi-use areas”, arguing that these were allowing 
aquaculture expansion in disguise. The positive position thus seems to 
be less adamant in the case of Steigen, possibly leading it towards future 
negative position on aquaculture expansion.
Overall, these cases illustrate the potential effects of change in the AC 
on future municipal positions on aquaculture expansions. As the AC 
changes its structure and size, or companies move their headquarters 
out, the dependency on or strength of ACs seems to shrink. Large 
multinational companies do not seem to bolster the local identity to the 
same extent as do smaller, locally owned and located companies: the 
result may be diminishing trust in that AC provides for the welfare of 
local communities, as implied in the cases of Steigen and Flatanger. 
Respondents in these municipalities argue that, with the small-scale, 
locally based companies being gradually replaced by large, multina­
tional corporations, residents have less trust in the industry’s willingness 
to provide benefits for the local community. This resonates well with the 
findings of Eriksen and Mikkelsen (2024) and similarly of Olsen et al. 
(2024) that social acceptance depends on environmental sustainability 
and economic fairness (distribution), as well as trust in industry and the 
governance system.
Our findings support our proposition that in most cases, environ­
mental problems do not gain precedence over economic growth—even 
when there is substantial knowledge of such problems calling for a stop 
in aquaculture area expansions. Environmental knowledge is also chal­
lenged by knowledge generated by the increasing professionalization 
within the aquaculture sector itself. As pointed to by our Flatanger in­
terviewees, university graduates with academic degrees in aquaculture- 
related disciplines are drawn to AC-dominated municipalities, which 
could strengthen arguments in favour of aquaculture expansion locally. 
Meanwhile, ecology and marine biology professionals are often absent 
from local-level administrations, especially in smaller communities 
(Lafferty, 2012), as also pointed out by our Flatanger interviewees.
5.3. Remaining cases: is it just the economy, then?
In this section we take a closer look at the remaining cases to check 
for nuances to the snap-shot picture shown in Table 4. Frøya, Ham­
merfest and Smøla all receive low scores on environmental problems and 
high scores on AC dependency – and are all in favour of expansion. Alta 
and Tromsø have low scores on both environmental problems and AC 
dependency, and they are both negative to expansion. The pattern 
conforms with our proposition, but we still subject these cases to ex­
amination to discuss the strength of the environment and AC de­
pendency factors.
Frøya scores low on environmental problems and high on AC de­
pendency. It thus seems like a good location for salmon farming and a 
close to ideal host municipality for salmon farming companies. The local 
industry delivers 20 % of the Norwegian salmon supply and represents 
40–50 % of the total value creation in Trøndelag County.11 Facing the 
open sea, salmon production in Frøya has advantageous ecological 
conditions, with strong, cleansing currents. Our respondents emphasise 
that they monitor the aquaculture industry’s environmental effects, and 
that the environmental assessment results are good. The locally based 
aquaculture company, Salmar, is headquartered on the island of Frøya, 
and the owner lives there. By contrast, in neighbouring, ‘twin’ island 
Hitra, most aquaculture revenues go to Bergen, a major city that is home 
to the large aquaculture corporations active on Hitra. The difference is 
about NOK 200 million in tax revenues, which helps to explain the 
positive attitudes towards aquaculture expansion in Frøya. This seem­
ingly points in the direction of the Misund et al. (2023) and Krali et al. 
(2022) findings that acceptance increases with proximity to aquaculture 
activities.
Smøla is also characterised by low levels of environmental problems 
and high dependency on the AC, but a closer analysis reveals nuances 
relevant to our main proposition. First, unlike in Solund and Steigen, 
there are no small-scale or local aquaculture companies in Smøla. The 
aquaculture industry consists predominantly of Salmar and Mowi, both

interests and lack of dependence on an AC. The fisheries sector has 
traditionally been strong and Tromsø has a thriving tourist sector, which

Tromsø is situated in a green PA, but the municipality is still concerned 
about other potential environmental problems, especially pollution from 
salmon farms, with a view to protecting the high levels of biodiversity in 
local Balsfjord.

significant challenges related to sea lice, which is contributing to their

the benefits are considerable – about 300 people are employed in 
aquaculture and various supply services. Why, then, did Alta resist

explanation appear to lie in Alta’s historical ties to wild salmon fishing 
in the iconic Alta River. The Alta River has also been the scene of one of 
the most legendary environmental conflicts in Norwegian history, as 
state hydropower development trumped environmental and indigenous 
people interests in the 1970s (Berg-Nordlie and Tvedt, 2024). The cul­
tural and economic value of the Alta River constitutes an important part 
of the municipality identity. Identity then may surely stem from many 
sources, not only an AC.
In addition, Alta is host to several aquaculture corporations, 
including Cermaq, Salmar and Grieg – all of them large-scale and 
externally based corporations. Respondents in Alta argue that trust in 
these companies dwindled when Cermaq relocated and established 
headquarter further south, and that the larger corporations generally 
foster less trust than would a locally based, smaller company. Interest­
ingly, in line with the TLS Expert Group advice and despite the green 
light from central authorities, Alta respondents add environmental 
problems to their main rationale for turning down the demand for 
expansion of aquaculture. They argue that the environmental assess­
ments cannot be trusted to cover all relevant aspects of the environ­
mental issues and problems involved, and the seabed assessments 
demonstrated negative conditions in a significant number of farming 
sites. Alta’s solid stand against expansion thus appears to be anchored in 
its strong identify and interest linked to wild salmon and presence of 
iconic salmon varieties (the Alta River salmon, resembling vossalaks in 
Osterøy)—and bolstered by environmental concerns. Moreover, Alta 
and Tromsø are large municipalities with a broad range of interests 
lobbying for area use beside the AC, thus reducing dependency. 
Conservationist groups and other economic interests are sufficiently 
strong to resist the demands from the aquaculture industry.
6. Concluding remarks
In this study we have examined the positions of Norwegian coastal 
municipalities towards expansion of salmon aquaculture, aiming to 
advance knowledge on when existing environmental problems may lead 
municipalities to refrain from authorizing new areas for salmon farming. 
We found that local dependency on strong industrial aquaculture clus­
ters (AC)—which bolsters the local identify while providing sizeable 
revenues, employment opportunities and funding of municipal activities 
and institutions—can explain the bulk of variation in municipal posi­
tions. Many coastal municipalities are highly dependent on ACs and 
highly supportive of salmon aquaculture growth and expanding area-use 
for farming, despite the existence of environmental problems. In line 
with our main expectation, many cases thus displayed a strong corre­
lation between AC dependency and positive municipal positions. In most 
of these cases, environmental problems did not sway municipal posi­
tions against aquaculture growth.
However, our findings indicate that changes in the AC structure, such 
as dismantling local ownership and the entry of external multinational 
corporations, can lead environmental concerns to gain increasing 
leverage on municipal positions. Municipalities with only one or a few 
locally based aquaculture companies are inclined to favour expansion, 
whereas municipalities in which the ACs consist of large-scale and 
externally based corporations (even if these contribute significantly to 
employment and production), are more likely to be against expansion. 
The local identity and trust in the AC thus seem to be eroded when the 
companies relinquish local ties, move their headquarters, or merge with 
larger, external corporations. A second facet is that in small, growth- 
oriented municipalities, the AC effect seems strong regardless of the 
nature of the AC. A third facet is that in larger municipalities, with low 
AC dependency, positions are mostly negative to aquaculture expansion 
even when environmental problems are low. These trends concerning 
the changing structure of ACs warrant closer empirical examination.
We also observe that rather than the aquaculture cluster, with ripple- 
effects and services catering to salmon farming, it is the local salmon 
farming company itself that represents the anchor of support for aqua­
culture industry within the municipality. In other words, the effect of a 
strong aquaculture industry cluster on positions thus seems to be medi­
tated by the type of salmon farming company. The implications could be 
significant for future developments: If the structural trends in aquacul­
ture persist, with consolidation and increasingly large and centralised 
corporations, coastal municipalities could become increasingly negative 
towards growth and expansion of coastal area-use for salmon farming. 
Municipal positions could still be more likely to hinge on AC dependency 
than on the actual state of the environment but dwindling local ties of 
AC could sway municipals towards declining aquaculture expansion. 
This finding leans in the same direction as those of Olsen et al. (2024)
and those of Eriksen and Mikkelsen (2024) that social acceptance hinges 
on trust and fairness, and that there is a growing reduced trust in 
aquaculture industry sustainability, equity and regulation.
Among the few negative municipalities, environmental problems can 
only partly explain their opposition to further aquaculture expansion. 
Interestingly, in these cases, the existence of environmental problems is 
still strategically drawn upon to legitimize opposition to aquaculture 
expansions that might negatively affect other interests, such as tourism 
or wild catch fisheries. This could indicate that environmental argu­
ments have gained strategic weight and, if our findings regarding the 
changing structure of AC dependency are bolstered, environmental ar­
guments might become more frequently used in the future. This would 
seem to contradict the comparative findings from Kraly et al. (2022) and 
Hynes et al. (2018) that proximity and being better informed about the 
sector tend to lead the Norwegian public towards more benign attitudes 
to aquaculture, compared to attitudes in Canada and in Ireland. The

catch fisheries, wild salmon, and environmental NGOs is warranted to 
examine how these may interact with the effects of environmental 
problems and AC dependency in the formation of municipal positions on 
aquaculture area expansion.
