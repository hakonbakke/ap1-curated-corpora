### Background
Rosendal and colleagues ask how environmental problems and local aquaculture industry clusters, separately and together, affect municipal positions on salmon-farming expansion. Municipalities designate or restrict coastal areas in the land-use part of the municipal master plan under the Planning and Building Act. The county coordinates localisation permits. National volume growth sits under the traffic-light system. The Aquaculture Registry is cited as showing 145 of 276 coastal municipalities hosting salmonid farming and related supply-chain activities.

The authors start from a long literature that economic interests often outweigh environmental concerns. Their proposition is that municipalities with strong aquaculture clusters will support expansion even when environmental problems are high.

### Methods
They select ten coastal municipalities from Norway's five largest aquaculture counties after a scoping review meant to secure variation in positions. The set covers eight of 13 production areas and red, yellow, and green traffic lights. The municipalities are Hammerfest, Alta, Tromsø, Steigen, Frøya, Flatanger, Smøla, Hareid, Solund, and Osterøy.

Positions are read from interviews with 39 municipal representatives (mayors, executives, and administrators, three to five per municipality, 45 to 90 minutes, online) and from land-use elements of master plans. Environmental problems are scored on five indicators: traffic-light colour, expert-group lice-mortality risk, share of sites over lice limits (2019-2022), delousing frequency (2019-2022), and B-assessment seabed results. Cluster dependency is scored on local investment and jobs, supply-chain ripple effects, Aquaculture Fund revenue per person (2019-2022 averages), presence of other strong economic interests, and local versus external headquarters. Scores are collapsed to high or low and placed in a four-cell qualitative comparative table. Interviewees are anonymous. Hareid lacks several lice and seabed scores.

### Main findings
Cluster dependency almost, but not fully, accounts for the positions. Environmental problems look less predictive. Four municipalities score high on both environment and cluster. Three of those (Steigen, Solund, Flatanger) are positive, in line with the proposition. Osterøy is negative on salmon expansion despite high cluster dependency and high environmental problems. It is in a red production area, close to Vossalaks, and has shifted toward farmed trout and closed-cage salmon. Interviewees there did not view environmental problems as serious, but used lice to justify no new salmon sites.

Solund and Flatanger stay positive despite County Governor advice to restrain growth. Both use multi-use areas to keep future farm space. Solund (population 742) is top of the set on Fund revenue per person. Flatanger interviewees say the local firm Bjørøya provides more local benefits than Mowi and Salmar. Steigen is positive but less adamant. Interviewees say Cermaq asked for twin sites to rotate, then used both to maximise production. Steigen was negative to multi-use areas, calling them expansion in disguise.

Hammerfest, Frøya, and Smøla combine low environmental scores, high cluster dependency, and a positive position. Frøya is said to deliver 20 percent of Norwegian salmon supply. A comparison with Hitra puts the local-headquarters tax difference at about NOK 200 million. Smøla and Hammerfest host large external firms and still plan for expansion. Both were criticised by County Governors for planning into protected areas.

Alta and Tromsø combine low cluster dependency, low environmental scores, and a negative position. The authors tie Alta to wild salmon identity (the Alta River) and a hydropower conflict history. Tromsø is a large municipality with fisheries, tourism and concern for Balsfjord biodiversity.

Hareid is scored high on environment and low on cluster. It was positive until 2021, then turned negative after large external companies sought major new facilities in popular coastal areas. It has no salmon farming sites in operation and received no Aquaculture Fund revenue.

The authors say the cluster effect is weaker where firms are large and externally based, and weaker in larger, more diverse municipalities. In small, growth-oriented municipalities the cluster effect looks strong even with external firms. They also say the local farming company, more than the wider cluster, anchors support. If consolidation continues, they expect more municipal resistance to area expansion. Environmental arguments are sometimes used to defend tourism, wild-catch fisheries, or other interests.

### Uncertainty and limitations
This is a ten-case qualitative comparison, not a statistical sample. Selection already used prior signals of positive and negative positions. The authors say the findings are tentative and should not be read as general causal statements. Environmental scores cover a limited set of problems and rely in part on reported lice counts. Hareid lacks several lice and seabed scores. Interview data cannot be shared.

### Relevance to this corpus
The paper is about municipal yes or no to new coastal area for salmon farming. That is the local land-use gate beside county locality clearance and national traffic-light growth.
