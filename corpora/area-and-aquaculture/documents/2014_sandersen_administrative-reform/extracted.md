This article discusses the impact of the Norwegian government’s administrative reform
on the management of the Norwegian aquaculture industry and coastal areas. The 2010
reform of government administration strengthened the County Councils’ role in issues
of aquaculture at the expense of the regional ofﬁces of the Directorate of Fisheries.
The aim of the reform was to increase self-governance through decentralization. How-
ever, international trends in coastal zone and marine resource management are moving
in the opposite direction, aiming at more integrated and ecosystem-based approaches
involving the management of larger, rather than smaller, geographic regions. This ar-
ticle examines the possible effects of this reform in light of the move from government
to governance, and in the context of a broad policy shift toward a more integrated,
ecosystem-based management (EBM) of the coastal zone. Based on insights from multi-
level and coastal zone governance debates, we argue that an unintended consequence
of the Norwegian administrative reform could be increased fragmentation of the aqua-
culture governance system, as well as a reduced capacity to implement EBM-related
measures. At the same time, the reform might improve coastal zone planning, although
a further step toward integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) would require a
greater delegation of authority to the County Councils.
Keywords EBM, ICZ, multilevel governance, Norwegian government administration
reform, sustainable aquaculture management
Introduction
The Norwegian marine aquaculture industry is the largest in the world, producing more
than 1 million tons of ﬁsh per year with an export value of 4 billion euros (Norwegian
Seafood Council 2013; Whitepaper 2005); and with an ambition to grow even larger. The
industry produces mainly salmon (salmo salar) and trout (salmo trutta) and has locations
all along the coast, with main areas of production extending from the west coast northwards.

The Norwegian regime governing the localization and management of aquaculture
is highly complex. Numerous national acts are relevant,1 implemented by different ad-
ministrative and political institutions with differing scopes of power and discretion over
decision-making concerning aquaculture (Directorate of Fisheries 2013). In addition to the
Ministry of Fisheries and Coastal Affairs,2 which allocates licenses and sets the overall
framework for the industry, the Directorate of Fisheries has played a central role. Other
state agencies relevant to the siting and management of aquaculture are the Norwegian
Food Safety Authority, the Norwegian Coastal Administration, and the County Governor.3
On January 1, 2010, a reform of government administration (“forvaltningsreformen”)
was adopted. The reform followed an international trend of delegation and regionaliza-
tion through which state-run tasks and activities were transferred to lower political levels
(Hooghe and Marks 2003; Peters and Pierre 2004). The general aim of the reform was to
strengthen the role, status, and autonomy of the County Councils by providing them with
more tasks and responsibilities (Whitepaper 2008). The reform affected aquaculture gover-
nance, since selected planning, coordinating, and decision-making tasks were transferred
from the regional ofﬁces of the Directorate of Fisheries to the regional County Councils
(Whitepaper 2008).
Aquaculture management now involves all three political levels; the state, counties and
municipalities. Besides the state level, there are 17 sea-bordering counties and 276 sea-
bordering municipalities, of which 14 and 160, respectively, have licenses for production
of salmon and trout (Ministry of Fisheries and Coastal Affairs 2013, ch. 4.1.3). Both
the regional and local levels have elected bodies (the County Councils and Municipality
Councils) to which have been delegated a number of issues to manage, as well as more
administrative tasks.
Norwegian municipalities have the last 15 years played an important role in holding
and allocating production localities for aquaculture. In Norway, coastal zone planning is
carried out primarily by municipal authorities, but their autonomy is often restricted by
sector authorities in areas such as the environment, ﬁsheries, navigation, veterinary affairs,
and other matters. Authorities in these sectors may raise objections to municipal coastal
zone plans if they conﬂict with the particular concerns/mandates of their sector (Jentoft
and Buanes 2005). The role of the counties in coastal zone planning has been to provide
an overall spatial plan to coordinate municipal plans, and to assist municipalities in their
planning and coordination efforts (Jentoft and Buanes 2005).
The transfer of tasks and responsibilities related to aquaculture governance from the
functional authority of the Directorate of Fisheries’ regional ofﬁces to the territorial au-
thority of the County Councils, as mandated by the reform, has affected the role of several
actors, transforming the established multi-level structure of aquaculture governance. This
transfer of authority has also contributed to a further increase in the complexity of the
aquaculture governance system through the introduction of an additional level of gover-
nance, as well as an increase in the number of decision-making units from the Directorate’s
seven regional ofﬁces to the fourteen coastal County Councils, thereby also narrowing the
geographic scope of each managing unit (see Figures 1 and 2).
In contrast to this Norwegian shift toward increased regional autonomy and self-
governance, there has been a concurrent international trend in marine and coastal man-
agement towards more integrated and ecosystem-based management approaches that ad-
vocate larger geographical scales, and focus on the challenges of dispersed knowledge and
management complexity (McLeod and Leslie 2009). The introduction of integrated coastal

The Reform and Aquaculture Governance
In Norway in the early 2000s, there was a political process to consider the reorganization
of the 19 counties into a few large super-regions (Whitepaper 2006a; Greenpaper 2000:22;
2003:19; 2004:19).5 However, such a regional reform was never adopted by the Parliament,
and instead there was a contrary move to strengthen the existing structure of the coun-
ties (Whitepaper 2008). As a result, several state-run tasks were transferred to the County
Councils, including new planning, coordinating, and decision-making tasks for aquaculture
that took effect on January 1, 2010. The aim of the reform appears to be driven neither by
a wish to improve the management of the aquaculture industry, nor by an inclination to
further integrate the management of the coastal areas, but rather by a desire to generally
strengthen the role of the County Councils by providing them with more tasks and respon-
sibilities (Whitepaper 2006a, ch. 4.1). The goals of the reform were to strengthen local
and regional democracy, promote efﬁcient governance, and facilitate strong municipalities
and robust regions able to exploit local and regional advantages (Whitepaper 2006a, ch.
4.1).
The County Councils had already played a role in aquaculture governance as the
result of a new management setup occasioned by the 1985 Planning and Building Act that
intended to allow for the more coordinated management of the coastal area. This coastal
zone management system is based on physical planning that extends one nautical mile from
the baseline into the sea. The 276 coastal municipalities designate the different zones of
their waters for certain types of use, either exclusively or in combination. Among them are
zones designated for aquaculture, making the municipality the main holder and manager of
the production site or locality—the limited core natural resource in aquaculture (Sandersen
and Nicolaisen 2007; Stokke, Hanssen, and Hovik 2006). As regional planning authority,
the County Councils were given the task to coordinate the municipal coastal zone plans
and provide regional master plans. After the revision of the Planning and Building Act in
July 2009, this role was further strengthened.
In terms of aquaculture governance, the County Councils have provided the ﬁsheries
authorities with recommendations on the allocation of aquaculture licenses since 2002.6
With the enactment of the government administration reform in January 2010, the County
Councils were granted the formal mandate to allocate licenses, coordinate the application
processes, and maintain the aquaculture registry (Whitepaper 2008). However, the Ministry
of Fisheries and Coastal Affairs still holds the mandate to decide the numbers of licenses
available for distribution in the counties. The County Councils also became responsible for
procedures related to the adjustment of licenses and the relocation of ﬁsh farms. Moreover,
the reform made County Councils responsible for aquaculture impact assessment proce-
dures and entitled them to raise objections to the municipalities’ coastal zone plans on

Multi-Level Governance and Norwegian Aquaculture
The multi-level governance approach was developed through studies of European integra-
tion, and focuses on the challenges created by the dispersion of public policy decision-
making and the relations between domestic state agencies, and regional and local govern-
ments (Hooghe and Marks 2003; Peters and Pierre 2004). This approach is also applied in
the analysis of challenges related to national coordination (Christensen and Lægreid 2008;
Lægreid and Serigstad 2006). According to the principles of multi-level governance, two
lines of policy coordination are relevant—the vertical interplay between local, regional, and
national management levels, and the horizontal interplay between different policy ﬁelds
and functional sector agencies (Bache and Flinders 2004; Friedmann and Weaver 1979;
Peters and Pierre 2004). In addition, there is a need for territorial (horizontal) coordination
at the regional and local levels if there is a mismatch between the scopes of the ecological
and administrative/political systems.
Vertical policy coordination is important to ensure that broader national interests are
considered and implemented at lower management levels. In addition, it is necessary
to coordinate between the different specialized sector agencies. In the Norwegian case,
the County Council therefore has to balance the (potentially conﬂicting) national aim of
industrial development and the obligation to protect the environment, at the same time
ensuring that the competence of the sector agencies is acknowledged.
Through the coordination of municipal coastal zone plans and master plans deﬁning
the areas for different activities, County Councils are furthermore faced with challenges
related to conﬂicting interests. A critical point raised in the literature on multi-level gover-
nance is the tension between the need for standardization and specialization among sector
agencies, in order to secure equal treatment of actors independent of their geographic lo-
cation, and the wish for a high degree of local and regional autonomy (see for instance
Underdal 1980; Pierre and Stoker 2000; Lafferty and Hovden 2003; Lægreid and Serigstad
2006; Christensen and Lægreid 2007; 2008; M¨antysalo, Saglie, and Cars 2011). Thus, the
further integration of sectors or interests might be difﬁcult, as the guiding principles, or
logics, are partly in conﬂict (Lægreid and Serigstad 2006; Christensen and Lægreid 2008).
In practical politics, standardization/specialization and political autonomy might lead in
opposite directions, making integration difﬁcult.
This dilemma is clearly illustrated by the setup of governance in the Norwegian aqua-
culture industry. The coordination of the different actors are inﬂuenced by the new formal
structures, but also by how those actors manage to frame the process through negotiation and
learning (Christensen et al. 2007). The extent to which a given case is characterized by an
authoritative and formal structure, or by a mutual learning process, might vary (Christensen
and Lægreid 2008), inﬂuencing the degree of success in coordinating the various actors and
adopting sustainable policies, while balancing the interests of environmental protection and
the different users of the coastal zone (Hovik and Stokke 2007). The complexity, related to
sectoral and territorial or vertical and horizontal coordination, increases further when we
also include the issues of integrated and ecosystem-based coastal zone management.
ICZM and EBM in Norway
The dominant frameworks of governance in coastal zone management are ICZM and,
more recently, EBM approaches (Aswani et al. 2011). Both concepts provide integrative

frameworks that aim to overcome different kinds of fragmentation, and in many ways
overlap. The two frameworks, however, originate from different scholarly orientations. The
seminal works in ICZM were written mainly by representatives of applied social science and
planners, and supported by natural scientists experienced in coastal management, whereas
EBM is more closely associated with applied ecology, and supported by economics and
other social sciences (Christie 2011; Cicin-Sain and Knecht 1998; Degnbol et al. 2006;
Kay and Alder 2005). Whether EBM should be seen as an expansion and reﬁnement of
ICZM or as a paradigm shift is debated (Aswani et al. 2011). Forst (2009, 302) claims that
the two approaches are increasingly converging. The difference in main focus, however,
implies that the conditions for ICZM and EBM will be differently affected by the reform.
ICZM is a “process designed to overcome the fragmentation inherent in single-sector
management approaches . . . in the splits in jurisdiction among different levels of govern-
ment, and in the land-water interface” (Cicin-Sain and Knecht 1998, 1). This approach had
its beginnings in the practice of conﬂict resolution and dispute mediation (Forst 2009), and
its focus is usually on cross-institutional collaboration, and formal and informal governance
processes (Christie 2011). Most deﬁnitions of ICZM emphasize sustainable use and the
bringing together of human (social, economic, and political) and ecological dimensions.
EBM, on the other hand, has its background in the observable and widespread decline
in ecosystem functions and services, and calls for an integrated approach that considers
the entire ecosystem, including humans. The goal of ecosystem-based management is to
maintain an ecosystem in a healthy, productive and resilient condition so that it can provide
the services humans want and need (McLeod and Leslie 2009). Accordingly, the EBM
approach has an emphasis on long-term considerations, and a more explicit commitment
to reversing these negative tendencies than ICZM, with its more short term pragmatic
approach aimed at arbitrating more tangible and manifest conﬂicts. ICZM embraces the
institutional and stakeholders complexity of the coastal zone, to which EBM also more
explicitly adds and addresses ecological complexity (Tallis et al. 2010; Espinoza-Romero
et al. 2011; Crowder and Norse 2008).
EBM is relatively cumbersome, knowledge-, data-, and time-consuming, and risks
causing delay in addressing the problems at hand (Tallis et al. 2010). Hence, when dealing
with an issue that is urgent, ICZM-dominated management practices may be preferable
(Sandersen et al. 2013). Typically, an ICZM approach is more appropriate than an EBM
approach if the scale of the management is smaller, problem resolution is more urgent, and
relevant codiﬁed knowledge is less available.
In aquaculture, the dispersion of nutrients and diseases follows dominant currents and
tidal movements, and can affect several local and regional ecosystems. Thus, it is extremely
difﬁcult to ﬁnd a relevant management scope for the various ecosystems. Olsen et al.
(2011, 257) contend that “ecological, governance and management complexity increases
with increasing geographic scale, with associated increases in uncertainty.” Sandersen et al.
(2013), on the other hand, argue that the complexity of local issues is vastly underestimated.
There is, in addition, no reason why complexity and scale should co-vary in the same way
for ecology, governance, institutions, user-group structure, and society.
EBM is most frequently adopted for geographically large, ecologically deﬁned areas,
while ICZM is, as the name suggests, applied in coastal zones, thereby usually covering
smaller, but heavily used and highly institutionalized contexts. In Norway, EBM has been
attempted only for its three large ocean ecosystems: Barents Sea, the Norwegian Sea, and the
North Sea (Whitepaper 2013; 2009; 2006b). Even though comprehensive ocean mapping
projects are implemented (MAREANO), it seems unlikely that a similar level of scientiﬁc
effort will be made to obtain ecological monitoring data for the entire local scale coastal

national coastal current database, in order to provide knowledge about the patterns and

ecological factors.
The current coastal zone management scheme in Norway can be described as a step
toward an ICZM system, where spatial planning, coordinating, and practical problem-
solving are carried out by the municipalities within the framework of the spatial master
plan made by the County Councils and the sector agencies with specialized competence
and standardized decision-making procedures. To fully be regarded as bona ﬁde ICZM
there would need to be a higher level of coordination or a reorganization of management
into a more hierarchical structure, integrating the different sector agencies. The question
then is whether the reform of the government administration will increase the complexity
of aquaculture and coastal zone management rather than reducing it, and how it will affect
the aim of further developing ICZM and EMB in the Norwegian coastal area.
Norwegian Aquaculture Governance, ICZM, and EBM
The reform of the government administration was aimed at strengthening the role of the
County Councils and was mainly an institutional response to public policy reform in
Norway. The reform was not ﬁrst and foremost established to improve the governance of
the aquaculture industry, or to further integrate the governance of the coastal areas. The
question addressed here is what have been the implications, or side effects, of the reform,
in terms of sustainable governance of aquaculture and the general policy goal of a more
integrated and ecosystem-based governance of the coastal zone? In the following sections,
we will discuss in more detail how the delegation of tasks from a national sector agency
to a regional political unit can foster or impede coordination, prioritization, and holistic
management of the coastal area.
From Administrative to Political Coordination
The reform transferred decision-making tasks from the hierarchically structured Directorate
to political institutions with a high degree of autonomy and political leeway. To some extent
this change can be described as a change from one management approach to another; that
is, from one inﬂuenced by functional responsibilities to one with territorial responsibilities
(Friedmann and Weaver 1979). However, representatives of the counties claim that the
reform was more a transfer of administrative tasks than it was a genuine devolution of
power to independent policy- and decision-making (Frisvoll and Rønningen 2012). The
content of the Aquaculture Act has not been changed by the transfer, the sector agencies
still have a form of veto, and the number of licenses for acquisition is still set by the Ministry
of Fisheries (beyond the control of the County Councils). Therefore, the delegation of new
tasks has not seemed to alter the standardized management setup and the control of the
sector agencies; in fact, there is clearly less room for political maneuvering following the
reform than the County Councils had initially hoped. An empirical example of the limited

standardization and regional political autonomy, therefore, seems unchanged, as the reform
only allows for a limited degree of regional autonomy. The government’s announcement
in 2013 of 45 so-called “green” salmon licenses with newly speciﬁed and stricter criteria
related to sea lice and escapes also illustrates the fact that the increased role of the counties
in aquaculture management was not deeply anchored in the government, but rather a side
effect of a broader reform with other aims, as in this case the assessment and allocation of
applications for licenses are to be carried out by a centrally appointed expert group, and
not by the counties.8
As to the new coordinating role played by the County Councils, the reform does not
seem to reduce the need for vertical coordination and integration, but might provide a better
distribution of tasks. The fact that the regional ofﬁces of the Directorate of Fisheries can
now concentrate on control tasks may make vertical integration and coordination better,
since they no longer need to handle industrial development issues, license allocations, and
control tasks as in the previous management setup. In sum, the new division of labor might,
therefore, ease the vertical coordination.
One can expect that the reform is likely to make horizontal cross-border coordination
between counties less efﬁcient, because there is less formal coordination between the
County Councils than between the regional ofﬁces of the Directorate of Fisheries. Already,
concern has been raised by industry actors about differing practices between counties,
calling for greater coordination and uniform practices between counties.9
The reform also inﬂuences vertical coordination between the counties and the munic-
ipalities, and horizontal coordination between municipalities. As to the counties’ relations
with the municipalities, narrowing the geographical scope of regional decision-making units
can be expected to facilitate improved coordination and integration within the counties. The
traditionally good, tight relationships between municipalities and counties make it likely
that the reform can facilitate greater regional coordination and improved efﬁciency in the
exploitation of the limited coastal spaces suitable for modern aquaculture. For the counties,
the incentives have now been strengthened for viewing the sea space within their borders
in a more holistic manner, and for improving the link between coastal zone planning and
aquaculture development. At the same time, the increased role and mandate of the County
Council to promote aquaculture development and to inﬂuence the policies of the municipal-
ities may negatively impact the collaborative relationships between the municipalities and
the counties. Rather than being the municipalities’ “best friends,” the counties may now
increasingly run into conﬂicts of interests with the municipalities with regard to coastal
planning and aquaculture governance. The municipalities have had many discussions and
conﬂicts with the Directorate of Fisheries and the other sector agencies over the allocation
of licenses, the availability of localities for aquaculture, and the localization of ﬁsh farms;
they will now have similar discussions and conﬂicts with the counties. One example that
illustrates this new relationship is the Nordland County Council’s objections to the Narvik
municipality’s decision to remove the (few) existing aquaculture localities from their coastal
zone.10 Another example of this conﬂict is when in November 2013, referring to their new
larger role in the aquaculture management system, the three northernmost counties in Nor-
way ﬁled a protest against the state’s suggestion to increase the municipalities’ portion of
economic compensation from aquaculture licenses at the expense of the counties (County
Councils 2013). These examples indicate a kind of “turf war” between the municipalities

consequences for the counties’ ability to coordinate with the municipal level remain unclear
and should be investigated further. Here, the nature of interaction is expected to be crucial
to success; a formal, authoritative, and standardized coordination process seems less useful
here than a more deliberative process of negotiation and mutual learning. Presumably, case
studies will reveal a high degree of variation.
Specialized Sector Logic, Regional Development Logic, and ICZM
As outlined above the ICZM approach is aimed at the coordination of all the different poli-
cies related to the various activities impacting the coastal zone, including environmental
protection, aquaculture, ﬁsheries, tourism, and other industries. In the Norwegian manage-
ment of the aquaculture industry and coastal areas, there are tensions over how different
interests should be dealt with and accommodated. Pressure on coastal areas increases con-
ﬂict and necessitates not only a higher degree of coordination and integration, but also
priorities with regard to area use. Management procedures, as prescribed by the ICZM ap-
proach, do not solve the distributive aspects of coastal zone management, as user conﬂicts
cannot always be resolved through coordination. Such conﬂicts are complex and include
social, economic, and ecologic aspects that need to be taken into account and weighed
against another; it requires actors to take into consideration different activities like ﬁshing,
aquaculture, tourism, and recreation, as well as conservation issues, involving not only
different interests, but also different knowledge systems (Jentoft and Buanes 2005; Hovik
and Stokke 2007; Tiller, Brekken, and Bailey 2012; Bremer and Glavovic 2013).
Prior to the government’s administrative reform, the County Councils already had the
task of coordinating the municipalities’ coastal zone plans and providing master plans.
Adding the task of coordinating aquaculture governance might therefore improve and
strengthen the integrative power of coastal zone management. The County Councils, as
broad, democratic institutions rather than specialized, state sector agencies, may also be
more inclined toward cross-sector information sharing, negotiation, and mutual learning.
The County Councils could therefore enhance the integrated aspects of coastal zone man-
agement, both through formal structures and informal learning processes, depending on
the chosen planning strategy (Hovik and Stokke 2007). Aquaculture executive ofﬁcers who
moved from the Directorate of Fisheries’ regional ofﬁces to the County Councils under
the reform claim that the presence and availability of information is much better at the
county level, and the conditions for more holistic aquaculture management have been im-
proved (personal communication September 2013). Still, the promotion of both industrial
development and environmental protection needs to be balanced and prioritized; this can,
as previously mentioned, transform the relationship between the County Councils and the
municipalities.
As for industrial actors, they are faced with increasing competition, having to compete
with other user groups in formal and informal processes, in order to gain access to suitable

localities for their farms. They also have to accept that interests other than their own
are being prioritized and that the management of the industry may increasingly follow the
logic and preferences of regional politics, rather than national or industrial interests (Stokke
et al. 2009; Hersoug and Johnsen 2012). One of the county’s tasks is to promote regional
economic growth and job creation, but many municipalities have become reluctant or
negative about increased production and aquaculture activity in their waters. This reluctance
is among others due to the limited trickle down effect on the local economy and job
creation, thereby giving little leeway to the County Councils (Sandersen and Nicolaisen
2007; Isaksen, Andreassen, and Robertsen 2012). Furthermore, the authority of specialized
sector agencies encroaches on the discretion of the County Councils. In general, however,
it seems like the role of the County Council as mediator and integrator of municipal and
sector interests can improve the ICZM qualities of the system, even if there is no genuine
devolution of power for independent policy- and decision-making.
The Reform and the Conditions for EBM
The EBM approach, as described, promotes the need to consider the entire ecosystem,
including humans, in order to maintain the ecosystem in a healthy condition. Generally,
the prospect for developing an integrated EBM within ecologically rather than politically
deﬁned boundaries has been limited by the reform, as it has reduced the geographical
scope of regional decision-making units from the 7 ﬁsheries management regions to the
17 sea-bordering counties, of which 14 have licenses for salmon and trout farming. It
is therefore unlikely that the reform will facilitate the necessary broad restructuring of
the spatial layout of aquaculture farms and the management regime, as suggested by the
national expert committee on effective and sustainable area use in the aquaculture industry
(Expert Committee 2011).11 The expert committee recommended the establishment of
larger production zones (each including four stocking/fallowing zones where all aquaculture
activities should be coordinated) with buffer zones between them. This restructuring would
reduce the risk of transfer of pathogens between farms as compared to the current situation.
This suggestion to establish ecologically based production zones,12 however, is certainly in
conﬂict with the government’s administrative reform that reduces the geographical scope
of regional decision-making units. Such a system is now even less likely to correspond
geographically with any of the existing territorial political boundaries. It would require
increased coordination at both the local and the regional level to look more holistically
at the entire ecosystem and the various human and non-human impacts—making it even
more difﬁcult to achieve the ambitions of the EBM approach. It is also more likely that the
counties now will go against such a new system, as it would reduce their newly acquired
autonomy both in coastal zone planning and license allocation.
Both the government and other stakeholders emphasize the importance of having
an aquaculture industry that has a site structure and usage of sea areas that reduce the
environmental impact and risk of disease. Certainly, the municipalities are concerned with
their local environment, but environmental knowledge based on current research is not
central to their regular coastal zone planning and decision-making processes (Hersoug
2013). In the case of aquaculture, it is frequently the ﬁsh farming companies that provide
the required background knowledge, especially when impact assessments are required
(Hersoug et al. 2012). A national coastal “current catalog” is under development and
will certainly be beneﬁcial in the search for smarter localizations of ﬁsh farms. But it is
questionable whether the 276 coastal municipalities and 14 counties with salmon farming
licenses will take advantage of this knowledge in their planning, as they lack the relevant

decision-making powers would have to be transferred to the counties, but preferably in
terms of management units that correspond to the geographical scope of ecologically based
production zones.
Conclusion
In this initial study we have assessed some of the possible consequences of the administrative
reform for aquaculture and coastal zone management based on three theoretical bodies of
literature: Multi-Level Governance, ICZM, and EBM. As previously mentioned, the reform
of the government administration, which aimed at strengthening the role of the County
Councils, was an institutional response to a public policy reform process in Norway and was
not ﬁrst and foremost motivated by a desire to improve the management of the aquaculture
industry or the coastal zone management system.
The focus of this article has been on the implications, or unintended side effects, of the
reform in light of the ambition for more integrated and ecosystem-based management of
the coastal zone. The question initially raised was whether the reform’s aim of increasing
the efﬁciency and autonomy of local and regional government supports the principles of
integrated coastal zone and ecosystem-based management approaches. The questions are
based on the assumption that transferring authority from a national sector agency to a
regional political unit affect the multi-level governance of the aquaculture industry and
the coastal zone by inﬂuencing the coordination, prioritization, and prospects for a more
holistic management of the coastal area.
We ﬁnd that the Norwegian administrative reform’s possible effects on aquaculture
matters are mixed. The reform implies a very limited de facto transfer of decision-making
authority that can be described as the mere delegation of administrative tasks. We therefore
argue that, because the role of the specialized sectors remains largely unchanged and there
is only a very limited degree of regional autonomy, the reform does not appease the tension
between national standardization and regional political autonomy. As to efﬁciency, the
degree of coordination between sector agencies remains unchanged, whereas the horizontal
coordination at the regional level between the counties is more likely to be reduced. Because
the reform makes territorial units smaller and more numerous, it requires more coordination
between regional levels, producing the danger of a less integrated and more fragmented
management of the aquaculture industry. In terms of the goals of EBM, the reform may
hamper the prospects of achieving a sustainable aquaculture industry, as it contravenes
the need for larger, connected, ecologically sound areas and the sufﬁcient integration of
relevant agencies. Considering the reform in terms of local or regional ICZM, the small,
but possibly signiﬁcant, increase in horizontal coordination between the municipalities is
a step forward, even though several elements in the planning system reduce the political
autonomy of the counties. The degree of success will be affected by the nature of the
interaction with the municipalities.

So, even though the reform has slightly altered the established multi-level governance
setup for aquaculture from a functional or sectoral one towards one governed more by terri-
torial or political logic, it has not involved any real devolution of authority to the counties.
However, the County Councils, as broad, democratic institutions rather than specialized,
state sector agencies, may improve the conditions for a more holistic and integrated man-
agement approach, especially considering the County Councils task of coordinating the
municipalities’ coastal zone plans. Still, the increase in the number of decision-making
units seems to hamper, rather than foster, the more holistic governance of the coastal zone,
even though there might be certain advantages within the counties.
The two basic principles for coordination—on the one hand, territorial, or political
coordination and, on the other hand, functional, sectoral, or hierarchial—have both pros
and cons. In this article, we argue that, in the Norwegian context, the ﬁrst principle seems to
facilitate local ICZM efforts, whereas the other is more inclined towards the development
of more comprehensive EBM projects. And with a reform that beneﬁts the ﬁrst type of
coordination at the expense of the latter, we conclude that the Norwegian government’s
administrative reform is more likely to improve coordination and ICZM at the municipal
level, but less likely to facilitate larger, cross-county comprehensive EBM efforts.
