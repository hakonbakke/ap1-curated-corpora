ABSTRACT
While the rapid growth in global aquaculture production has made the industry a success story in terms of providing nutritious 
food, it is often criticized for its limited socioeconomic effects. While there is a burgeoning literature investigating socioeco-
nomic impacts, this is a difficult topic due to limited data availability. In this paper, we will review the socioeconomic impacts 
of the Norwegian salmon aquaculture industry using economic impact analysis, focusing on two impact measures: employment 
and value added. In contrast to earlier studies, we will cover the whole supply chain from input suppliers to sales rather than 
focusing on one part of the chain and accounting for ripple effects in several rounds. This is highly important as there, in 2022, 
were 11,175 direct jobs in production, 8500 jobs in additional core activities related to processing and sales, and 38,000 jobs with 
different suppliers and service providers, for a total of 57,800 jobs in the industry. The production activities are highly dispersed 
geographically and mostly conducted in coastal communities. While this is also true for some types of services, the employment 
in the industry becomes more concentrated and moves toward larger communities and cities as one moves downstream or away 
from the core activities.
1   |   Introduction
Globally, aquaculture production is rapidly growing, with pro-
duction spreading to new species and countries [1–3]. One would 
expect that there are strong positive economic and social im-
pacts associated with this growing industry, as profitable com-
panies are necessary for increased production, and it is difficult 
to increase production without using more inputs, including em-
ployees [4, 5]. However, the socioeconomic impact of aquacul-
ture has been strongly questioned and criticized. Aquaculture 
is, in some narratives, portrayed as an export-­oriented industry 
providing products for wealthy and often foreign consumers, 
while at the same time marginalizing rural farm workers and 
reducing rural access to nutrient-­dense seafood [6]. It has also 
been shown that some business models worsen social equity 
and can create exploitative relationships in the aquaculture pro-
duction systems [7, 8]. In extreme cases, it has been argued that 
aquaculture can even undermine local community development 
at the expense of its resources [9–11]. The socioeconomic impact 
varies with production system, and specific production systems 
like marine aquaculture that require large-­scale enterprises 
with highly automated systems are perceived to contribute little 
to employment and food security among the poor [12]. The ques-
tionable socioeconomic impacts together with the environmen-
tal externalities have led several authors to explore the existence 
of a social license to operate for the industry and what factors 
shape this [13–17].

ture has many similarities to the literature highlighting the

aquaculture production. Given that the global aquaculture 
industry is highly heterogeneous with a number of different 
production practices and governance systems, it is not surpris-
ing that more recent research shows that the environmental 
impacts and the degree of environmental sustainability also 
vary [18, 19]. There is also a burgeoning literature providing 
a more balanced perspective on the socioeconomic impact 
of aquaculture, showing that there are significant benefits 
associated with the industry that may balance the potential 
negative impacts and even make the industry an asset as an 
engine of economic growth and social sustainability [20–26]. 
Aquaculture can contribute to local economic development 
and poverty alleviation by increasing employment opportuni-
ties and reducing income inequality through providing local 
ripple effects. Increased aquaculture production can also in-
crease the accessibility and availability of nutritious foods 
[1, 27], and the industry is in general less export-­oriented than 
wild fisheries [28, 29]. Garlock et al. [5] use a global dataset to 
show that the three pillars of sustainability are positively cor-
related for aquaculture, and as such, that there are few trade-­
offs between different types of sustainability.
Still, our knowledge with respect to socioeconomic impacts of 
the aquaculture industry is limited beyond the fact that the in-
dustry is profitable for a large enough share of the companies 
operating in it and therefore continues to be rapidly growing 
[1, 4], and that it has a huge potential to create jobs and eco-
nomic activity [30]. This is a challenge as limited knowledge 
makes it difficult to assess the trade-­offs associated with a 
growing industry and may accordingly provide impediments 
to continued growth. In this paper, we will provide a review 
of the socioeconomic impacts of the Norwegian aquaculture 
industry, focusing primarily on one social indicator, employ-
ment, and one economic indicator, value added.1 By reviewing 
the impact of the whole industry in a single country through 
the whole supply chain, a comprehensive perspective can be 
provided. Our approach allows the industry's ripple effects on 
the local, regional, and national levels to be assessed, cover-
ing the combined economic effects on the industry's suppli-
ers, their suppliers, and so on. However, the analysis stops at 
the national border, meaning that international ripple effects 
from imported input factors and services, as well as services 
and processing after the fish has left Norway, are not con-
sidered. These are potentially nontrivial as imports provide 
92% of feed ingredients and downstream processing abroad is 
highly significant [33, 34].
The Norwegian salmon industry is of general interest as it is 
one of the clearest examples of a capital-­intensive, industrial-
ized aquaculture industry that is technology and knowledge 
leading in several dimensions [35–39]. This is an organiza-
tional form with a large number of specialized suppliers of 
both physical input factors and services [4, 40–42], and as 
such, a setting that most clearly will show the importance of 
accounting for ripple effects. While a growing industry with 
large production, the salmon farms are spread across nearly 
1000 sites, covering most of the Norwegian coastline, making 
this industry highly important for many coastal societies.2 It is 
accordingly an industry with small locally owned companies 
as well as large multinational companies with headquarters in 
the larger cities [43].3
2   |   Background
Global salmon aquaculture production has, since its modest start 
about 50 years ago, grown to a production of around 3 million 
tonnes in 2022 and is currently the second largest aquaculture 
species by value after shrimp [1, 45]. Norway is the largest pro-
ducer of farmed salmon, with a share of around 55% [46]. With 
a production of about 1.6 million tonnes of Atlantic salmon and 
salmon trout in 2022, the Norwegian industry has experienced 
high and quite steady growth since its inception in the 1970s 
(the production in 1980 was a mere 8000 tonnes). Currently, 
about 95% of the Norwegian salmonid production is Atlantic 
salmon, while the remainder is large rainbow trout, also known 
as salmon trout. The share of trout has been declining slowly 
over time [47]. As trout is regulated as salmon and requires the 
same license, we will, in the remainder of this paper, imply both 
Atlantic salmon and trout when we say salmon. There is also 
a tiny production of a few other species, like cod, halibut, and 
turbot, that will be disregarded here, so that when we say the 
Norwegian aquaculture industry, we refer to the salmon aqua-
culture industry. Due to Norway's limited population, the do-
mestic market is limited, and more than 95% of production is 
exported [48].
Salmon production takes place along the entire Norwegian 
coast, mostly in remote coastal areas. Hence, the industry 
can be very important for sustaining the population, and 
even causing growth, in communities otherwise often char-
acterized by depopulation [46]. The strong increase in salmon 
production has also resulted in a growing importance of the 
industry for Norway's economy. The aquaculture industry has 
since 2012 grown from around 0.5% of Norwegian (mainland4) 
Gross Domestic Product (GDP) to around 1.5%, as value cre-
ation increased from 8 to 47 billion Norwegian kroner (NOK) 
(Figure 1).5
The success of salmon farming can be attributed to an increas-
ing degree of control over the production processes facilitated 
by innovations in biology, technology, organization, products, 
and marketing [4, 39, 49]. With systematic breeding, salmon 
grow faster [50, 51]; with effective vaccines, challenging disease 
problems have been managed [52], and better feed has resulted 
in faster growth and more efficient feed utilization [53], while 
improved logistics and marketing increase demand as more 
potential consumers can be reached [54–57]. In salmon sales, 
one can observe both vertical integration for some of the larger 
companies, but also independent sales companies of different 
sizes [58].
The Norwegian salmon aquaculture industry accordingly relies 
on a range of input factors, with feed as the single largest item, 
accounting for around half the production cost [46]. Moreover, 
as aquaculture production has increased and farms have grown 
larger, the sector relies on an increasing range of ever more special-
ized goods and services. With increasing scale, many of the tasks 
originally performed by the salmon farmers are now performed

3   |   Methods and Data
The main tool for our review of the impacts of the Norwegian 
salmon industry is the economic impact or input/output (I–O) 
analysis. An economic impact analysis is a commonly used 
tool to investigate the socioeconomic impacts of an industry 
and calculate how an industry contributes to the national and 
regional economy. An I–O analysis is an economic modeling 
technique that examines the interconnectedness of differ-
ent industries within an economy. It helps understand how 
changes in one sector can impact others by tracking the flow 
of goods and services between industries. I–O analysis is, for 
instance, used in economic planning, policy formulation, and 
decision-­making. I–O matrices represent the flow of inputs 
(for instance, raw materials, goods, and services) required 
to produce outputs (goods and services). I–O analysis may 
thus be used to assess the direct and indirect consequences of 
changes in one sector on the entire economy. The procedures 
used for this paper are in line with well-­proven methods for 
I–O analyses [62–64].
The tool has recently been used in relation to specific aqua-
culture species (e.g., [65]), for the production segment in a 
country [25] or parts of the supply chain [66]. However, to our 
knowledge, this approach has not been used for the whole sup-
ply chain of an industry within a country, where models could 
yield regional or local decomposition. Johansen et  al. [67] 
studied the overall effects of the Norwegian seafood industry 
but used national account data to estimate the total effect 
on the economy (from each part of the value chain) without 
being able to break down results by county or municipality. 
In Norway, the customary starting point is to use I–O matri-
ces from Statistics Norway, the public agency that provides 
the official statistics for Norway. With accounts for individual 
firms and purchase data (internal firm data), we are also able 
to adapt I–O matrices to suit the actual purchase behavior of 
the Norwegian seafood industry.
As socioeconomic impacts from the Norwegian salmon indus-
try, we consider value creation and employment in all parts of 
the domestic aquaculture value chain (Figure 2). We consider 
the activities that move the salmon along the value chain as core 
activities, which consist of three main activities: production (roe, 
smolt, and grow-­out), processing, and sales of salmon. Ripple ef-
fects are then calculated for 10 successive rounds of purchases 
from suppliers, from the suppliers' suppliers, and so on for each 
link in the value chain. We focus on the industry's activity, thus 
excluding induced effects, that is, the use of the income from 
employees that may yield societal impacts beyond the industry, 
such as general services (e.g., restaurants) or general public sec-
tor services (e.g., schools and health services) even though these 
can also be impacted by the activity created by a growing aqua-
culture industry [23].
Value creation is calculated on the basis of individual compa-
nies' financial statements, which are publicly available infor-
mation in Norway, held by the Norwegian Register of Company 
Accounts register6 and may be accessed and downloaded for re-
search through data management services like www.​forva​lt.​no. 
Similarly, data on employment in all Norwegian enterprises are 
publicly available through the Central Coordinating Register for 
Legal Entities (CCR).7 Both data sets include business addresses, 
allowing the geographical placement of each company. Before 
calculating ripple effects, internal trade within corporations is 
removed from the purchases, as well as raw materials (such as 
fish) and imports (as we calculate only national ripple effects) 
(Figure 3).

Ripple effects are calculated on the basis of I–O matrices pro-
vided by Statistics Norway (www.​ssb.​no). These are updated 
every year and show trade between industries at either the two-­
digit NACE8 level or for groupings of two-­digit categories. This 
is a high level of industry aggregation and a limitation when the 
goal is to analyze the economic impact of more narrowly defined 
industry segments, such as aquaculture or its downstream pro-
cessing/sales activities. Earlier studies have often approached 
this limitation by using disaggregated I–O matrices based on 
additional information collected through interviews, question-
naires, and similar methods. We have, as an alternative, chosen 
a bottom-­up approach to constructing the starting point of the 
analysis. To define the core activity, we have built a custom-­
made database of aquaculture companies where the sum and 
structure of purchases are obtained from financial statements 
and collected supplier ledger data. This enables us to precisely 
define our core activity outside the constraints of predefined 
NACE categories and further create subsegments according to 
production type, geographical location, and other relevant fac-
tors. Thus, two important adjustments are made before connect-
ing to the I–O model and conducting our calculations:
First, on the basis of detailed ledgers from a sample of aquacul-
ture firms, we have established a detailed pattern of purchases 
(both by industry and region/municipality) to calibrate our 
I–O model, allowing for more precise estimations than using 
standard aggregate I–O matrices. With the first round of rip-
ple effects calculated by actual purchases from the aquaculture 
industry, effects in Cycles 2–10 will also be modeled more pre-
cisely. Second, we also made some important modifications to 
the I–O matrix of the second cycle of calculations with respect 
to where the suppliers purchase their inputs. Most of the core 
industry's purchases are made from Norwegian suppliers, lead-
ing to a low “import leakage” of ripple effects. These suppliers, 
on the other hand, purchase a significant share of their inputs 
from abroad, meaning that there is an import leakage in Cycle 2,

The geographic distribution of ripple effects (value creation and 
employment) is calculated for every municipality in Norway, 
based on the principles of economic gravity models, originally 
made to model trade between countries, but recently also used 
for I–O analyses for interregional domestic trade [69, 70]. In our 
model, trade between two municipalities is determined by the 
distance between them and the size of various industries in each 
municipality. We first calculate the distribution of ripple effects 
by distance and size, respectively; then, the effects are weighted 
to estimate the final impact. For the core activity, the geographic 
distribution of effects is determined by the registered business 
address of individual companies in our population. Here, neces-
sary adjustments are made to place the activity in regions where 
the actual production activity takes place, since several larger 
companies often consist of a main office registered in a larger 
city, with production facilities in different regions. Especially in 
aquaculture, the production activities might be located in sev-
eral different municipalities, while the license owner has a sin-
gle business address.
4   |   Results
The results will be presented for the core sectors production, 
processing, and sales, respectively, before we sum up the ag-
gregate socioeconomic impacts and show the ripple effects. For 
each section, we present an overview of the activity over time 
(production and processing) and then a look at the geographical 
distribution at the municipality level.
4.1   |   Production
The main reason for the increased importance of aquaculture in 
Norway is the increased production, from around 600,000 tonnes 
in 2005 to around 1,650,000 tonnes in 2022. However, there are 
also important regional differences in terms of where produc-
tion takes place, and these are changing over time (Figure 4). 
Production has grown more slowly in the south and west, mean-
ing that over time, the center of gravity for the industry has 
moved northwards. For Northern Norway (Nordland, Troms, 
and Finnmark), their share of production has increased from 
33% in 2005 to 42%–44% in later years. Vestland, on the other 
hand, decreased its share from 28% to 21%–23%, as production 
in 2022 was only marginally higher than in 2012.
Salmon production is found in most municipalities along the 
coast, and thus, municipalities with a large proportion of em-
ployees in aquaculture can be found all along the coast from 
Rogaland to Finnmark. There are 11,175 employees in produc-
tion, including broodstock and smolt. Frøya is the largest munic-
ipality by production, with an average of 71,000 tonnes in 2021 
and 2022 (Figure 5). Production (and thus production shares) 
may vary by 50%–100% from year to year in some regions due to 
strict coordination of smolt release, slaughter, and fallowing over 
a production period of 14–24 months. This effect is particularly 
strong in Møre and Romsdal and Trøndelag. For a meaningful 
comparison between municipalities, we thus need an average of 
at least 2 years. Since 2005, trends have been very clear, though.

4.3   |   Sales
In any given year, 90%–95% of Norwegian production of salmon 
is exported [48]. The rest is distributed fresh or processed for 
domestic consumption. The export of salmon takes place both 
through large, vertically integrated companies (that sell their 
own fish, the likes of Lerøy, Mowi, and Grieg) and a handful 
of independent exporters (like Coast Seafood, Norwell, and Star 
Seafood) that sell salmon from a range of small or medium-­
sized producers. Exporters are to a larger degree found in larger 
cities, like Bergen, Ålesund, and Tromsø, or in smaller cities 
with either a large producer (like Salmar in Frøya) or strong 
sales traditions (like the municipality Kinn, where one finds 
the abovementioned Coast Seafood, Norwell, and Star Seafood) 
(Figure 8). In 2022, approximately 1200 people were employed 
in the sales of salmon.

4.4   |   Ripple Effects and Aggregate Socioeconomic 
Impacts
The total number of employees in core salmon production 
(the roe, smolt, and grow-­out sectors) had reached 11,175 in 
2022 (Figure 9). Including the ripple effects constituted by the 
suppliers, we estimate that these constitute 25,000 employees, 
and accordingly, that the total employment is around 36,000 
for the production sector. In processing, around 7300 people 
are directly employed, and we estimate another 9400 employ-
ees in ripple effects, giving a total employment of 16,800 in 
this part of the supply chain. The direct employment in sales 
is fairly limited at 1200, but with even larger (in relative terms) 
ripple effects of 3800 employees. In total, we estimate that 
there are 19,600 employees in core activities and more than 
38,000 employees created by the ripple effects of the indus-
try, giving a total employment of 57,800 associated with the 
Norwegian aquaculture sector.
When looking at value creation, the production created a value 
of 40 billion NOK, while ripple effects were estimated to be an 
additional 27 billion NOK. As such, the industry reached a total 
value added of 67 billion NOK. Processing created a value of 5 
billion NOK and 10.6 billion NOK in ripple effects, while sales 
created a value of 2.3 billion NOK through core activities and an 
additional 3.9 billion NOK in ripple effects.
The two panels in Figure 9 illustrate the importance of using 
both employment and value creation to measure ripple effects, 
as they tell different parts of the same story. Looking at employ-
ment, ripple effects from production seem huge, while looking 
at value creation, they seem modest. This difference is evident 
when looking at multipliers for each segment of the value chain. 
Multipliers are calculated by dividing the sum of e.g., employ-
ment in the core activity and as ripple effects over core activ-
ity only.
The multiplier for employment in production (3.23) (see also 
Table 1) is significantly stronger than for value creation (1.66). 
The large multiplier for production (3.23) is a result of the way 
salmon production in Norway is organized, where producers 
rely on a range of inputs (like feed) and services, like well-­boat 
transportation, de-­licing, net-­cleaning, diving, etc. For value 
creation, the multiplier is much lower, 1.66.
The difference in impact as measured by employment and value 
creation, respectively, is primarily explained by the extremely 
high value creation in production, as the industry has been im-
mensely profitable for more than a decade [36, 37, 72, 73]. While 
the average value creation per employee in Norway is around 
890,000 NOK, aquaculture production creates 3.6 million NOK, 
or four times the average (Figure 10). Value creation in process-
ing, on the other hand, is lower than the national average due 
to low profitability (and, as might be expected for an industry 
competing with low-­cost locations like Poland and Lithuania, 
wages are not high either).9 On the other hand, value creation in 
sales is also high.
For processing, multipliers are not very different for employ-
ment and value creation, but quite high. A multiplier of 3.19 in 
processing may seem high, but it may be down to the fact that 
processing is highly automated and large-­scale, only requiring 
NOK 1 per kilo produced in labor cost, while some of the input, 
packing in particular, has become more expensive, constituting 
around NOK 2 per kilo [74].
For sales, the difference is again large: 2.69 for value creation 
and 4.08 for employment. The high multiplier for employ-
ment is due to the high amount of transport services bought 
by sales organizations. Again, the explanation for the lower 
multiplier for value creation is due to the high value creation 
in sales organizations (1.84 million NOK) vs. the lower wages 
(1.0 million NOK) in the sectors constituting the ripple effects 
from sales.

5   |   Discussion
5.1   |   Ripple Effects and the Suppliers to 
Aquaculture
Norwegian salmon aquaculture production relies, as the ripple ef-
fects demonstrate, on a range of input factors and services. While 
feed is the most important in terms of value (roughly half the pur-
chase from aquaculture is feed), this share is not reflected in its 
importance for creating ripple effects in Norway, as 92% of the in-
gredients are imported [68]. What creates the huge ripple effects is 
the range of goods and services that the industry purchases, partly 
because a number of the functions that farmers used to do them-
selves have been scaled up, professionalized, and are performed by 
independent suppliers [42], and an increasing effort at controlling 
lice and disease [75–78]. Ripple effects from aquaculture produc-
tion in Norway are more than twice as large as core employment 
(around 25,000 vs. 11,200, as seen from Figure 9 above).
Ripple effects from aquaculture are found all along the coast. 
With each production site, some ripple effects are present, as 
farmers buy a range of services locally, including those from 
craftsmen like plumbers, electricians, and carpenters, as well 
as general services like transportation, cleaning, and food. 
There are also some aquaculture-­specific services that are 
very dispersed, like diving, but the general trend seems to be 
that the more specialized the product or service, the more con-
centrated the suppliers tend to be. Still, most of the ripple ef-
fects are found in the five largest counties for production, but 
with significant ripple effects also in counties with no produc-
tion, such as Viken and Oslo (Figure 11). While the center of 
gravity in aquaculture production and processing has moved 
northwards, the majority of suppliers are still to be found in 
Trøndelag and the south. While the share of production for 
Nordland, Troms, and Finnmark has been 42%–44% in later 
years (as shown earlier in Figure 4), the share of employment 
with suppliers is below 30%.

Looking at employment from ripple effects at the municipality 
level (Figure 12), we find a range of larger cities among the top mu-
nicipalities. The capital, Oslo, has the highest ripple effect (mea-
sured as employment), with Bergen, Ålesund, Stavanger, Bærum 
(neighboring Oslo), and Trondheim high on the list. While larger 
cities score highest, it is also an important trait that aquaculture 
municipalities with large production in remote areas like Frøya, 
Senja, Nærøysund, and Hadsel also score high on ripple effects. 
These municipalities are home to both a large number of suppliers 
and some of the more specialized ones. While the larger cities may 
seem dominant here, the number of municipalities that receive 
ripple effects ensures that most of the ripple effects are found in 
the periphery (or at least not in the larger cities).
The growing base of specialized suppliers to the aquacul-
ture industry also increasingly serves a global aquaculture 
industry, meaning that suppliers may grow even larger than 
the Norwegian aquaculture industry allows for. Such suppli-
ers include producers of feed, nets and rings, feeding barges, 
mooring systems, monitoring systems, de-­licing equipment, 
genetics, vaccines, RAS-­systems, well-­boats, service boats, 
and a range of other goods and services [59]. This, of course, 
makes suppliers an even more important part of the Norwegian 
seafood industry. Exports from the specialized supplier indus-
try have been estimated by us to be 10.3 and 11.9 billion NOK 
in 2022 and 2023, respectively.
5.2   |   Tax Contributions and Returns
The aquaculture industry has also become important for its tax 
contribution to the Norwegian economy, from companies, em-
ployees, and salmon farming tax schemes. While the aquaculture 
industry constitutes 1.5% of the Norwegian mainland economy 
(Figure 2), its tax contribution is around 5.7% of Norwegian taxes 
from businesses, or almost four times as much as the average for 
businesses [71]. The main reason for this high contribution is of 
course a very high profitability. It is worthwhile to note that these 
tax contributions are only ordinary business taxes; since 2023, 
the producers also pay an additional “rent tax” of 25% on top of 
the 22% tax on profits (with a certain “free profit” before the land 
rent tax applies) [79].
The industry is also contributing to public revenue through other 
fees, such as production licenses (also not included in the per-
centages above; if included, the tax contribution in 2022 would be 
7.4% of Norwegian taxes from businesses). Every second year, the 
industry may increase its production by a maximum of 6%, with 
no growth or negative growth in areas with too high lice levels. 
The increased capacity is auctioned either as new licenses or as a 
maximum of 6% additions to existing ones. The proceeds from the 
auctions are collected for the Aquaculture Fund,10 before being 
split between counties and municipalities hosting aquaculture. 
This incentivizes municipalities to facilitate aquaculture, as the 
proceedings are calculated based on both existing and new sea 
sites. In 2022, 142 municipalities received a tax contribution from 
the Aquaculture Fund, with payments to municipalities ranging 
from 116 million NOK down to 0.6 million (Figure 13).
Payments from this fund totaled 2.7 billion NOK in 2022 and 
have reached a total of 14 billion NOK over the period from its 
establishment in 2017 to 2024.

With an employment of 57,800, including ripple effects, the aqua-

dustry (34,000), the industry traditionally perceived to be the main 
factor in maintaining remote coastal communities. However, this 
is a mixed blessing in that a number of policies are restricting the 
fishing industry's ability to exploit new opportunities, while the 
aquaculture industry has been able to try out different organiza-
tional structures. This has led to a heterogeneous aquaculture in-
dustry with smaller as well as larger companies, and where some 
activities have been outsourced from the producers, while others 
have been integrated. While we do not have good data to assess 
the ripple effects of the industry in its early days, it is still safe to 
say that the number of tasks that are performed by specialized 
service providers and suppliers outside of the production compa-
nies has increased significantly, as in the industry's early days, the 
farmers did virtually everything including mixing the feed [80]. 
Hence, the importance of the ripple effects cannot be understated 
if one is to assess the socioeconomic impact of the industry. Our 
results show that most of the employment occurs outside of the 
industry, with 38,000 jobs relative to 19,700 jobs in the core ac-
tivities of production, processing, and sales. It is also interesting 
that the value added is much more concentrated in the production 
part of the supply chain than in employment. However, this is not 
surprising in a highly profitable industry, as the primary produc-
ers are the place in the supply chain where rents generally occur, 
as one can also observe in oil, mining, and fishing.
The aquaculture industry provides an increasing number of 
jobs in a range of coastal communities. A significant share of 
the employment, and not only in the production sector, is found 
in smaller communities along the coast. This makes the aqua-
culture industry very important for the sustainability of these 
communities, although there is also significant heterogene-
ity, as processing and supporting industries are often more 
concentrated than production. However, it is also interesting to 
note how a significant part of the ripple effects occurs in the 
larger cities. While there are many potential reasons for this, 
access to skilled labor and thicker input markets may be a key 
factor, and Asche et al. [81], Naylor et al. [3], Prodhan et al. [82], 
and Rahman et al. [83] all provide examples of how knowledge 
capacity is important for an aquaculture industry to succeed and 
grow. Norway's salmon industry is showing the same, but to an 
even larger extent.
The great reach and importance of the ripple effects from the in-
dustry is the most important general insight from the analysis. 
This suggests that a focus on the production part of the industry 
or not accounting for suppliers and service providers can signifi-
cantly understate the socioeconomic impact of the industry. In 
some dimensions, these effects may be larger for the Norwegian 
industry than for other industries, partly due to the size of the com-
panies and the capital-­intensive pen-­based production technology. 
However, the industry is highly dependent on imports for feed, 
the most important input factor, and it is highly export-­oriented 
with a significant share of the processing taking place after the 
salmon have left Norway [58]. An industry that to a larger extent 
uses domestic raw materials for feed would generate larger ripple 
effects in that dimension. Belton et al. [28] show that farmed sea-
food is traded to a lesser extent than wild seafood. Garlock et al. [1] 
and Hobbs et al. [84] note that farmed seafood is often produced 
closer to the final market and large population concentrations, 
and Garlock et al. [85] show that countries with higher aquacul-
ture production consume more seafood. Hence, the many aqua-
culture industries where the production is consumed locally are 
also likely to generate larger domestic ripple effects.
