### Background
Metier AS, commissioned by Kartverket, updates a socio-economic analysis of Marine grunnkart i kystsonen. Version 3.0 is dated 9 November 2023. Earlier versions are 15 October 2021 and 19 October 2022. The programme is owned by Kartverket, Norges geologiske undersøkelse, and Havforskningsinstituttet. The report is written as support for a state-budget bid. The cover marks it exempt from public access under offentleglova section 22.

The problem stated is a missing common infrastructure for coastal marine data. Kartverket is said to have mapped about 35 percent of the coast, NGU 10 percent, and HI 5 percent. Data sit in many agencies. Municipalities designate coastal-plan area. Counties adopt regional sea-area plans. Uncertified private mapping is said to force remapping. Only two private firms are said to be certified for official sea surveying.

### Methods
The analysis is a cost-benefit study following Finansdepartementet circular R 109/14 and DFØ guidance. Priced effects are in kroner where the authors judge that defensible. Other effects are scored from + to ++++. The coast is defined as land to one nautical mile outside the baseline (93 825 km2). Offshore farming, oil and gas, and Svalbard are excluded. Version 3 presents only the full-mapping and full-access alternative (former Tiltak 3). Optimal mapping is assumed at 80 percent of the coastal zone (75 000 km2), with data in a seamless infrastructure. The analysis life is 40 years (2025-2064) at a 4 percent discount rate.

Metier states that judgements are its own, and also that cost and benefit estimates use input from Kartverket, HI, NGU, and user groups. Annex 1 lists named interviews from March to June 2021. Uncertainty workshops were held on 27 and 31 May 2021. The 2023 update carries those ranges forward after a price refresh. Benefits are identified through stakeholder talks and written sources. No experience figures are said to transfer directly.

### Main findings
Table 1 states expected net present value of 34 033 million NOK in 2023 kroner, up from 24 647 million in the 2021 version (2021 kroner). The authors attribute most of the rise to higher first-hand value of salmon and rainbow trout in 2021-2022, which enters the two largest aquaculture benefit items. The summary page once writes 34 033 billion NOK. The table, the figure parts in million NOK, and the later text use million. That is the unit filed here.

Six priced items enter the NPV. Annual benefit at the 80 percent mapping assumption is 3 939 million NOK. Cost NPV is 4 458 million, including a 20 percent tax-financing cost of 743 million. The two largest aquaculture items are a one-off 3 percent production increase (3 030 million NOK per year from 2021-2022 volume and first-hand value) and lower operating cost from more suitable sites (4 percent of volume moved from poor to good sites, 10 000 NOK per ton, 659 million NOK per year). Reduced application costs are 9 million NOK per year (about 36 refusals a year from FAKS 2021-2022, a 50 percent cut, 0.5 million NOK per application). Fisheries items are fuel (44 million), time (189 million), and CO2 (8 million).

Table 6 also prices kelp restoration (936 million NOK per year from a 3 percent restoration of 2 000 km2 urchin-grazed kelp valued at 15.6 million NOK per km2) and economy-wide ripple (1 806 to 1 809 million NOK per year at 0.597 kr per kr of the 3 030 million production item). Chapter 7.1 keeps both out of the NPV. Maps alone are said not to restore kelp, and circular R 109/14 is cited for staying one step in the cause chain.

Non-priced effects scored ++++ include new coastal industries (land-based farming, other species, kelp, IMTA, floating solar, offshore wind) and coastal-area planning (higher-quality plans under the Planning and Building Act, including aquaculture and nature consideration zones). Inter-municipal planning and shorter processing are scored +++. Chapter 4.6 uses Herøy (harbour works), Stavanger (multi-use sea toward 2040, lobster protection, nature consideration zones), and Statsforvalteren in Rogaland (sediment and pollution cases) as pilot examples. The Husevågøy cauliflower-coral case is used as a conflict that maps might have avoided after a permit was withdrawn and later litigated.

Mapping is assumed to ramp from 1 percent in 2025 to 5 percent a year from 2029, reaching 80 percent in 2040. Benefits are assumed to start two years after mapping. The first five years (priority areas, about 15 percent of the coast, about 10 500 km2) are assumed to give about 50 percent more return. After 20 years, annual benefit is assumed to fall linearly toward zero in 2063 as maps age. Chapter 8 concludes that real-option value is limited.

### Uncertainty and limitations
The authors say there are few realised-benefit figures and that uncertainty is large. The 3 percent production increase is an assumption (new permits, expansions, and higher use of existing MAB), not a measured effect of maps. It needs uptake by management and industry, a political wish to grow salmon and trout, a market, unchanged prices, and sustainable production. NIVA is cited as saying as much as 60 percent of today's sites may be less than optimal for carrying capacity. That figure supports the 4 percent move assumption. It is not a measured map effect.

Benefit uncertainty analysis reports expected benefit 7 107 million NOK (including kelp and ripple in the 6 684 million base) with a 31 percent relative standard deviation. The tornado diagram names the aquaculture production item, ripple, and kelp as the largest drivers. A pessimistic production case is that maps are unused and do not solve lice. User costs of capturing the benefits are not included in priced benefit. Investment-cost uncertainty is 34 percent relative standard deviation. Political annual appropriations are named as a cost risk.

### Relevance to this corpus
This is a mapping-investment case. It argues that a shared marine fact base would help coastal plans and aquaculture locality choice. It does not evaluate how municipalities or counties actually allocate sea area. Tag A4 only.
