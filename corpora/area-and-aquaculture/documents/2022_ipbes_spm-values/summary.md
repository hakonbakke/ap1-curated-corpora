### Background
This file is the IPBES Summary for Policymakers (SPM) of the Methodological Assessment Report on the Diverse Values and Valuation of Nature. The Plenary of 139 member States approved the SPM at IPBES 9 in Bonn, 3-9 July 2022. It is the short agreed text. It is not the full chapter volume (see 2022_ipbes_values-assessment).

### Methods
The underlying assessment used about 100 selected experts, about 200 contributing authors, more than 13,000 scientific publications, and a body of indigenous and local knowledge. The SPM states key messages with traceable accounts into chapters and IPBES confidence language (well established, or established but incomplete). This curation walked all 52 extract pages, including front matter, KM1 to KM10, background messages A to D, figures, and the appendix on confidence.

### Main findings
KM1 links the biodiversity crisis, and chances to address it, to how nature is valued in political and economic decisions. Unsustainable use and unequal access to nature's contributions are tied to a narrow set of values, often those traded in markets.

KM2 states that most policymaking has prioritized a narrow set of values at the expense of nature, society, and future generations, and has often ignored values associated with indigenous peoples' and local communities' world-views. GDP-style indicators generally capture only market values of nature.

KM3 and background A offer a typology. Specific values are grouped as instrumental, relational, and intrinsic. People may live from, with, in, and as nature. Those frames can overlap. KM5 states that more than 50 valuation methods exist, grouped into four families. Choice involves trade-offs among relevance, robustness, and resources.

KM6 and C3 state that less than 5 percent of published valuation studies report uptake in policy decisions, and that a majority of countries had not met Aichi Target 2 (integrating biodiversity values into strategies, planning, and accounting) by 2020.

KM7 to KM9 treat institutions and transformative change. Four leverage points are named: undertaking valuation, embedding values in decisions, reforming policy, and shifting societal goals. A9 states that predominant economic and political decisions have prioritized market-based instrumental values, often at the expense of non-market instrumental, relational, and intrinsic values.

Figure SPM 3 maps and characterises valuation studies (about 79,000 identified, 1,163 reviewed in depth after sampling). Forests are stated as 25 percent of ecological contexts. Aquaculture appears as one listed context beside forests, cultivated areas, coastal areas, wetlands, deep sea, and others. Remaining context shares sit in a bar chart in the extract. An exact aquaculture percentage is not filed. The SPM does not analyse aquaculture operations or Norwegian coastal planning. A8 mentions local and indigenous institutions for protecting forests and coastal ecosystems as an example of norms that emphasise responsibility for nature.

### Uncertainty and limitations
Confidence terms vary by message. A value-action gap is named. Values alone do not fully explain behaviour. Partner statements in the front matter are not key messages. This SPM does not contain a Norway aquaculture case. Figure SPM 1 and parts of Figure SPM 3 are garbled in the extract.

### Relevance to this corpus
Peripheral. The object is diverse values of nature in global decision-making. Aquaculture and coastal ecosystems appear only as a figure category or a general example. The file belongs on A3 (values in trade-offs), not as a siting paper.
