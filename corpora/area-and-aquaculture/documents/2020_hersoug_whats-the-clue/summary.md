### Background
Hersoug, Mikkelsen and Osmundsen ask why Norwegian salmon farming reports a shortage of coastal space, and what planning, technology or money could change that. Government ambitions cited include a possible doubling by 2030 and a fivefold rise by 2050.

### Methods
The article mixes review and original synthesis. It draws on more than ten years of the authors' work, Norwegian and English literature, Mikkelsen et al. (2019) on possible planning changes, and a qualitative reading of municipal incentives (jobs, value added, environment, electoral incentives). Future supply and demand are assessed qualitatively. Several tables and numbers in this extract are OCR-damaged.

### Main findings
Grow-out still happens mainly in open coastal pens. A farm needs a production licence and a locality licence. Counties coordinate the locality permit. Several sector authorities can veto. Municipal plans under the Planning and Building Act apply to one nautical mile beyond the baselines.

Physical pen area is described as small (about 59 km2 for 900 licences in 2010). Anchoring-area occupation is about 420 km2, with a later update around 520 km2, slightly more than 0.5 percent of inshore coastal area. The tighter constraint is sanitary spacing (at least 2.5 km between farms and 5 km to a processing plant, illustrated as a 5 km zone occupying about 20 percent of coastal-zone water), plus fisheries, shipping, tourism, recreation, conservation (about 3 percent of the coastal zone, ambition 7 percent), 53 national salmon rivers and 27 national salmon fjords, energy, and military use (Navy exclusive rights to about 30 percent of inshore area in Troms).

Production rose from 474 000 tons in 1999 to 1 350 000 tons in 2019 on 966 localities. The in-year active-use count is OCR-damaged. Hordaland used 99 percent of approved localities. Finnmark used 73 percent, with much lower density. The authors say the binding limit at the time was mainly production licences and traffic-light growth, not empty sea. About 900 localities are given a total of about 3 million tons MAB. With around one third fallow, actual potential is around 2 million tons MAB. Doubling by 2030 is called impossible under the then licence-growth rules, while a doubling is theoretically possible within already allocated areas.

Central government could force aquaculture priority only by overhauling allocation and municipal planning, which they call politically unlikely. Only three planning changes are judged likely to increase aquaculture area. Inter-municipal planning is the most promising inside-the-box option. Remaining paths are lower lice, emissions and escapes, more legitimacy, and a larger municipal share of value. After a rejected 40 percent resource-rent proposal, government offered a production fee of NOK 0.40 per kg and cut the municipal share of new-capacity payments to 40 percent. Land-based, offshore, closed pens and large smolt could change coastal demand, with uncertain effect on Norway's global share.

### Uncertainty and limitations
Technology and incentive effects are qualitative. Some tables in the extract are damaged. The paper does not produce a new statistical test of how much area is missing. Whether the fee and Fund change will unlock space is left open.

### Relevance to this corpus
It is the clearest single map of the area challenge: not empty sea, but usable, permitted, spaced and accepted sea.
