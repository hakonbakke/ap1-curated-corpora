### Background
Mikkelsen, Karlsen and Osmundsen ask how area planning of Norwegian sea areas could be changed, and what that might mean for aquaculture access, other interests, and the efficiency of plan processes. The report is part of FHF project Havbruksforvaltning 2030 (area-management work package). Growth in salmon volume had stalled in 2016 and 2017 relative to 2015, after lice restrictions and a tighter lice regime from 2015.

### Methods
The report is a policy options paper. It reviews planning theory and the Planning and Building Act, describes current aquaculture and sea-area administration, discusses incentives to facilitate aquaculture (jobs, tax, Havbruksfondet), and compiles 2017 site-use and biomass statistics from the Directorate of Fisheries. Six planning aspects are varied in turn: plan area, planning authority, participation, knowledge base, trade-off methods, and plan form. There is no new interview study in the extract.

### Main findings
Industry respondents in a 2015 survey cited by the authors (Hersoug et al. 2014) named area access as the largest problem (more than 60 percent). In 2017, Hordaland used 99 percent of approved salmon and trout sea sites during the year. Other counties left about 10 to 30 percent unused. Average monthly use of approved sites was about 50 to 70 percent. Hordaland also had the highest locality density (more than eight times Finnmark) and the highest peak biomass per km2 of coastal zone. Møre og Romsdal had the highest standing biomass relative to approved locality MTB. The three northern counties hold more than 60 percent of coastal-zone sea area, and with Trøndelag more than 75 percent.

Chapter 2 treats municipal willingness to host sites. Value-chain employment is cited as a little over 33 000 årsverk in 2016, with about 21 percent in aquaculture itself. Property tax on cages and buildings (not fish) is described as modest (about 26 500 kr per approved locality per year at the then maximum rate). Havbruksfondet and 2018 capacity sales are stated as more than 3.9 billion kroner, with 80 percent to municipalities and counties. Municipalities received about 3 million kroner per locality for 2018-2019 combined. The authors say this raises the incentive to host sites, while lice, escapes and treatment controversies weaken it. They cannot rank municipality, county and state.

Many possible planning changes are assessed. Few are said to raise aquaculture area with certainty or high probability. Three candidates are named: larger plan areas (so more interests can be accommodated in one plan), auction of pre-screened commercial blocks, and marine industrial parks where commercial users coordinate inside a public envelope. If auction income went to planning authorities, that would add an incentive to designate blocks.

Other changes are proposed to make trade-offs more predictable rather than to expand area: more equal treatment across geography for regional and state interests, systematic knowledge collected outside plan processes, and public access to that knowledge so debate is less driven by misconception. Requiring inter-municipal plans or making regional coastal plans legally binding is judged politically unlikely in the near term after the 2019 Municipalities Act.

### Uncertainty and limitations
The authors state there is large uncertainty and disagreement about aquaculture effects on environment and other users. Reform effects are reasoned, not measured. Coastal-zone open-cage technology is taken as the baseline. Offshore, closed and mobile systems are flagged as possible later changes to planning premises. Some figure values in the extract are images and not fully readable as exact shares. Value-creation per locality is labelled a rough estimate.

### Relevance to this corpus
This is a core options map for who plans sea area, at what scale, with what knowledge and trade-off method, and which money incentives may move municipalities.
