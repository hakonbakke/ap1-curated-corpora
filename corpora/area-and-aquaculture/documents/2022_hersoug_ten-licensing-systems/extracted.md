Norwegian aquaculture

A B S T R A C T  
Licensing has been a key instrument in the management of Norwegian aquaculture.This article reviews the 
licensing regime right from the start in 1973 to the present.Originally there was one licensing regime for 
commercial production of salmon and trout, while today there are ten different systems. The article reviews these 
ten systems, with particular emphasis on the special purpose licenses, most often created on request from the 
industry. The green licenses as well as development licenses are described more in detail, while licenses for land- 
based and offshore production are also included. While the special purpose licenses no doubt have served col­
lective needs in the industry, such as education, research and innovation, they have also acted as backdoor to 
cheaper licenses. (The ordinary commercial licenses now are traded in the range of USD 15–20 million for a 
standard license of 780 tons of maximum allowable biomass (MAB)). Their effects on the ordinary license system, 
where growth is now regulated by the traffic light system, seem to be largely underestimated. In spite of turning 
the allocation of licenses into a more objective process, there is still a large degree of political and administrative 
discretion involved. The complete system has over time become very complicated and difficult to control. At­
tempts of reforming the system have so far largely been unsuccessful. The article ends by describing various 
reform proposals and concludes with some generic lessons.   
1. Introduction 
The Chinese leader D`eng Xiǎopíng is known for the idea of "One 
country, two systems" as a possible model for the reunification of China. 
His idea was that there was only one China, but that areas such as Hong 
Kong, Macao and Taiwan could have separate economic and political 
systems, while the rest of China continued under a so-called socialist 
system. If we move to salmon farming in Norway, there is one industry, 
but it is subject to ten different licensing systems. From the start, there 
was only one license system, while the others have been established 
later, mainly on request from the industry. Many have questioned 
whether the current plethora of licensing systems is rational, and 
whether it is necessary, or whether, on the contrary, it is a sign that the 
industry over time has developed different collective needs, which are 
best taken care of through flexible license schemes. 
If we look at the development in and of the aquaculture industry in a 
50-year perspective, there are several striking contrasts. The industry 
has gone from being a small and marginal industry, run by small en­
trepreneurs based on trial and error, to a large, industrialized, science- 
driven industry, which today is Norway’s third largest export industry. 
Although open cage farming is still dominant, there are now several 
alternative solutions, such as land-based, offshore farming, post-smolt 
production (on land) and closed or semi-closed cage facilities. At the 
same time, also the administration of the sector has changed. Admit­
tedly, the system of licenses is fixed, as in 1973, but the authorities today 
are less concerned with who gets a license than with how they operate, 
which follows from a stronger focus on the environment and 
sustainability. 
The system that regulates industry growth has changed from an 
irregular, discretionary allocation to more objective criteria, based on 
indicators (until now, only the number of salmon lice). Furthermore, the 
system of allocation of permits for free has been abandoned in favor of 
auction and, to a lesser degree, the use of fixed prices. At the same time, 
there has been a strong concentration of companies, where ten com­
panies are currently responsible for about 70% of total production [44]. 
Municipalities and county municipalities that previously did not receive 
any compensation for the use of local coastal areas, have now received 
an annual production fee as well as 40% of the payments for new

2. Regulating through licensing 
In the classic economic literature, the process of public regulation is 
seen as a consequence of market failure, while in political science and 
organizational literature the transformation from market to public 
management is often seen as a change from one steering system to 
another. According to Hernes [16], inspired by Dahl and Lindblom [8], 
we can operate with four steering systems: markets, hierarchies, poly­
archies and negotiations between organized interests. In the empirical world 
we will seldom find pure systems, instead it is a question of what type of 
goods and services are best (and most legitimately) distributed through 
the various systems. The crucial point is that with market solutions 
public authorities will normally limit themselves to manipulating the 
framework, for example by the use of subsidies or setting of standards, 
thus influencing the choice of operators. With hierarchical control 
certain allocation issues are removed from the field of individual choice 
and transferred to public organs, which in turn will be responsible for 
deciding who should be allowed to enter and who should be excluded. 
Such decisions have to be made according to law, thus granting legiti­
macy to the decisions. While criteria regarding market operations are 
seemingly straight forward (connected to buying power), the criteria for 
selecting operators in public processes are most often complicated and 
fuzzy. They end up being decided according to political and managerial 
discretion [17]. 
As pointed out by Mikalsen [37], the administrative allocation of 
rights does not imply the elimination of competition, but that access to 
competition is regulated (and normally limited). According to Hernes 
[16] licensing may be seen as foreclosing the decisions of the market. 
Without intervention the market would have eliminated many operators 
and in the next round possibly opened the arena for new operators. 
Hence, the judgment of the market is not effectuated, because effects 
may be deemed too costly in political terms. However, with the transfer 
of responsibility from the market to a public hierarchy, the public au­
thorities gain responsibility for the result of the allocation. In our case it 
means that the salmon farmers become clients. Consequently, the critical 
issue is how the authorities organize the allocation in terms of goals, 
criteria and administrative processes [21,23]. 
A license is according to the dictionary “an official document that 
gives you permission to own, do, or use something” [5]. In general that 
means you are prohibited from performing certain activities without 
permission, i.e. a license. In the Norwegian context the aquaculture li­
cense is in reality twofold: a production license that gives the opportunity 
to have a certain production volume, stated as tons of maximum 
allowable biomass (MAB) at any time, and a site (locality) license that 
gives the opportunity to farm in a given area. Both licenses are required 
to farm. Furthermore, there are two different types of licenses, where the 
ordinary commercial licenses are subject to a separate regime, the traffic 
light system (see Section 5), which regulates growth, while the special 
licenses mainly follow a different logic, and are not subject to the same 
restrictions. 
Licenses have been used in Norway for more than 100 years to 
regulate industries exploiting natural resources such as fisheries and 
hydroelectric power production. Hence, stakeholders were quite 
familiar with this tool when the Ministry of Fisheries moved to limit the 
number of fish farmers in 1973. This is a process that contains an 
element of path dependency [33]. It is nevertheless interesting to note 
that licensing as a central administrative tool started out in the period 
characterized by direct governance, (hands on) while still “surviving” in 
the present neo-liberal period characterized by “governance at a dis­
tance” [56]. In the meantime, the criteria for being allocated a license 
have changed considerably. 
As pointed out by Scott [57] the state’s capacity to license is special, 
as it is linked to the state monopoly over legitimate coercive power. 
Consequently, regulating aquaculture is done for different reasons, but 
they all depend on being accepted as necessary and valid by most 
stakeholders [30]. This is increasingly framed as a question of sustain­
ability, i.e. environmental, economic and social sustainability. As 
demonstrated by Christiansen and Jakobsen [7] and Aarset et al. [2], 
there is considerable room for different interpretations in the discourses 
dealing with sustainability. Finally it should be noted that while 
licensing in marine aquaculture in general is done to reduce negative 
externalities, the use of special purpose licensing is implemented in 
order to achieve positive externalities, such as increased knowledge, a 
qualified workforce, more innovations, etc. However, this does not 
preclude that even with specific, accepted goals these arrangements may 
also have unanticipated consequences, which will be dealt with in this 
article. 
3. Materials and methods 
The article is a review article, meant to illustrate the development of 
the Norwegian license system for salmon aquaculture from the start (in 
1973) to the current day (2021). Hence we have concentrated on books 
and published (peer reviewed) articles dealing with various aspects of 
the ten licensing sub-systems. Furthermore, we have relied on White 
Papers, evaluation reports as well as press clippings from three of the 
most prominent aquaculture publications (iLaks, E24 and Intrafish). For 
the development of the general licensing system (currently called the 
traffic light system) we have primarily relied on previous research 
connected to the large xlaks project, funded by the Research Council of 
Norway [20], the history of Norwegian aquaculture [69,26] and studies 
related to license allocations [6,21,67]. For the special purpose licenses 
we have used detailed studies of the various subsystems [18,31,19,64, 
9]. The bulk of these studies are based on reports and articles developed 
in the Salmon Regulation Assessment (SARA) project, also funded by the 
Research Council of Norway.

4. From “whom” to “how”: license allocation 1973–2013 
When the first temporary fish farming act was passed in 1973, there 
were 287 fish farms of very different sizes. In the period until the per­
manent law came into force in 1977, a total of 91 new licenses were 
granted. In practice, almost everyone who applied for a license was 
allowed to start, provided they met the mandatory requirements set out 
in the law (which deals with fish diseases, pollution, unfortunate 
placement and technical requirements). Originally, the permits were 
granted with a volume limit of 8000 m3, but in 1975, the size was 
adjusted down to 5000 m3. In 1977, a moratorium was introduced. The 
moratorium lasted until a new law and regulations were enacted in 
1981. The moratorium was due to fear of overproduction and lack of 
smolt, a position strongly supported by the already established salmon 
farmers. The effect was a strengthening of the new industry in the south, 
while it helped to stop growth in the north, where fish farming had just 
started [14]. 
The Permanent Aquaculture Act of 1981 was largely based on the 
recommendations of the Lysø Commission in 1977. The Act was inten­
ded to facilitate the conditions for development by emphasizing the 
following main objectives: "that the development takes place so that 
production is in reasonable proportion to the sales opportunities, that it 
becomes possible to carry out sound financial operations, that the fa­
cilities are located so that the best possible district effect is achieved, 
that the facilities provide safe and good jobs, and that as far as possible 
an ownership structure established based on the owners operating the 
facilities themselves." (Author’s transl.). 
While the preliminary law in 1973 sorted under the Ministry of 
Fisheries, the fish farmers, their organizations and the Ministry of 
Agriculture had fought over how to regulate the new industry; as an 
extension of the fishery or as a support activity for agriculture. In 1981, 
it was evident that the fisheries model had won, even though the disease 
problems still had to be dealt with in accordance with regulations under 
the Ministry of Agriculture. In the meantime, the fish farmers had 
organized themselves in the Norwegian Fish Farmers’ Association 
(NFF).1 They had also established a mandatory sales organization, the 
Fish Farmers’ Sales Union (FOS) in accordance with the provisions of the 
Raw Fish Act, which thirty years earlier had given fishermen exclusive 
rights to ex-vessel sales. This, together with the technological develop­
ment (with the use of net cages in the sea), gradually moved the aqua­
culture industry towards the "fisheries". In addition, the salmon farmers’ 
social background led in the same direction. Aquaculture was definitely 
part of the fishing industry, and the institutions as well as the manage­
ment measures were part of a well-known repertoire in the Ministry of 
Fisheries [26]. 
Therefore, the administrative setup was based on the known struc­
ture with three levels that had already been established for the fisheries. 
All applications were first processed by the local Fisheries Board at the 
municipal level, which submitted its priorities to the Director of Fish­
eries at county level, where the applicants were prioritized by the 
Fisheries Board. The final allocation was made by the Directorate of 
Fisheries, while the Ministry of Fisheries was responsible for processing 
complaints. The number of new licenses and the criteria for allocation 
were determined by the Norwegian Parliament and then delegated to 
the Ministry of Fisheries. 
With such general and partly conflicting goals as stated above, the 
new Aquaculture Act needed more precise regulations. Licenses should 
only be granted to people living in coastal and rural areas with 
employment problems. These applicants should first and foremost have 
no other profession, while exceptions were made for fishermen, farmers 
and owners of small processing plants. There was still plenty of room for 
discretion, depending on the knowledge of the individual applicant and 
the composition of the various committees and boards. In the next round 
this implied all kinds of accusations; of friendship, special treatment and 
outright corruption. However, room for discretion was somewhat 
limited by the general decision (made by the Ministry of Fisheries) that 
licenses should only be awarded to municipalities in the extreme pe­
riphery, which belonged to the most favored zone according to the 
criteria of the District Development Fund. It was also decided that most 
licenses in the first round would be awarded to the three northernmost 
counties, i.e. Nordland, Troms and Finnmark [26]. 
Although the interest was great, the Ministry of Fisheries decided to 
grant only 54 permits in the first round in 1981. Each permit was limited 
to a cage volume of 3000 m3, which theoretically meant an increase of 
10% of the total production capacity. The next round took place in 1983, 
when 100 licenses of 5000 m3 each were awarded, of which 52 ended up 
in northern Norway. In addition, all existing fish farmers could expand 
the volume to 5000 m3, and thus the total production capacity increased 
by approximately 70–80%. In 1985, there was a new round of allocation 
of 150 licenses, and now the volume was set at 8000 m3, which led to a 
dramatic increase in production capacity. In 1989, another 30 licenses 
were granted, but this time only in northern Norway. The production 
volume now increased to 12,000 m3 for a standard license [1]. 
In 1982, a conservative government took over, and the incoming 
Minister of Fisheries publicly declared that his goal was to abandon the 
Aquaculture Act. This policy met with strong opposition in the aqua­
culture industry as well as in the Ministry of Fisheries and the Direc­
torate of Fisheries. It would take three years to draw up a compromise in 
the coalition government, in the form of a new aquaculture act in 1985. 
In this act, the production of smolt was liberalized, i.e. exempted from 
the licensing regime. This is because the shortage of smolt had been the 
de facto limiting factor during the expansion in the late 1970s and early 
1980s. The ownership regulations related to salmon and trout farming 
were liberalized, so that the owners did not necessarily have to operate 
the facilities, but they were to be "local". It was still required that no one 
could have controlling majority interests in more than one farm [17,26]. 
In the meantime, a number of industrial interests had entered the 
field and they wanted to remove all the constraints that were usually 
associated with fishing and fish farming. When the aquaculture sector 
was dealt with in a new report to the Norwegian parliament in 1987, the 
message was consolidation, and the changes that were implemented in 
the Aquaculture Act in 1989 were basically cosmetic. No new rounds of 
license awards were in sight, and buying an existing facility (with a li­
cense) was the only way into the lucrative salmon farming. In 1991, the 
ownership requirements were further liberalized, so that an owner could 
now have a majority interest in several farms, and that such ownership 
should only "as far as possible" be local.

From 1985–2002, there were no ordinary allocation of new licenses. 
It contributed greatly to the consolidation of existing fish farming 
companies. When a new government took office in 2001, it was too late 
to abolish the licensing scheme, so that the path to further growth went 
through new rounds of allocation [21]. For all the allocation rounds in 
the period 2002–2013, specific political goals were to be met. These and 
supplementary provisions were laid down by the Ministry of Fisheries. 
Various considerations were prioritized in each allocation round. The 
process took place in such a way that applicants who were qualified 
according to the minimum requirements were set on an equal footing, 
while the prioritization was made based on the extent to which the 
applicants could contribute to realizing the government’s objectives. It 
could be anything from improved fish health, through ownership and 
company structure to environmental considerations. 
In 2002, 41 licenses were announced, directed to specific munici­
palities along the entire coast. While licenses had previously been 
granted free of charge, a fee of NOK 5 million was now set, with the 
exception of the municipalities in the extreme north (North Troms and 
Finnmark), where the price was NOK 4 million. This was the first time 
that a fee was demanded for the licenses, and according to the Office of 
the Auditor General [51] it was now important to increase the state’s 
revenues. Furthermore, a clause was attached, stating that the licenses 
could not be resold for ten years, and that they had to be kept within the 
municipalities that were prioritized at the time of allocation. Unfortu­
nately, the allocation came at a time when the aquaculture industry was 
in its worst crisis since the wave of bankruptcies that followed the FOS 
bankruptcy in 1991, and the licenses in Finnmark could not be sold, 
even at reduced prices. For the other licenses, the interest was great with 
a total of 113 applicants. The increase in capacity was relatively modest, 
because many fish farmers started stocking much later. The strict re­
quirements for geographical location were undermined only three years 
later, when it was decided that fish farms could be relocated relatively 
freely within the Directorate of Fisheries’ seven regions [21]. 
To get the industry back on track after the crisis in 2002, it was 
already in 2003 decided to allocate 50 new licenses, in addition to the 
ten that were not sold in the previous round. The price was the same, 
NOK 5 million, but NOK 4 million in Finnmark. This time the re­
quirements were more detailed and specific. First, it was acknowledged 
that salmon farming required competence, and that such competence 
had to be required by the manager of the facility, but not necessarily by 
the owner. Second, the call should contribute to technological innova­
tion, to make fish farming more efficient. Third, the allocation would 
help to make local communities more financially sustainable, and 
finally, permits would only be awarded to municipalities that could 
present a coastal zone plan making space available for aquaculture. 
Women were specifically encouraged to apply, but they were not given 
any priority. The original plan was to reserve the permits in this round 
for small and medium-sized enterprises, but due to EU regulations, this 
had to be abandoned. Before the crisis in 2002, the license prices had 
been up to NOK 35 million, while in the period after they had fallen to 
NOK 6.5 million, or just a little more than what the state demanded for 
the new permits [66]. In practice, however, only the established 
companies could afford to buy new licenses. Due to the conflict with the 
EU and accusations of dumping and subsidies, the government 
constantly had to keep an eye on total production, which limited the 
number of licenses that could be issued. 
Despite lower license prices and higher permitted MAB, it was 
difficult to accelerate development in Finnmark. The ten permits that 
were not sold in the last round were finally sold through an open auction 
in 2009. The permits had to be kept in the allocated municipalities for 
ten years, and could be withdrawn if no concrete activity was imple­
mented within a year. The control of the other license conditions ap­
pears to have been as absent as for the permits granted in 2002 and 2003 
[21]. In the White Paper on a strategy for a competitive aquaculture 
industry from 2007 [40], the government had announced a new allo­
cation round in 2009. The number of permits was set at 61, after a 
thorough assessment of environmental and fish health conditions as well 
as the market situation, including developments in competitor coun­
tries. The licenses were to be issued along the entire coast, but the 
Ministry had prepared a plan in advance for where aquaculture was to 
be promoted. While previous allocations had emphasized where aqua­
culture was possible, there was now a focus on where aquaculture should 
not be further increased. Smaller fish farming companies were to be 
given priority, but the definition of "smaller" was relatively generous, as 
it included 1–19 permits, i.e. companies with a turnover of millions of 
NOK. The price for a permit was now adjusted up to NOK 8 million but 
NOK 3 million in Finnmark. 
5. Green licenses and the traffic light system 
In 2011–2012, a further expansion of aquaculture was not on the 
agenda. The Ministry of Fisheries and Coastal Affairs had received 
strong criticism from the Office of the National Auditor (2012), 
regarding the management of the Norwegian aquaculture industry, and 
particularly towards controlling sea lice. In 2012, a planned expansion 
of 5% of MAB across the country had to be canceled. Still, farmers had a 
strong desire to increase production, as prices were high with few 
problems on the market. The solution was the introduction of so-called 
“green licenses”, with which farmers were given the opportunity to 
expand production if they developed and adopted new production 
methods that could reduce the problems of sea lice and escapes. The 
scheme was eventually relatively complicated, with three different 
groups of licenses (A, B and C), altogether 45 licenses. The Ministry 
wanted to embed regional priorities, maintain a diverse farming struc­
ture in terms of company size, and use both auction and allocation by 
fixed price. The environmental criteria were also differentiated [18]. 
The scheme was positively received by farmers as well as environ­
mentalists [31]. The farmers finally had a chance to expand, while the 
environmental lobby was satisfied with the prospects of new technolo­
gies and new solutions to the problems of sea lice and escapes. However, 
the administrative costs connected to this experiment were consider­
able, several decisions were subject to appeals and some ended up in 
court. When the production finally got on stream, from 2016 onwards, 
there was considerable uncertainty regarding the conditions. Leading 
politicians in the government coalition as well as the opposition 
concluded; “Green licenses never again!” [18]. 
Time was ripe for a new system, which regulated growth based on 
more objective criteria. The new system, called the traffic light system, 
was based on the recommendations from the Area Committee [3], and 
recommended specific production areas where growth could be deter­
mined based on environmental indicators. The principles were further 
elaborated in a strategic report to the Norwegian Parliament in 2015 
[35]. Much of the development work was carried out by the Institute of 
Marine Research, which acted as the government’s most important 
advisor. Finally, it was decided to use only one environmental indicator, 
namely salmon lice [53]. The coast was divided into 13 production 
areas, where the number of lice affecting wild salmon and trout would 
be decisive for further growth. In green production areas, the chance of

of salmon and an optimistic view of the future. Capacity corresponding 
to a standard license (780 tons MAB) was sold at prices between NOK

number of smaller fish farmers were able to pay the price for extra ca­
pacity [38]. In the next round, in 2020, the map was slightly changed, 
two production areas turned red, two turned yellow while the remaining 
nine were green. One percent of the allocated growth was sold at a fixed 
price, while the remaining 5% was sold on auction, with prices ranging 
from NOK 122 to 200 million for a standard license.

farmers in production area 4 decided to sue the state for unfair treat­
ment. They questioned the legality of reducing MAB for all farmers in an 
area, claiming that this implied collective punishment [27]. Although 
the farmers lost in court, the protest was a clear sign that the system is 
not considered legitimate by all farmers. Also on the environmental side, 
there were skeptical reactions, which primarily criticized that the traffic 
light system is based on only one indicator (salmon lice), where envi­
ronmentalists wanted to see other indicators included, such as fish 
mortality, escapes, local pollution, use of medical substances, etc. 
6. Special purpose licenses 
Licenses for special purposes are all regulated by the Salmon Allo­
cation Regulations [11] and include salmon, trout and rainbow trout. 
The special purpose licenses apply to broodstock, education, research, 
display facilities and development. (In addition, special licenses are 
granted for fish parks and slaughter cages, which are assumed to be of 
little importance in this context). In contrast to the ordinary system, the 
number of special licenses is not number-regulated, and with some ex­
ceptions for licenses issued before 2011, they are limited in time. 
Licenses for broodstock, research and development are granted for up to 
15 years, while for display facilities the period is ten years. The Nor­
wegian Directorate of Fisheries grants the licenses, and both new ap­
plications and renewals are assessed by a separate scientific committee. 
Applications are assessed continuously, with the exception of develop­
ment licenses, where the application period was two years (2015–2017). 
The special licenses are granted free of charge. In practice, they will all 
involve financial obligations, both in the form of investments and 
operating expenses. Although most special purpose licenses are operated 
by commercial companies, they cannot be included in the companies’ 
biomass, and they are often subject to other restrictions, which makes 
the operation less profitable than for ordinary food fish licenses. Table 1 
gives an overview of the number of special licenses and associated 
biomass, measured in the number of tons of MAB. 
As can be seen, the special licenses make up 21% of the total number 
of licenses, and 17% of the total MAB capacity. Unlike the ordinary 
licenses, special purpose licenses are not limited in number. As the 
Directorate of Fisheries processes the applications continuously, there is 
no limit to the maximum number of licenses within each special purpose 
category, and there will also be variations in the amount of biomass 
allocated to each license. The number of licenses, duration and biomass 
for these will vary over time, as some will be extended, some will expire, 
and new licenses will be granted. Special licenses are usually granted 
according to a two-stage model, where a license is granted to produce 
salmon, but owners must also obtain a license for the site of operation. 
For certain special licenses the Directorate may require that the license is 
located at a specific site. 
Most licenses for special purposes are operated by or in collaboration 
with fish farming companies that also produce salmon with ordinary 
licenses. Only educational institutions (upper secondary school, college/ 
university, etc.) can obtain an education license, and some of the 
research licenses are owned by research institutes. In most cases, these 
licenses are leased to companies that produce salmon and trout, while 
educational and research institutions are responsible for education/ 
research programs. Salmon belonging to special licenses such as edu­
cation, research and display are often co-located in places with pro­
duction from several ordinary licenses and are subject to the same 
inspections (lice counting, internal control routines, etc.). However, 
each special license is granted with some unique criteria, which are 
based on what is described in the application. 
7. Development licenses 
The lesson from the green licenses introduced in 2013 was that the 
experiment had only to a limited extent succeeded in creating new 
technological solutions that could at the same time reduce the envi­
ronmental impact and solve the area challenge [18]. The most recent 
category of special licenses is development licenses, which were intro­
duced in November 2015. This was a temporary scheme, where licenses 
could be granted to projects that involved significant innovation and 
significant investments. When the application period expired in November 
2017, the Directorate of Fisheries had received 104 applications 
requesting 892 standard licenses (780 tons MAB). To put this figure in 
perspective, it corresponds to 85% of the total production capacity at 
that time. By the end of 2020, 20 companies had received development 
licenses for a total capacity of 80,000 tons of MAB, while two applicants 
are awaiting a final answer. In addition, the majority of applicants who 
had been rejected have appealed the decision to the Ministry of Trade, 
Industry and Fisheries. 
The development licenses were said to contribute to the development 
of technology that can benefit the entire industry, and further strengthen 
restructuring and innovation in the aquaculture industry. The estab­
lishment of these licenses came as a follow-up of a White Paper [35], 
where the government stated that it would follow a strategy to facilitate 
technology development and further development of new operating 
methods. Incidentally, this was something the industry itself had 
requested for a long time. 
After a round of consultations that invited stakeholders to express 
their opinion on how a new licensing scheme should be organized and 
regulated, a separate license for development projects was established in 
2015. Like research licenses, development licenses can be granted for a 
duration of up to 15 years, without restrictions on the maximum number

A review of the guidelines for assessing applications and associated 
regulations shows that they imply a considerable degree of adminis­

uncertainty. The process of evaluating all applications took far more 
time than expected, and indicates that the authorities were not prepared 
for the scope and complexity of the assessment of these applications. 
Two factors in particular are central to the assessment process; that the 
projects will involve significant innovation and significant investments. 
The technological solutions must also differ from solutions that have 
already been granted a development license. If two similar solutions 
meet the requirements but are considered to be equal, the first appli­
cation will be granted, while the second will be rejected. 
Development licenses are meant to be a risk-reducing scheme for the 
large projects that the industry itself cannot or will not take the risk of 
carrying out on its own, and it is therefore required that significant in­
vestments are made. First and foremost, this means assessing the actual 
size of the investment, but the Directorate of Fisheries can also assess the 
applicant’s ability to make such investments. Since the development 
licenses are convertible, it is the value of granting a commercial license 
that is the authorities’ contribution to the development projects, i.e. risk 
reduction in the form of licenses that can be at least as profitable as the 
commercial ones are today. 
When assessing significant investments, the investment amount is set 
against the need for biomass and the value of the licenses. The value of a 
license is based on an estimated value of a commercial license, and until 
June 2018, the Directorate of Fisheries estimated this at approximately 
NOK 50 million. After June 2018, this changed to approximately NOK 
150 million (due to the results of the auction held in the spring of 2018). 
Therefore, the rules of the game changed during the application process. 
Most applications that were processed after June 2018 thus received a 
different assessment of the degree of investment (and thus the number of 
possible licenses) compared with those that were assessed before this 
time. This "game changer" took place after the application deadline had 
expired and created a possible bias among the applicants. The applica­
tions submitted before June 2018 received a potential advantage (the 
possibility of several licenses for the same investment size) compared to 
the applications granted after June 2018. Very few of the companies that 
were granted licenses received the number they had applied for. The 
Directorate of Fisheries has reduced the biomass (and thus the number of 
licenses) for several projects, often with the argument that the tech­
nology can be tested sufficiently with less biomass. Of 104 applications, 
82 were rejected, and 70 of these applicants have appealed the rejection. 
Until now, few of the appeals have been overturned by the Ministry of 
Trade, Industry and Fisheries. 
8. Land-based, post-smolt and offshore licenses 
In addition to the seven different license schemes we have dealt with 
so far, there are three more: licenses for land-based operations, for 
production of post-smolt and for offshore salmon farming, which is still 
in the making. 
Even if land-based production has been shown to be far more costly 
than the present sea-based cage culture, there are several new projects in 
the process of being realized [68,48]. In Norway there is as per the end 
of 2020 only one small facility put in operation, in Fredrikstad, south of 
Oslo. However, several large-scale projects are being planned, and the 
first ones have already got their technical approval and are now under 
construction. They will probably start actual production from 2022 on, 
provided that they are able to secure the required financial resources. 
The great incentive for land-based production is that the government has 
decided that the license is granted for free, and that there is no limit to 
the number of licenses that can be granted. However, land-based farms 
are still liable to all the technical requirements connected to land-based 
constructions, and not least, to strong restrictions on emissions into the 
sea. Production volume is granted in terms of tons MAB, with no explicit 
maximum limit. According to ilaks [28] there are currently 31 
land-based projects being planned in Norway, either based on 
RAS-technology or flow-through systems. Knowing that new production 
capacity at sea will require a cost of NOK 150–200 million (= USD 15–20 
million) for a standard license (780 tons MAB), it is easy to understand 
the economic logic behind the new projects, although massive in­
vestments are needed to provide some economy of scale. However, the 
government considers land-based farms as an opportunity for export of 
Norwegian equipment and expertize, and in 2016 the aquaculture reg­
ulations were changed to accommodate the new production alternative. 
The second option is production of large postsmolt. While the current 
production model is based on smolt weighing 80–120 g, an increasing 
number of smolt producers have started to produce large smolt on land 
(200 gr up to 1 kg), thus reducing the time spent at sea, and hence 
reducing the salmon lice problems, while at the same time using their 
(sea-based) MAB-capacity more efficiently. Production of large smolt on 
land is largely based on RAS-technology and requires both more space 
and higher capital investment. Still, with the current costs involved in 
reducing sea lice, it has proved financially worthwhile, and a large 
number of post-smolt farms are now under construction. As explained in 
Section 4, the license limitation on smolt production was lifted already 
in 1985, but licenses were still granted for a certain number of smolt per 
year. This was changed in 2016, when the production of postsmolt was 
regulated in the same manner as land-based production, i.e. with no 
license fee, no restriction in terms of weight (formerly 200 g), and with 
production volume given in tons of MAB, largely based on the applicants 
physical capacity and limits set by the regulation of emissions [42]. 
Finally we have the option of offshore salmon farming. Due to new 
technological solutions developed under the development license 
scheme, such technologies are already available. However, two of the 
most famous projects, Salmar’s production rig and Nordlaks’ production 
vessel, are still located in coastal waters, well inside the base lines. 
Nevertheless, the first application has already been received, and the 
government has taken the signals and prepared a draft for how the 
offshore areas (outside the base lines + one nautical mile) can be 
managed [41]. The solution seems to be somewhat similar to what has 
been practiced in the petroleum sector, with area blocks being auctioned 
in the most promising areas, where the state is exclusively responsible 
for allocation and management. However, much remains to be done 
before this scheme can be operational, not least in terms of coordinating 
other offshore interests, such as fisheries, petroleum activities and the 
new offshore wind sector. In the following analysis we concentrate on 
the seven existing systems at sea. 
9. A complicated administrative world 
As pointed out in the introduction, the Norwegian management 
system is based on the fact that one must have a license to farm salmon, 
trout and sea trout. This has been the case since 1973, when the tem­
porary Aquaculture Act was passed. Furthermore, there are at least four 
agencies that in reality have a “veto right” regarding new establish­
ments. The veto right refers to the fact that these state agencies may 
deny access to a locality, if found contravening objectives in the sector 
laws. The system, as shown in Fig. 1, is therefore very complicated and 
cumbersome, both from the perspective of the industry and the 
administration.

companies’ balance sheet, and the value of the production license also

preferred by all industry actors, resisting any idea of doing away with 
the production license. Secondly, the system with two licenses provides 
an opportunity to concentrate the business on the best locations. Today, 
fish farming companies will be able to give up their least favorable lo­
cations, usually in exchange for increased MAB on the best ones. In a 
system with only site licenses, there would probably be few companies 
that were willing to abandon even bad sites. Furthermore, there is a 
back-up argument that Norwegian salmon producers may once again 
face trade restrictions and threats of boycott or punitive tariffs as 
recently experienced with China and Russia [13]. In such situations, it 
would be beneficial for the authorities to still have an opportunity to 
limit production through a production license, which in principle is 
flexible. It has also proved useful when the need has been to adjust the 
MAB regulation as compensation for closed markets (Russia) or high 
mortality as a result of algae invasion (in Nordland and Troms in 2019). 
Furthermore, there are good fiscal reasons for maintaining the license 
system, as the state and the municipalities can achieve large revenues by 
selling new capacity. This has played an increasingly important role, 
especially after 2013, when the sale of green licenses revealed the actual 
willingness to pay for new licenses and increased capacity. Finally, and 
probably most important, the license system is solidly entrenched, both 
politically and administratively and hence, very difficult to change 
without large political and economic costs. 
What about the special license schemes? They have all been created 
after close cooperation between industry and administration, in order to 
serve common interests. Thanks to enthusiasts at the then Norwegian 
Agricultural College (now NMBU), brood stock production began as 
early as the late 1960s, but production was not regulated with special 
licenses until 1986. The purpose of giving more companies access to 
brood stock licenses was to strengthen the industry with the best 
breeding material and reduce the risk by having more producers 
geographically dispersed. Over time, the development has gone in the 
direction of two large producers, two medium-sized and some smaller, 
primarily associated with trout production. Both of the two large pro­
ducers are today affiliated with large foreign-owned genetics companies, 
which are increasingly using patenting of the breeding material. What is 
the salmon industry’s common interests and what are primarily the 
companies’ own financial interests are today difficult to distinguish. 
Thus, the special purpose licenses are also disputed, especially because 
the criteria have been unclear and the control weak. Violations of the 
original conditions have never been sanctioned [23]. 
The same applies to education licenses and research licenses. Both 
represent a back-door to increased production, which is considerably 
cheaper than buying new capacity via the ordinary system. The educa­
tion licenses have proved to be very lucrative, for both the educational 
institutions and for the companies that run the licenses on their behalf. 
However, both types of licenses are time-limited and also subject to a 
number of other restrictions. The education licenses are few and 
represent a small problem, as long as the criteria are clear (must lead to a 
trade certificate or other qualifying certificates). The research licenses 
are considerably more controversial, especially because the research 
institutions (universities and colleges) only control a small part of the 
licenses. Research on a large, industrial scale is undoubtedly useful for 
the entire industry, but there is a great lack of transparency about the 
results and control has until recently been weak [9]. 
Display licenses also represent a back-door to larger production and 
have thus been of great interest to many fish farming companies, despite

to be over 17% of total MAB capacity and in many cases a detour ( not a

the most challenging aspect of the special purpose licenses is how they 
relate to the main system (the traffic light system). As shown by Hersoug 
et al. [24], several of the special purpose licenses are not part of the 
traffic light system. There is no ceiling on the number of licenses, and 
they are not bound by the status of the production area. For example, a 
new research license may well be placed in a red production area. The 
special licenses will also not be subject to a reduction in MAB should the 
environmental condition turn to red in the production area. This chal­
lenges the logic of the entire traffic light system, which is based on an 
action rule which in turn is based on an objective indicator (number of 
salmon lice per fish). This weakens the legitimacy of both the traffic light 
system and the system of special purpose licenses. 
Precisely how the development licenses will affect the traffic light 
system is too early to say. Until now, only one company has converted its 
development licenses to ordinary licenses. Several of the projects will 
take several years to realize and even longer before they can possibly be 
converted into ordinary licenses. It is nevertheless a paradox that a 
scheme that was implemented to bring innovation and better environ­
mental conditions (reduce escapes and salmon lice) does not oblige 
farmers to operate with the technology that was developed after a 
possible conversion. If a company finds that they are better served by 
returning to ordinary, open cage operations, it is up to the companies 
themselves to decide. Consequently, the environmental impacts on the 
traffic light system are highly uncertain. 
A final point is that so many schemes, with very different operational 
conditions, create problems for the administrative apparatus, both in 
ministries and directorates. One aspect is the laborious process of 
evaluating new applications and then processing a large number of ap­
peals. More problematic is the control and follow-up itself. The special 
schemes have, except for the latest (development licenses), largely "gone 
under the radar", i.e. control and follow-up have until 2015 been weak 
or absent [24]. This has meant that the operators have been relatively 
free with regard to implementation, knowing that any breach of the li­
cense conditions will in any case not be strictly sanctioned. 
10. An objective system? On the use of political and 
administrative discretion 
Much of the criticism of the licensing system before 2017 was based 
on three factors: First, that the granting of licenses involved a large 
degree of administrative and political discretion. Second, further growth 
was highly irregular, based on random allocation rounds. Shortly sum­
marized, the system was unpredictable, a fact that was strongly 
emphasized in the hearing document leading up to the important 
parliament paper introducing the new traffic light system [35]. Third, 
the lucky ones (who received a license) were seldom or never checked 
afterwards, with regard to what they had promised in the applications 
[21]. The traffic light system was intended to remedy these shortcom­
ings. Based on an action rule and so-called objective indicators (so far 
only salmon lice), the fish farmers could know well in advance where 
there would be growth, where there would be a standstill and where 
reduction (of MAB) was necessary. Thus, the degree of political and 
administrative discretion which had characterized license allocations 
should be reduced. With political discretion means the room for 
constantly changing the criteria for allocation of licenses, as described in 
Section 4. With administrative discretion means the flexible exercising of 
judgment and decision making allowed to public administrators. 
In practice, it was not so easy to reduce the use of political and 
administrative discretion. (We disregard here that the actual counting of 
salmon lice, until it is mechanized, in itself implies considerable 
discretion on behalf of the counting teams [58]). More importantly, the 
limits set for the status of the production areas (traffic lights) were 
clearly discretionary. Salmon fishermen and nature conservation in­
terests believe that the limits are set too generously, so that an accep­
tance of high mortality is given up to 30% (in yellow areas). The point 
here is that the limits could just as easily have been set at, for example, 
5% and 15%, which would have made it considerably more difficult to 
achieve green (and yellow) status and thus reduced growth in the 
aquaculture industry. However, discretion does not end here. The rec­
ommendations from IMR on how the zones should be colored (and thus 
decisive for growth or reduction), are also subject to political review. 
This is given authority, or as it is described in the White Paper: "In an 
overall assessment, it may also be natural to look at the socio-economic 
consequences of different choices" [35]. In the first round, the Minister 
of Fisheries changed the color of a production area (from yellow to 
green) precisely on the basis of such an assessment. Hence, there is still 
considerable room for both administrative and political discretion in the 
"objective" traffic light system, although it should be stressed that the 
two concepts (“objective” and “discretionary”) mark a continuum. The 
political goal of introducing a new system, strongly argued by the in­
dustry, was to reduce the degree of discretion and base further growth on 
more objective criteria. 
We find an even greater degree of discretion in the county governor’s 
environmental department and their determination of the site’s carrying 
capacity. The load capacity is determined in the number of tons of MAB, 
but this obscures an extensive and complicated process where a number 
of factors are assessed and finally a figure is given. The sustainability of a 
fish farm at sea will depend, among other things, on the natural condi­
tion of the water body (natural treatment capacity), organic load 
(various inputs including other anthropogenic sources such as sewage, 
industry, etc.), chemical conditions in the sediment, use of chemical 
agents, benthic composition, current conditions, water exchange cycles 
(threshold fjord) and finally the aquaculture biomass and production at 
the actual site [49]. There is no direct correlation between the results of 
a bottom survey (MOM B or C survey) and MAB. So you cannot calculate 
from environmental condition to MAB. The effects of the environmental 
impact of a fish farm with a given MAB may differ from one locality to 
another [32]. This means that there is a strong element of professional 
discretion in how to determine the individual site’s carrying capacity, 
which in turn has significant consequences for how effectively a fish 
farmer can utilize the site. If you have five sites allowing 1000 tons of 
MAB each, or one site allowing 5000 tons of MAB it makes considerable 
difference in terms of logistics and hence in the economic results of 
farming. 
A third example concerns the allocation of scarce areas. Often there 
will be situations where two or more companies are interested in the 
same site (or in sites close to each other). Who will then have access to 
the new site? In the current system, it is the county’s aquaculture 
manager deciding who should be prioritized, based on an assessment of 
who has “the greatest need”. It goes without saying that there is 
considerable room for discretion here, which has been documented in 
several specific disputes [12]. Several municipalities, which have local 
companies, often want to prioritize these, based on the idea that locally 
owned companies have the greatest local multiplier effects. Currently, 
they do not have the opportunity to do this, and any locally prioritized 
company may in the next round be downgraded by the county

A final example concerns the development licenses. Here it was

11. "The tragedy of the public administration" 
For many years, salmon farmers dreamed that it would be possible to 
return to the so-called "Trøndelag model", where ordinary location cases 
could be processed efficiently and in a short time [20]. No such "quick 
fix" exists today. The battle for space in the coastal zone is intense, and 
more stakeholders are signing up with demands for space and influence 
on how the coastal zone is to be managed. Several interests have been 
given veto opportunities, and aquaculture has no special position, other 
than that the central government has contented itself with expressing 
that "The counties and municipalities set aside sufficient area for the desired 
growth in the aquaculture industry through updated plans, which also take 
into account environmental considerations and other societal interests” [43]. 
As can be seen from Tveterås et al. [63], there is little reason to 
expect major changes in the basic structure of the management of the 
aquaculture sector. The system of licenses is fixed, the traffic light sys­
tem can obviously be improved, but the system of growth based on an 
action rule is fixed. Whether imposed reductions stand up in the judicial 
system remains to be seen. The system of special licenses will un­
doubtedly be continued, but it will be a challenge how these will be 
integrated into the traffic light system. Closer control and greater 
transparency about the finances of these schemes are also required [24]. 
Yet, this is not where the main challenge lies. 
Today, the licensing system is based on a dual process, where you 
must have both a production license and a site license. Growth in pro­
duction is regulated via an action rule in the traffic light system, while 
allocation of sites takes place in accordance with the Planning and 
Building Act, where the municipalities in principle decide which areas 
where aquaculture can be practiced. In principle, this is a transparent 
system, which represents a number of different compromises; between 
growth and protection, between different levels of government (state, 
county and municipality), where the system must also take care of 
different considerations, such as food safety, animal welfare, environ­
mental considerations and a varied ownership structure. The problems 
arise as many of these considerations are enshrined in legislation and 
regulations, where several bodies are involved, often with overlapping 
areas of responsibility and control. No public agency is willing to 
relinquish power and authority, despite the fact that most administra­
tors realize that the regulations have become too extensive, too detailed, 
too time-consuming and difficult to control. 
According to the journal Norsk Fiskerinæring [47], the aquaculture 
industry is subject to 344 paragraphs in different laws and regulations 
that directly interfere with establishment and operation of salmon 
farms. Fig. 1 gives an idea of the complexity, even though this is a static 
picture of who is involved, and not which processes take place. Olsen 
[52] gives a good picture of the process: "The administration can grow 
like a coral reef, where new principles are laid on top of each other 
without any overall plan for institutional design". A telling example is 
the consultation statement on new regulations for discharges from 
aquaculture in the sea: "In addition, the ministries have stated that the 
authority and division of labor that the Directorate of Fisheries and the county 
governor currently have, should be changed as little as possible" [50]. It 
seems that most governmental authorities want to keep the current 
structure, i.e. they want to retain their power and authority over the 
subject areas that they have today. Hareide [15] has characterized this 
as "the tragedy of the public administration". In contrast to “the tragedy 
of the commons”, the result is not ruin and destruction of common re­
sources, but a management system that is confusing, cumbersome and 
characterized by many internal conflicts. Proposals for cooperation and 
better coordination have already been put forward for more than ten 
years, so far with modest results [54]. Which steps can be taken is not 
obvious, although everyone seems to agree on the need for simplification. 
12. Reform or revolution? 
In the aquaculture industry, it is constantly advertised that there is "a 
sea of opportunities". That is hardly the case with regard to the man­
agement structure. Strong interests are attached to the structure that has 
now been established, even though the traffic light system still lacks 
general legitimacy. Here we will look at some of the proposals that have 
been raised through the project "Aquaculture Management 2030", 
financed by the industry organization FHF. 
A separate aquaculture directorate has been proposed ([20,54]), but 
such a solution also has several unresolved problems, including that the 
directorate should promote the aquaculture industry, propose regula­
tions and check that the regulations are complied with. It was such 
conflicts of interest that led to the Petroleum Safety Authority being 
separated from the Norwegian Petroleum Directorate. But this model is 
not directly transferable to aquaculture, at least not to aquaculture in the 
coastal zone, where several ministries and directorates have their own 
areas of responsibility. In addition, the municipalities have a very cen­
tral place, guaranteed through the Planning and Building Act. Never­
theless, it is worth looking at the possibility of dividing the responsibility 
for aquaculture into two: one part whose task is to promote the devel­
opment of aquaculture in the broadest sense, while another part is 
responsible for control and follow-up. Another proposal has been to let 
one ministry have the final responsibility for establishment or not, by 
abolishing the right of veto that de facto exists today. This would mean 
that all factors eventually were weighed, both positive and negative, 
economic as well as environmental, where one ministry decided difficult 
cases, where there are objections from different directorates [36]. The 
question would then be; which ministry? While the aquaculture industry 
would point to the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Fisheries, others 
would like to emphasize the Ministry of Local Government and 
Modernization, which is currently responsible for the Planning and 
Building Act. The environmental interests would definitely point to the 
Ministry of Climate and Environment. Nor do the salmon farmers’ own 
organizations have any patent solution to the problem. The interest 
organizations involved are strikingly modest regarding concrete pro­
posals for reorganization. 
A third proposal concerns a new round of development licenses, but 
this time aimed at closed or semi-closed cage technology, given that the 
biggest problems with current technology are related to lice, escapes and 
discharges. Such an arrangement could be set up as a two-step process, 
where the responsibility is mainly left to the actors themselves, i.e. the 
fish farmers. The first step would then be to have the technology certi­
fied as closed (or semi-closed). Given that lice are currently the only

Several other proposals have been up for debate in recent years, 
including time limitation (of the licenses), use of specific environmental 
requirements from the municipalities, other administrative area solu­
tions, and increased cooperation between farmers in the same area [39, 
67]. The first three won little support in the Aquaculture Administration 
2030 project. Time constraints will contribute to increased uncertainty 
and provide incentives for short-term behavior. In addition, the Nor­
wegian Food Safety Authority and the fisheries administration already 
have opportunities today to reduce the time for given localities or 
licenses, if environmental considerations indicate reduced activity or 
full stop. More specific environmental requirements have been proposed 
by several municipalities, including priority for given technologies. 
Although it is easy to sympathize with the municipalities’ care for their 
own environment, such regulations will also be able to open up for "a 
race to the bottom", i.e. those who are most dependent on new activities 
will make the weakest environmental regulations. It has also been sug­
gested that new administrative units could be given responsibility for 
environmental regulation at area level, including water regions, pro­
duction area boards, counties or state “industrial parks”. None of the 
proposals have received special support, as all of them lack regulatory 
instruments. Instead, the research has pointed to increased 
inter-municipal cooperation, which in several counties has produced 
good coastal zone plans, for larger areas (ecosystem-based) with effec­
tive use of scarce expertise. Finally, the need for increased cooperation 
has been pointed out. As demonstrated by Tveterås et al. [63], such a 
collaboration on localization could yield large gains, provided that 
mechanisms are found where the winners can compensate the losers. 
The fish farmers themselves prioritize voluntary cooperation, knowing 
that if this does not succeed, there will be mandatory cooperation, such 
as joint de-licensing, joint fallowing, synchronized release of smolt, etc. 
Finally, the project “Aquaculture Administration 2030” recommended 
that social sustainability be emphasized more than today, which means 
first and foremost that the aquaculture industry must strengthen its 
legitimacy, both locally and on the national level [55]. 
What are the more generic lessons learned from the Norwegian ex­
periences so far? While the early success of Norwegian salmon farming 
was largely attributed to the farmers’ willingness to cooperate and 
active state support for research, education and a protected sales system, 
recent developments have changed the system significantly. Salmon 
farming has become industrialized and largely science-based, with a 
strong concentration of farming companies as a result. Management has 
changed correspondingly, although the basic licensing system has 
remained almost unchanged since 1973. According to aquaculture 
economists, continuous productivity growth has been the most impor­
tant driving force behind the growth in Norwegian salmon farming [4, 
62]. The challenge, per 2021, when productivity growth has slowed 
down [29], is the difficult balancing act between how to safeguard 
sustainable development, while at the same time encouraging innova­
tion. From a sustainability perspective, it is not guaranteed that the 
Norwegian system is the best answer. No other salmon-producing 
country has copied the Norwegian traffic light system to regulate 
growth, even though they all use licensing schemes and environmental 
considerations to regulate the aquaculture industry. Nor have they 
copied the system of special purpose licenses, leaving these functions 
largely to the private operators, i.e. to the market [22,45,65]. 
From an administrative perspective, the question of legitimacy is 
absolutely central. Ultimately, the success of any management regime 
rests on legitimacy, i.e. the system is respected by most, if not by all, 
stakeholders. Here it should be underlined that “stakeholders” in this 
context refer to all users of the sea area, not only the salmon farmers 
[20]. The present licensing system seems to be stable, despite many 
weaknesses (limited opening for new players, high regulatory rent, high 
administration costs, etc.). The license system has worked for almost 50 
years, under changing political regimes, it has scientific support, and it is 
solidly administratively rooted. The establishment of an aquaculture 
register, where the use of MAB as "common currency" has facilitated all 
types of transactions [19]. The traffic light system, on the other hand, is 
much more controversial both scientifically and politically. Much work 
remains before the system has general acceptance. Regarding the special 
purpose licenses, the lesson is that the authorities must take into account 
not only the good collective goals, but also the "perverse incentives" (the 
back door to increased production), as well as the effects on the ordinary 
management system. If the ultimate goal is sustainable growth, largely 
based on environmental considerations, there is limited room for special 
arrangements that follow other rules. Therefore, the scope of special 
licenses must be limited, the purposes carefully delimited and the licenses 
carefully monitored and controlled. 
Finally it may be asked; why all these complaints (from industry 
actors, politicians, administrators and the general public)? Norway is 
still the leading salmon producer in the world, and the industry has 
proved to be extremely profitable. The answer is complex, but somewhat 
simplified: The markets for salmon are expanding, the farmers would 
like to expand, a position shared by governments of different political 
shades, while the industry has largely stagnated, producing in 2020 
more or less on the same level as in 2012. Compared to the official 
ambitions, presented by various governments [34], Norway is an 
underperformer. The main reason is lack of environmental sustainabil­
ity, which in turn has made the industry less socially acceptable. The 
so-called “social license to produce” has been questioned, not only in the 
general population but also in the affected coastal areas [60]. Whether 
new production methods (offshore, land-based, post-smolt) are going to 
meet these concerns remains to be seen. The most pressing issue is to 
reduce the environmental effects of the still dominating production 
regime; open net pen farming. This is largely a question of new and 
improved technology, but also a regulatory challenge. Hence, licensing 
is central – in essence the questions are the same as in 1973; who should 
be allowed to do what, when, where and how.
