### Background
Osmundsen and Olsen evaluate how Norwegian counties have administered the Aquaculture Act after the 2010 management reform. Troms fylkeskommune commissioned NTNU Samfunnsforskning. The work ran from September 2024 to 1 March 2025.

From 1 January 2010 counties took over allocation of aquaculture permits from the Directorate of Fisheries. They also received objection authority for aquaculture in municipal area plans. For locality clearance the county coordinates the whole process and makes the final decision. Deadlines were introduced at the same time. The reform aimed to strengthen the regional elected level, improve coordination, strengthen industry development, and support more comprehensive coastal-zone planning. Reactions at the time were mixed. Critics questioned county competence and processing quality. Rogaland was the only county that was not positive about the transfer. The Directorate estimated 11 full-time equivalents. Counties staffed more than that, and over half of the new staff came from the Directorate.

### Methods
The authors combine document studies (public papers and internal FAKS material), FAKS decision-base statistics for 2020-2023, and 36 interviews with 38 informants from October 2024 to January 2025. Informants came from the seven counties with a continuous aquaculture caseload (Finnmark, Troms, Nordland, Trøndelag, Møre og Romsdal, Vestland, Rogaland), municipalities, sector authorities, and industry. Interviews were semi-structured and anonymised. Two were pairs. The rest were individual. The authors observed a FAKS gathering and a coordination meeting but do not quote those meetings.

Processing-time analysis is limited to 2020-2023 because earlier decision-base data are incomplete. The authors include new-locality and change-of-existing-locality cases that are classified as routine or non-routine. Agder sits in some national totals only. 2024 figures were left out because they were not yet reported. There is still no single official definition of a routine case.

### Main findings
The report states that counties were well prepared in 2010 and have largely met the delivery goals. Partners describe counties as accessible and as a coordinating driver, even though counties cannot sanction other bodies that overrun their deadlines. Adding the county is said not to have made applications more cumbersome. FAKS, informal from 2010 and formalised in 2016, is presented as the main vehicle for shared practice, knowledge, reporting, and more equal treatment. From 2016 all counties have delegated decision authority to the administration. Table 1 shows 565 Nordland and 280 Troms political treatments in 2010-2016, then a sharp drop. Only two decisions were changed in political treatment in 2010-2018.

The deadline regulation sets 22 weeks as the total limit. For routine cases, mean processing time was 21 and 20 weeks in 2020-2021, then rose to 24 weeks in 2022 and 31 weeks in 2023. About 80 percent of applications were routine. Many routine cases are changes on existing sites. New-site means were 56, 42, 50 and 79 weeks. Change-of-site means were 22, 21, 24 and 27 weeks. All-case means were 30, 26, 30 and 38 weeks. Medians stay at 20.7 to 23.7 weeks, so a few cases of 70 to more than 200 weeks pull the means. More than 85 percent of applications were granted in 2021-2023. The coded set for the seven main counties is 1 141 change-or-new-site cases.

Counties themselves largely kept the four-week final-decision deadline. Møre og Romsdal used 7.7 and 10.4 weeks in 2022-2023. Statsforvalteren used about 14-18 weeks in 2020-2022 and almost 24 weeks in 2023. Mattilsynet used 12.7 to 16.5 weeks. Municipal treatment sat a few weeks over the 12-week limit. Causes include extra documentation, applicants pausing cases, appeals, several applications on the same area, and resource shortages in other authorities. Informants say almost all decisions are now appealed. The reversal rate on county decisions is described as very low. Cod decisions rose from 7 in 2020 to 30 in 2023.

Some sector-authority informants question whether industry interests and environmental concerns are balanced. Others tie that question to an unclear border between what belongs in the area plan and what belongs in locality clearance. Industry wants the county to push harder for aquaculture area in municipal plans. Municipalities describe the county as a useful partner, including in inter-municipal coastal plans. Some informants want a share of Havbruksfondet earmarked for coastal knowledge. Teaching permits and their school income are said to be at risk if NOU 2023:23 is followed.

Informants agree that locality structure needs improvement. They do not agree on the remedy. Stronger state steering and stronger regional or local coordination are both discussed. Industry informants say old gentleman's agreements on where to apply no longer hold, and that firms apply to keep others out. Counties cannot see the Directorate's historical capacity-utilisation data. The authors find that the county lacks tools to change existing site structures. Very few informants trust the industry to restructure sites alone.

### Uncertainty and limitations
The 2010 transfer is 15 years old. Few of the original people were available, and recall is shaped by later events. The interview sample cannot represent more than 100 municipalities and nearly all aquaculture firms. The authors therefore do not score each county. Processing times cannot be compared year by year from 2010, or with the period before the deadline regulation. Internal FAKS papers say 2010-2015 means were in line with 22 weeks. Whether more comprehensive coastal-zone planning was achieved is hard to measure because several actors share that outcome. The study did not count objections or evaluate internal planning-versus-aquaculture cooperation. Means are pulled by a few very long cases. Table 3 raw-file counts can differ from the decision base.

### Relevance to this corpus
This is the administrative account of who decides locality licences after 2010. It separates county coordination and final decision from municipal area designation and from sector authorities that hold their own laws. Chapter 6 speaks to planning and municipal incentives (A1, A6). Missing capacity data and the plan-versus-licence mapping dispute speak to A4.
