### Background
Qviller and colleagues simulate whether moving farmed salmonid biomass onto fewer, larger, more distant farms reduces salmon lice and treatments. The setting is Norwegian production area PA3 (Bjørnafjorden and Hardangerfjorden), not a national planning system. Host density is treated as a driver of parasite load. Open net-pens exchange lice larvae. The paper is a lice-network study that touches area only through farm structure.

PA3 is stated as 3646 km2 with 147 active farms in 2014-2017. Average standing stock per farm in PA3 was 544 000 fish. National background figures in the introduction include a monthly average standing stock of 713 000 fish per farm in 2014-2017 and a farmed-to-wild host ratio cited from Dempster et al. (2021).

### Methods
They run the Aldrin et al. (2019) stochastic lice model on historic fish numbers and locations, weekly from 2014 to 2017, after a 16-week spin-up. The historical downward-trend parameter from that fit was set to zero. Treatments start when simulated counts exceed 0.5 adult females per fish, or 0.2 in weeks 16-21, and cut adult-female and other-motile lice by 75 percent. Biomass from closed farms is moved to remaining farms with matching cohorts, so total host biomass does not change.

Nine scenarios: unchanged structure, random removal of 20, 30, 40, or 50 percent of farms (10 random draws each), and strategic removal of the same shares using a hydrodynamic connectivity ranking from Huserbråten and Johnsen. Random scenarios use 10 000 simulations each. Strategic and baseline use 1000 each. The paper states 45 000 simulations in total.

### Main findings
All redistribution scenarios reduce simulated lice and treatments relative to baseline. Adult-female lice fall by 4-15 percent, other motile lice by 6-23 percent, and annual fish treatments by 6-25 percent. Closing more farms gives larger reductions. Strategic removal beats random removal. The strongest stated case is strategic removal of 50 percent of farms: median cuts of 15 percent adult females, 23 percent other motiles, and 25 percent treatments. Random-removal treatments fell by 6-17 percent. Strategic-removal treatments fell by 14-25 percent.

The authors interpret this as network fragmentation: larger distances cut between-farm dispersal. Adult-female reductions look smaller because treatment is triggered by adult-female counts. They say location of the closed farms matters, and that the qualitative direction holds even though the lice simulator is distance-based while the targeting list is hydrodynamic.

### Uncertainty and limitations
The authors say this is a modelling exercise and not a procedure to optimise farm structure in PA3. Remaining sites are not checked for carrying capacity. Some farms become unrealistically large (over 5 million fish, maximum 18.2 million), outside the data used to fit the model. Treatments occur the next week, which they call unrealistic when wellboats are scarce. Treatment efficacy is set at 75 percent, above the 44-47 percent in Aldrin et al. (2019). Background infestation from wild sources remains. Extrapolation outside the fitted farm-size range adds unknown uncertainty.

### Relevance to this corpus
Adjacent. The paper is about lice and farm-network structure in one production area. It is not a municipal coastal-plan study, not a locality-licence evaluation, and not a claim about how counties or municipalities should designate sea area.

### Relation to other documents
