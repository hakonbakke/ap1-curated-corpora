### Background
Osmundsen, Karlsen, Robertsen and Hersoug study self-regulation among groups of Norwegian salmon farmers as a way to handle shared-water problems. Open net-pen farms share the same water, so disease, lice and other externalities cannot be handled by one firm alone. The paper asks how far private collaborations go, why farmers join them, and what fails in daily operations and in crisis.

### Methods
An initial mapping used email and phone questions to 22 respondents (16 industry, 6 authorities). An in-depth national survey then reached 37 farmers (about 35 to 40 percent of salmon and trout farmers in 2019, total about 100), first as a web survey and then by phone. Two Food Safety Authority staff were interviewed in autumn 2018. Seventeen interviews cover the 2019 toxic algae bloom in Nordland and Troms. The in-depth survey and algae findings are stated as earlier summarised in Karlsen et al. (2019). Figures in this extract are images and were not read as data.

### Main findings
All 37 in-depth respondents cooperate with other farmers on operations and emergency preparedness. The main motive is reducing disease and parasite risk, then environmental impact, then profitability. Coordinated fallowing and sea transfer are rated more important for production than coordinated delousing, which the authors link to greater use of cleaner fish. Thirteen of 37 agree that authorities should establish cooperation. Twenty-one say cooperation should be voluntary. Twenty-three of 37 report good cooperation in their area. Seven say cooperation includes only the small companies, three only the large companies. Two report no good cooperation. Two say they are alone in their area.

Conflicts are reported more often where many farms share an area and distances are short. Newcomers into an established structure are named as a source of conflict. Food Safety Authority respondents name access to sites as the main barrier. Some farmers mention an unwritten rule not to compete for sites, which the authors say does not apply everywhere. Reluctance to give up sites can make fallowing zones too small. The Authority increasingly mandates coordinated fallowing by approving the sum of operation plans. Farmers say it is easier to agree on a plan than to hold it when one farm must take a costly action.

In spring 2019, Chrysochromulina leadbeaterii hit 14 farms in northern Nordland and southern Troms. Farmers lost 8 million salmon (14 000 tonnes). Some lost up to 85 percent of fish in the pens. None were threatened by bankruptcy after five profitable years, but processing plants faced two years of lower deliveries. The response was farmer-led, with public and research support, and included farms of all sizes. Site-level preparedness plans often assumed the same local suppliers. When many farms were hit at once, that capacity was exceeded. Farmers improvised with purse seiners and the Coast Guard as a sea lab. Smaller farmers without unused sites needed authorities to make emergency sites available.

The authors conclude that area cooperation should as far as possible be industry-initiated and voluntary, but that it needs a public shadow of the state for resources, permits and emergency sites. Collective emergency plans exist in some areas, not all. Practical exercises appear very rare.

### Uncertainty and limitations
Findings are self-reported. Effects of cooperation on lice, disease or profit are not measured. Farmers disagree on who should initiate cooperation and on universal versus tailored rules. Formal contract texts are not analysed.

### Relevance to this corpus
This is the journal statement of how shared water is self-governed, and where public design of emergency sites is still needed.
