A B S T R A C T  
This article focus is on the perceived impact that aquaculture industry has on coastal communities in Northern 
Norway. Here, aquaculture is key industry with natural, social and economic impacts. In natural resource 
management in general, identifying and monitoring the perceived social impacts can be a useful tool for local 
planning. In order to ensure the blue growth goals of the Norwegian government and avoid conflict and mistrust 
in the future, it is important to understand how both the general public and stakeholders perceive the aqua­
culture industry, how it affects them and its use of space in the coastal zone. Hence, we ask a) how do coastal 
communities perceive the aquaculture industry and b) is there a legitimacy gap between the blue growth stra­
tegies of the Norwegian Government and the public? In order to answer these questions, we lean on theories 
related to legitimacy and stakeholder’s participation. Original data were collected from structured (N = 150) and 
semi-structured interviews (N = 10) in two coastal communities in Northern Norway (Alstahaug and Brønnøy). 
Our findings suggest that a legitimacy gap does exist between blue growth goals and fishers in the communities 
studied, while the general citizen holds a positive attitude towards aquaculture. Insights from this study are 
useful for local, regional and national decision makers with responsibility for natural resource policies and 
development efforts.   
1. Introduction 
Sustainable development remains problematic for aquaculture 
around the world (Mazur and Curtis, 2006; Hersoug and Johnsen, 
2012). Concerns related to negative environmental impacts and disputes 
about access to space are mixed with benefits such as economic growth 
and employment. Northern coastal communities are receiving 
increasing international attention, due to its potential for value creation 
based on its natural resources (Hersoug and Johnsen, 2012). In 2018, 
the total value-added contributions from the overall seafood industry, 
including ripple effects, passed $ 100 billion for the first time in 2018 
(Richardsen et al., 2019). The overall employment effect of the Nor­
wegian seafood industry has stabilized around 66,000 man-years over 
the past two years. 29,000 full-time equivalents are produced directly in 
the industry, while as many as 37,000 full-time equivalents are created 
as ripple effects (Richardsen et al., 2019). Typically, authorities in these 
contexts focus on the environmental impacts of industries that wants to 
develop and exploit these, and a variety of procedures are in place to 
evaluate and monitor the environmental impacts. Often, however, the 
social impact that these industrial developments have, are overlooked 
(Billing, 2018). Social impacts are here understood as all impacts – both 
positive and negative – that industrial development have on local 
communities. Moreover, the environmental impacts also affect peoples’ 
everyday life as they can generate a variety of feelings such as fear or 
hope for the future (Giddens, 1984; Bjørkan and Veland, 2019). Hence, 
environmental impacts also have a social dimension. It is important to 
understand how that there are significant differences in how different 
sectors in society perceive such impact in general. This is because there 
is a variety of values and belief systems, which means that sustainability 
and impacts is subject to different interpretations (Dryzek, 1997; Mazur 
and Curtis, 2006). 
In this article, we focus on the perceived impact that the aquaculture 
industry has on coastal communities in Northern Norway. In natural 
resource management in general, identifying and monitoring the 
perceived social impacts can be a useful tool for local planning: the local 
authorities can get valuable information for planning and effective 
management; and the aquaculture industry can become aware about 
community opposition to aquaculture development that can be key to

The Norwegian government has an explicit goal of ensuring growth

Also, user conflicts in the coastal zone is expected to increase as new 
interests such as aquaculture and fishing tourism are claiming more 
space (Aanesen et al., 2018; Buanes et al., 2005; Hersoug, 2013; Aanesen 
et al., 2018; Jentoft and Buanes et al., 2005; Hersoug and Johnsen, 2012;

risks, such as environmental pollution, devastating effects on the wild 
salmon stock as well as too little retention of benefits to the local com­

others user of the coastal zone is considered a major obstacle (Hersoug, 
2013; Sandersen and Nicolaisen, 2007, Johnsen and Hersoug, 2012). In 
addition, there has been a growing concern about the lack of benefits 
and share of the values to the municipalities by the aquaculture in­
dustry, and some argues that a few are reaping the benefits from the 
Norwegian coastal commons (Hersoug, 2013). 
Sometimes, there is a divide between what the formal authority and 
popular opinion support, often referred to as the legitimacy gap. Here, 
we explore issues related to coastal zone management, focusing on local 
perceptions and legitimacy of the aquaculture industry, and ask: a) how 
do coastal communities perceive the aquaculture industry and b) is there 
a legitimacy gap between the blue growth strategies of the Norwegian 
Government and the public? In the Norwegian coastal zone management 
context, four axes of conflicts regarding aquaculture are identified. 
These are “1) access to sea areas, 2) effects on natural environment, in 
particular sea lice and escapes, 3) fishing, commercial- and recreational 
fishing, and 4) community development, the distribution of advantages and 
disadvantages” (Karlsen et al., 2015: 87). We will use the four axes of 
conflict to explore the local perception of aquaculture and how it affects 
its legitimacy. 
The aim of this article is to contribute to the literature on the issue of 
social legitimacy in a bottom-up perspective in contexts where there is a 
tendency to rather look to technical and economic data (Billing, 2018). 
Also, the article contributes to enlighten the importance of power in­
equalities in participatory management practices (Adger et al., 2005; 
Pomeroy et al., 2004, Stepanova., 2015); and give voice to the less 
important fishers – that is, in economic terms compared to aquaculture – 
perspective in the Nordland region. There is increasing consensus that 
sustainable and legitimate Coastal Zone Management depend on an 
interdisciplinary approach combining three pillars of sustainability. 
Accordingly, in addition to the biological aspects, the economic and 
social issues are relevant (Stephenson et al., 2017; Buanes et al., 2004). 
We draw on research that has explored whether there are differences in 
perceptions about the risks and benefits of aquaculture among stake­
holders and within the broader public in Northern Norway. 
1.1. Conceptual framework: Legitimacy, space and stakeholders 
Differences in perception mean that the same activity, such as 
aquaculture, can be viewed differently by different stakeholder groups 
or individuals. One way of defining environmental perception is as the 
“awareness of, or feelings about, the environment, and as the act of 
apprehending the environment by the senses” (Zube, 1999: preview). As 
further described below, we are particularly concerned with how fishers 
who has a stake in the matter and the general public perceive aqua­
culture and its impacts on the environment and society. 
If society does not accept aquaculture as wanted industry that is 
accepted to impact nature, society and to occupy space in the coastal 
zone, this can be an important barrier for blue growth. This put legiti­
macy at the center stage of this article. Legitimacy is a much-used term 
in social theory. Credit is typically given to Max Weber (1946) for 
linking legitimacy with conformity to social norms and formal laws 
(Gehman et al., 2017). Suchman (1995) has a widely cited definition of 
legitimacy “a generalized perception or assumption that the actions of an 
entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially constructed 
system of norms, values, beliefs and definitions” (Suchman, 1995:574). In 
general, legitimacy is assumed to induce compliance, encourage 
participation and lower costs. Accordingly, legitimacy is important to 
ensure the stability of social, political or economic systems. Scharpf 
(1999) makes a distinction between “input legitimacy” and “output 
legitimacy. In line with Løvbrand et al. (2009) we understand input 
legitimacy as a procedural ideal, here in relation to actors affected by 
aquaculture, a voice in the planning process; and relate output legitimacy 
to the capacity or the efficiency (or problem-solving capacity) of the 
coastal zone plans in place. That is, e.g., the space assigned for aqua­
culture sites and how these are perceived as causing problems or not 
regarding issues such as pollution, hampering other activities, and so on. 
With regards to input legitimacy, coastal zone planning is organized 
to be democratic process to allocate areas for different interests 
involving both the general public and stakeholder (Hersoug and John­
sen, 2012). As put by Jentoft (2000), “the issue of space affects the entire 
society”. This is because it affects everyone’s access to the coastal zone; 
where you can fish, where you can do recreational activities, where 
aquaculture pens can be put and so on. The assumption is that including 
stakeholders will make the process more legitimate (Buanes et al., 
2004). If this is the actual outcome is an empirical question, depending 
on issues such as power, the level of activity and whose concerns and 
interests are understood as relevant and justifiable according to the key 
coordinators of the process (Buanes et al., 2004). Based on Freeman 
(1984) definition of stakeholders where any group or individual affected 
by these activities are understood as stakeholders, any group or indi­
vidual in coastal communities could be defined as stakeholders. Who the 
relevant stakeholders are, will be a dynamic and empirical question. 
Fishermen, fish farmers, farmers, fish processors, land proprietors, 
reindeer herders, second home developers, tourist entrepreneurs and 
transporter are some of those with economic interests in the coastal 
zone. Some, like coastal fishermen, are strongly linked to the coastal 
communities, while others, such as international aquaculture farms, are 
less so (Buanes et al., 2004, 2005). 
In this article, we differentiate between fishers as the key stake­
holders, since they are in direct competition for space with the aqua­
culture industry and their income can be affected by the potentially 
negative impact that aquaculture have on the environment (i.e., sea lice 
agents, escapes and pollution); and the general public which comprises 
latent stakeholders’ and expectant stakeholders in the two communities. 
Hence, these are all with a registered address in Alstahaug and Brønnøy.

2. Methods 
We have used a mix of qualitative and quantitative methods to 
investigate both how the general public and key stakeholders perceive 
aquaculture. Since perceptions are not observable as such, we have used 
semi-structured and structured interviews with questions related to the 
aquaculture industry in the two communities studied. 
This article is mainly based on data collected for the EU project 
“Regina”. One of the main tasks was to realize a “Social Impact Analysis” 
(SIMP) which aims at identifying, monitoring and managing social im­
pacts of large-scale industries in our cases. SIMP is tool designed to: 
provide strategic planning benefits for municipal planning, private 
sector industry and local residents alike. For municipalities, it is a 
tool for predicting and planning local developments in relation to 
large-scale industries, which helps to improve social sustainability 
and retain the local benefits of industrial growth. For industry, it 
offers a way to gain a social license to operate (SLO) and obtain local 
acceptance for the project (Suopaj¨arvi et al., 2017: 9). 
Hence, a SIMP can be used to solve problems in a way that leads to 
more desirable outcomes (Franks 2012; Franks et al. 2010; Franks & 
Vanclay 2013.7 Porter et al. 2013, p. 658). When developing the ques­
tions for the structured interviews that the SIMP was based on, we were 
mainly inspired by challenges addressed by the media as well as issues 
discovered in semi-structured interviews with key stakeholders 
(Table 1). Based on this, we developed a structured interview form with 
ten questions carried out by a professional data collecting bureau 
“Polarfakta” (see appendix for the questions). Some of these questions 
are relevant for the purpose of this article and some that are not. The 
questions were easily “translated” into the four axes of conflict. For 
instance, in order to interpret the results regarding access to space, we 
chose to understand comments regarding how aquaculture impacts 
other industries, such as tourism and recreational activities, as related to 
space. The informants for the SIMP were chosen by “Polarfakta” in order 
to represent a variety in occupation and age (N = 70 in Alstahaug and N 
- 80 in Brønnøy). The questions followed a structure that can be exem­
plified with the following: “The aquaculture industry participates in the 
development of your municipality – please rate from 1 to 6 where 1 is 
very discontent and 6 is very content”. Note that there was also the 
option of “do not know”. Depending on the context and the issue at 
hand, some of those asked can of course also have a stake. Here, we 
relate to those interviewed for the SIMP as the general public since they

We find that the structured interviews provided useful and relevant 
data. Still, based on extensive research over several years in the region,

related to the conflicts between fishers and the aquaculture industry.

collected data through face-to face interviews based on semi-structured, 
open-ended interviews (Table 1). We draw on ten semi-structured in­
terviews that were done in person on-board fishers’ vessels or in their 
home. Two interviews were carried out in 2016–2017 (Rybråten et al.,

2.1. Data analysis 
The structured interviews were analyzed statistically by “Polarfakta” 
and a report was compiled with figures illustrating the quantitative re­
sults, including the differences between the two communities (see Figs. 2 
and 3 for examples). The qualitative, semi-structured interviews lasted 
from one to 2 h and were recorded and transcribed. The questions were 
open-ended and structured loosely around the issue of a) perceived 
changes and challenges and b) how this has impacted them (see also 
Bjørkan and Rybråten, 2018). The analysis has been carried out in four 
steps; first as a part of the larger EU project Regina; second, for this 
paper we have chosen to focus on issues from the data with particular 
bearing on perceptions of aquaculture; third, the semi-structured in­
terviews, observations and survey data allowed us to investigate local 
perception of aquaculture from different angles and hence triangulate 
data (Silverman, 1993; Storbj¨ork and Hedr´en, 2011); and finally, we 
analyzed the data by organizing them into the predefined categories that 
our framework provides, that is, the four axis of conflict. 
3. Context 
3.1. Norway as a seafood nation 
Norway is a major seafood nation, and the growth potential of 
Norwegian marine-based industries is huge (Olafsen et al., 2012; Almås 
and Ratvik, 2017). The Government has developed a marine strategy 
(20171) stating that the goal is for Norway to become the world’s 
leading seafood nation, with an aquaculture production of 5 million tons 
in 2050. This is almost four times the current production (PwC, 2017). 
The marine strategy is presented over more than 100 pages; hence, the 
key points related to aquacultures development is summarized in 
Table 2 below. 
In 2019 an updated version of the strategy underlines the need to 
focus on three policy areas: climate change, to keep on top of techno­
logical and digital developments and of special importance here; to 
promote local value creation “to reduce the vulnerability of regional 
business communities and strengthen the adaptability of the regions” 
(2019; 73). Exactly how this should be achieved is not stated in this 
strategy, rather, it is addressed in the “Regional Strategy” 
(Distriktsmeldinga4). 
In Norway, aquaculture sites are found all along the coast. The main 
areas have been in the west, but the production is extending from the 
west coast towards the north. While there has been an enormous growth 
the last 25 year in production (six-fold) and the number of licenses has 
doubled, there are actually fewer sites today (Report to the Storting, 
2012–2013). Hence, the production on each site has increased, and there 
is a trend towards bigger sites and more “super-locations” that allows a 
Maximum Tons Biomass over 5.400 tons or more (Isaksen et al., 2012). 
With larger sites comes the need for bigger and better-suited areas 
(Gullestadutvalget, 2011). This is also related to veterinary regulations 
that impacts the distance between sites, the need for fallowing and a 
rotating use of sites (Gullestadutvalget, 2011).

ment opportunities and regional as well as national wealth. At the same

3.2. The development of the Norwegian aquaculture industry 
The aquaculture industry was from its beginning in the 1970s small 
and family run companies rooted in their local communities. How the 
role of the aquaculture industry has developed and changed over the 
year is interesting because it affects how people perceive as legitimacy, 
and this section draws largely on Holm and Henriksen (2016). When 
aquaculture was established as a new activity in the Norwegian coastal 
zone there were clearly defined policy goals for the industry to 
contribute to strengthening the rural areas and support the local fish­
eries (Holm and Henriksen, 2016, St.meld.nr.71, 1979–1980). In a 
policy brief (St.meld.nr.71, 1979–1980) which led to the first permanent 
aquaculture law in 1981, it is stated that 
it is a goal to achieve the largest possible positive impact on society by 
developing the aquaculture industry, through the establishment of as 
many good and safe workplaces as possible in the districts (St.meld. 
Nr.71, 1979–1980). 
Three key steps were taken in order to ensure this: locally controlled 
ownership rather than a large-scale industry based on big capital from 
“the outside”. In order to achieve this, the size of each site was limited as 
well as ownership restrictions that would ensure local majority interests. 
Also, the rights (konsesjoner) was given based on a district-friendly 
profile, focusing on areas in need for more industrial activity. More­
over, the market was organized to protect the owners of the aquaculture 
sites who were considered as the “weaker link” in negotiations with the 
buyer. This is in line with how the traditional fisheries in Norway is 
organized. 
From the early 1990s, it became clear that key framework conditions 
(i.e., ownership restrictions pointed to above) in place to ensure the 
industry as a district industry, were limiting for the operations with 
regards to market-oriented profit. We may say that early on, there were 
obvious conflicts between two objectives; the profit on the one hand and 
the policies focusing on the need of the rural areas. Over time, these 
polices were gradually removed. Hence, a significant structural change 
took place in 1990’s, the industry shifted from this small family run 
businesses to large-scale international companies with more cost- 
effective concentrations. This was a result of the Aquaculture Bill of 
1991 as it became possible to own several licenses and sell these (Her­
soug, 2015; Hersoug and Johnsen, 2012). Around the turn of the cen­
tury, during a very profitable period for the industry, the Norwegian 
companies became part of an internationalization wave, with acquisi­
tions in Chile, North America and the UK. In Norway, there were five 
major companies which since early 2000 are all part of Marine Harvest,6 
the world’s largest aquaculture company. Also, new medium sized 
companies, such as Lerøy and Salmar has emerged. Marine Harvest is the 
main firm in our case studies. 
Today, the large-scale organization with fewer but bigger companies 
and more cost-effective concentrations dominates the industry (Sand­
ersen and Nicolaisen, 2007). While production has grown, ownership 
has been concentrated. Numerous mergers and acquisitions have made 
local ownership much smaller, while many functions have been 
centralized to fewer and larger locations. The aquaculture industry has 
about the same employment today as in the mid-80s, although

towards further aquaculture growth. This is related to a number of is­
sues, such as environmental concerns (e.g., sea lice, escapes, feces and 
chemicals), competition with other users of the same coastal space (e.g., 
fishers, tourism and leisure), and the modest local economic benefits – 
due to the above explained restructuring of the industry. Hence, when 
the communities do not allocate new productions sites in line with the 
aquaculture industry’s growing need for spaces, they are described as 
gatekeepers, hampering blue growth (Hersoug, 2013; Sandersen and

benefits to the municipalities enabling aquaculture sites. How the 
“aquaculture fund” will work in practice will be interesting to follow – at 
the time of the study there had been no allocations made by the fund. 
3.3. Planning and legal framework 
The Planning and Building Act of 1985 (PBA 85) was the first step 
towards coastal zone management in Norway, granting municipal au­
thorities with the right to establish legally binding plans for adjacent 
coastal waters (Buanes et al., 2004). Accordingly, the sea within 
municipal waters now became subject to zoning for various categories of 
use. In a context with a rapidly increasing number of interests such as 
the offshore oil industry and the fish farming industry causing territorial 
conflicts with fishermen in particular, coastal zone planning became an 
important planning tool (Buanes et al., 2004). By law, coastal zone 
planning in Norway is intended to be open, transparent, inclusive, 
participatory and deliberate (PBL, 1989). In Norway, the coastal zone 
plans are the responsibilities of the 276 coastal communities, which has 
the authority decide spatial planning to the base line plus one nautical 
mile (Karlsen et al., 2015). Within this area, the coastal municipalities 
have the responsibility to formally invite stakeholders to participate in 
the planning process. The user groups and stakeholders involved in 
coastal zone planning may differ from community to community, since 
there are natural and social conditions that plays a role (Buanes et al., 
2004). 
While the coastal zone planning proses is anchored at the municipal 
level, the local political body do not have total autonomy. The municipal 
autonomy is limited by sector laws, national guidelines and policy 
provision (Sandersen and Kvalvik, 2015). This puts state agencies in a 
position to raise formal objections to municipal coastal zone plan pro­
posals if these do not comply with national policies. The Department of 
Environment is the final decision-maker. Still, the municipalities are 
quite independent in their decision-making (Sandersen and Kvalvik, 
2015). 
3.4. Study area: Alstahaug and Brønnøy 
The two municipalities Alstahaug and Brønnøy are both located in 
Nordland County, Northern Norway (Fig. 1). Nordland County holds 
mountains, forests and fjords, and a coastline with a large number of 
islands, generating both opportunities and challenges for its inhabitants. 
The conditions are exceptional for fishing, farming and aquaculture. In 
both communities, the multinational aquaculture leviathan Marine 
Harvest (now MOWI) have several locations. This predominantly is a 
rural area with long geographic distances between towns and settle­
ments and hence difficult and expensive logistics. Also, migration from 
rural to urban areas is a continuous challenge for smaller communities 
(Amundsen, 2012; Kaltenborn et al., 2017), despite the explicitly stated 
objective of the Norwegian Parliament to maintain the main features of 
the current settlement pattern (Report to the Parliament, 2016–2018). 
The municipality of Brønnøy have an approximate of 7.900 inhabitants. 
The government agency “Brønnøysundregistrene” contributes to about 
75% of the jobs in the municipality in the tertiary industries. In Brønnøy, 
a number of companies in agriculture, fisheries, aquaculture, construc­
tion and various service industries have developed business activity in 
the municipality. Brønnøy Kalk AS is a mining company located in the 
eastern part of the municipality, and there is a helicopter base for oil 
related activities in the region. The municipality of Alstahaug have are 
approximate of 7.400 inhabitants. Alstahaug municipality has a varied 
industry consisting of the engineering industry, shipbuilding, oil supply 
base, aquaculture, agriculture and trade. A number of companies make 
deliveries to the oil and gas industry. 
In the municipalities studied, as in Norway in general, there has been 
a steady decline in fishers. In Brønnøy, there was 122 fishers in 1990 and 
83 in Alstahaug. In 2019, there was 32 fishers in Brønnøy and 26 in 
Alstahaug. In Nordland, the total number of fishers (main occupation) 
was 2071 in 2019.8 
4. Results 
Below, we present the results from the social impact analysis tar­
geting the general public and interviews from fishers as key stake­
holders. We structure the results from the general public and 
stakeholder’s perception based on the four access of conflicts (access to 
space, natural environment, fishing, community development).

career. In Brønnøy, the perception is overwhelmingly positive and more 
than 70% gives this a high score (five out six or more); and in Alstahaug 
50% gives this a high score (five out of six or more). Only 14% is very

Alstahaug. Here, more than 93% finds that the aquaculture industry has 
no negative or small negative impacts on the tourism (score 1–2). In 
Brønnøy, those asked even stated that aquaculture is positive for 
tourism. This may be related to the establishment of an exhibition center 
for aquaculture open for tourists (“Toft Havbrukssenter”). Furthermore, 
about 86% in Brønnøy finds that the aquaculture industry has no 
negative or small negative impacts (score 1–2) on the recreational/ 
outdoor possibilities. In Alstahaug, more than 81% finds that the 
aquaculture industry has no negative or small negative impacts (score 
1–2) on the tourism. More than 65% finds that the aquaculture industry 
has no negative or small negative impacts (1–2) on the recreational/ 
outdoor possibilities. With regards to the tourism and recreational 
possibilities, there hardly seems to be any perceived impacts in any of 
the municipalities. 
In sum, the aquaculture industry seems to be perceived as an industry 
with acceptable impact, even if not directly related to the way of life of 
those asked. It is perceived to generate local employment, ripple effects 
for suppliers and increased income for the municipality. 
One rather surprising issue occurred as the structured interviews 
started in Alstahaug: quite a few respondents did not know about 
aquaculture farms located in their municipality at all. This was unex­
pected to us since aquaculture is a key industry in the area, and because 
the industry is quite regularly in the press for negative reasons, including 
chemical treatment, escapes and pollution (Fiskeribladet, 2016, 2019; 
Tollesrud and Dreyer, 2016). In order to investigate the reason for this 
lack of knowledge about the presence of aquaculture pens in the mu­
nicipality, we had to change the approach in Alstahaug slightly. We 
decided to start the interview by asking: Do you know if there are any 
aquaculture sites in the municipality Alstahaug. Interestingly, 48% of the 
persons asked did not know that their municipality has aquaculture sites 
in their coastal zone. We then informed them that there are four local­
ities and also, the regional headquarter for Marine Harvest in Northern 
Norway is located in Alstahaug. We further asked the informants about 
the reason for not knowing about the aquaculture activity in their mu­
nicipality. Almost 20% answer that they “simply don’t know about it”, 
while almost 13% “don’t care about it”. More than 31% relates this to 
what they consider to a part of the municipality area – the changes in 
municipal structure (took place in 1965). More than 35% “had forgotten 
about it”. 
4.2. Fishers perception 
Looking at the data from the SIMP alone, does not provide a full 
picture of the situation with regards to the attitude towards aquaculture. 
Drawing on participatory observation during a workshop9 in 2017, for 
instance, we find that there are more negative attitudes easily available. 
Here, the interrelated issues of space, planning and process was sug­
gested by a planner as a key barrier for Brønnøys’ strategic development. 
Importantly, out of 40 persons present, 30 persons agreed on this. Also, 
the same planner expressed frustration about the increasing competition 
for space, the process of allocating this space and the lack of resources in 
the planning process. In addition, when interviewing key stakeholders 
–fishers – who are in direct competition for the same space, the conflicts 
are more obvious (see Rybråten et al., 2018; Bjørkan and Rybråten, 
2018) for a more detailed description of the space conflicts). 
Quite a few shrimp fjords in the region can no longer be entered by 
shrimp fishers. This is because the aquaculture sites block the entrance 
in a way that makes it impossible to access or because it is risky for the 
gear, typically due to tethering and bottom installation invisible to the 
general public. Also, shrimp fishers find that the shrimp has changed its 
behavior as a consequence of aquaculture. One fisher described how he 
experienced the change of a shrimp field: 
We noticed that after they placed the aquaculture pens there, the shrimp 
stopped coming in (Informant #1, 2017). 
Another fisherman described that 
the sea is filled with hagfish (Myxine glutinosa). It has never been a 
problem before, but now we keep getting it. It is disgusting” (Informant 
#4, 2019) 
The fisher related this to the growth of aquaculture sites in the area. 
In addition, the shrimp stock seems to have declined considerably 
(Bechmann et al., 2017, 2018). As further explained in Bjørkan and 
Rybråten (2018), shrimp fishers are convinced that the decline in shrimp 
is a direct effect from the increased aquaculture operations in the region 
– there is also increasing evidence for aquacultures negative impact on 
crustaceans (Bechmann et al., 2017, 2018). Other aspects of the natural 
environment are also perceived to be affected in different ways by 
aquaculture. One fisherman explained: 
There are no [parasites] left. The halibut is not eaten [by parasites]. We 
can leave the gillnets for 14 days, and the halibut is untouched. This is 
very unusual, so it is pretty bad down there (Informant #2, 2017).

environment in the sea. Another problem that fishermen is experiencing 
is that the aquaculture fodder attracts wild fish, especially saith. The 
saith feeds on the excess fodder and becomes very fat and the quality of 
the liver and even the filet is perceived as ruined. At least some of the 
fish buyers do not buy this “fatty saith”. A fisherman explained:

5. Discussion 
In summary, the general public in municipalities asked think that the 
aquaculture industry does have a positive effect for the municipality 
especially with regards to socio-economic aspects while the negative 
effects are perceived as the impact the aquaculture industry has on the 
natural systems. They are quite neutral about how aquaculture impacts 
their and others access to space, e.g., fishing and tourism. Fishers, on the 
other hand, have a negative perception of aquaculture. There are several 
reasons for this, as the quotes above exemplifies: fishers access to areas is 
restricted as aquaculture monopolizes space; they experience decline in 
shrimp catches and that the quality of fish changes when fish feed on 
aquaculture fodder; but they are less negative about the impact they 
perceive aquaculture has on community development. Following the 
public debate about aquaculture and a substantial body of peer reviewed 
literature, the impression is that the industry has no legitimacy and faces 
a massive resistance from the Norwegian population (see for instance 
the series of articles by the newspaper Morgenbladet “Dark counter­
forces11” (Mørke Motkrefter). Hence, it was surprising to find such pos­
itive attitudes in the two communities studied which does not coincide 
with national nor international discourses on aquaculture. Indeed, we 
expected that the answer to our research questions would be of a 
negative note: that the coastal communities does not accept the de­
cisions made about aquaculture pens in their coastal zone and that there 
is a legitimacy gap between the blue growth strategies and the public. As 
our findings points to, the answer is more complex than a simple yes or 
no. While the output legitimacy of aquaculture in the general public is 
high, it is very low with key stakeholders, here fishers. This is not sur­
prising, since the fishers’ perception of the aquaculture industry is 
affected by the experiences they have in their practices in the coastal 
zone. Arguably, the general public do not experience the impact that 
aquaculture have on the environment and their income is not directly 
affected by aquaculture. Fishers, however, are directly affected by 
aquaculture with regards to all four of the axes of conflict (see Table 3): 
access to area, natural environment and fishing. They are less negative 
towards aquacultures impact on community development. 
Some studies have found that there is a link between the legitimacy 
of aquaculture and the perception of socioeconomic benefits for local 
areas and lower environmental impacts (Katranidis et al., 2003; Mazur 
and Curtis, 2006). A general trend in the data is that in Brønnøy there is a 
greater sense of a positive return to the community in terms of increased 
employment, education and career-opportunities than in Alstahaug. 
Also, Fish farming is very visible in Brønnøy and has always been visible. 
The display facility at Toft has been active in showcasing the industry. 
The establishment of a local “aquaculture academy12” is probably 
important as well. In addition, Brønnøy has kept its focus on the primary 
industries. Main attention for industrial development has been on the 
ripple-effect from agriculture, fisheries and aquaculture as well as the 
mining industry. In Alstahaug, the main focus has been the oil and gas 
development, and the municipality promotes itself as an oil and gas area. 
This has turned out to generate little employment and revenue. Hence, 
our results may suggest that there indeed is a link between perceived 
socio-economic impacts and the legitimacy of the aquaculture industry. 
Still, the lack of knowledge about aquaculture sites in the region re­
flected in the answer in the poll is an issue that should be kept in mind 
when trying to understand what result from this SIMP really means. 
According to literature, there is a “legitimacy gap” in current aqua­
culture management, “where aquaculture is failing to meet its potential 
because of the lack of integration of knowledge relating to social, 
ecological and economic issues” (Billing, 2018: 2. See also; Krause et al., 
2015). 
While our findings suggest that the general public don’t see aqua­
culture as problematic and that there are socio-economic benefits, it also 
underlines the importance of differentiate between key stakeholders and 
others. We find that the fishers, who are directly impacted by the 
aquaculture industry, has a negative perception of the aquaculture in­
dustry. In line with Buanes et al. (2004), we find that the opinion of key 
stakeholders should be given more emphasis. This underlines the need to 
differentiate between the general public in such matters and the stake­
holders who are directly affected by the activity. Our findings resonate 
with Aanesen et al. (2018). In their article they argue that the resistance 
against aquaculture industry is actually found in the larger cities, while 
those living in the rural coastal areas needs a livelihood and see aqua­
culture as an important employer. Indeed, their study also points to the

there is no legitimacy gap between the blue growth strategies and the 
general public, but for the fishers, this legitimacy gap is quite big. 
This is related to a dilemma address by Gehman et al. (2017): should 
a vocal minority overrule the will of a less vocal majority, that is, should 
fishers overrule the will of the general public in the communities stud­
ied? Fishers have a stake, an interest, as they are competing with 
aquaculture pens for space, hence, their opinion should be given 
emphasis (Buanes et al., 2004). Of course, the same interest can cause 
suspicion since their criticism is not based on an objective observation. 
At the same time, it is reasonable to assume that fishers have experience 
based knowledge about the marine environment that the general public 
do not, since they regularly go out to sea and experience – observe, 
smell, see, touch – any changes to the natural environment in general 
and shrimp in particular (See for instance Bjørkan, 2011: on experience 
based knowledge). Whose interest should be given priority in a coastal 
zone plan is a political matter, and there is no one answer to the ques­
tion. But turning to literature on legitimacy, the aquaculture industry 
would arguably be a perceived as more legitimate if key stakeholders 
like fishers were more involved in the making of coastal zone plans and 
establishing some formal arenas for dialogue – that is, improving the 
input legitimacy for key stakeholders would possibly affect the output 
legitimacy for the same group. At the same time, “legitimacy is discur­
sively determined, it is constantly subject to review, assessment and revalu­
ation, this is a potentially volatile tribute” (Gearey and Jeffrey, 2006: 130). 
Hence, there is no quick fix to issues related to local perceptions and 
legitimacy. 
6. Conclusion 
Based on literature and reports about the lack of social sustainability 
of the aquaculture industry, we expected a much more negative attitude 
towards aquaculture in the municipalities than this study suggests. Still, 
our findings suggest that a legitimacy gap does exist between blue 
growth goals and fishers in the communities studied. This may possibly 
be true for other key stakeholders as well, since the numbers of stake­
holders is increasing both in number and variety. Hence, in order to 
ensure the blue growth goals of the Norwegian government and avoid 
conflict and mistrust in the future, it is important to understand how 
both the general public and stakeholders perceive the aquaculture in­
dustry, how it affects them and its use of space in the coastal zone. If not, 
a likely consequence is a negative spiral of conflict and mistrust (Mazur 
and Curtis, 2006). This in turn is likely to hamper the blue growth goals 
of the Norwegian Government, and hence add to global challenges that 
blue growth is expected to solve, such as hunger and employment. As 
demonstrated, different perspectives can lead to conflict over coastal 
zone planning and management. The take-away message is that gov­
ernments at different levels will be better equipped to develop a socially 
acceptable and sustainable industry by understanding the range of views 
on aquaculture.
