ABSTRACT 
This paper estimates the effect of spatial proximity to other 
salmon farms on aquaculture firms’ costs. Open cage salmon 
aquaculture farms cause and face externalities, such as various 
diseases and salmon lice. Hydrodynamic forces and the spatial 
density of farms influence the transmission of these external­
ities. We analyze the effects of salmon farm proximity on pro­
duction cost using a translog cost function estimated from a 
panel data set of Norwegian aquaculture farms. We employ 
two indicators of the spatial density of aquaculture produc­
tion. The first indicator is the mean distance to the nearest 
farm, and the second indicator is the number of farms within 
20 km distance for all the farms. We find that increased prox­
imity to other farms is associated with significant cost 
increase. The results imply that there are economic welfare 
effects due to the farm location structure and the spatial bio­
logical externalities between farms.

Introduction
Norway is the world’s largest farmed salmon producer (Afewerki et al., 
2023; Garlock et al., 2023). Total production has increased from a few 
thousand tons in 1980 to about 1.6 million tons today. As a consequence, 
the Norwegian coast has become increasingly utilized by salmon farms. At 
individual farm locations, production has during the same time period 
increased from around 100 tons per year to several thousand tons, and 
there has been a significant consolidation and developments in firm struc­
ture (Asche et al., 2013; Oglend et al., 2022; Pandey et al., 2023; Straume 
et al., 2024). The increased production at each site is associated with 
greater concern about environmental and disease externalities caused by 
each farm as well as the spread between farms, a feature the industry has 
in common with most other aquaculture industries (Asche, Eggers, et al., 
2022; Garlock et al., 2024). These externalities affect biological and

economic productivity of salmon farms (Tveterås, 2002), and one would 
expect that the impact differs with farm density in any area. In the absence 
of sufficient innovation to moderate the externalities, further growth of sal­
mon aquaculture can lead to, for example, increased salmon lice prevalence 
and treatments, which can reduce biological productivity and animal wel­
fare (Dean et al., 2021). In terms of geographical space, externalities can be 
local at a farm site, regional affecting sites close to the farm, and within 
larger areas with many farms. In this paper, we analyze the impacts of a 
more intensive area use involving several farms on production costs.
Salmon aquaculture faces significant biological challenges, with diseases 
and other fish health problems, salmon lice, salmon escapees, and organic 
emissions identified as key externalities affecting the industry (Bjørndal & 
Tusvik, 2020; Love et al., 2023, 2024; Pincinato, Asche, et al., 2021; Walde 
et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2012). In recent years, the salmon lice problem 
has emerged as the most significant in terms of economic impact (Abolofia 
et al., 2017; Oglend et al., 2024; Pincinato et al., 2025) as well as govern­
ance impact, including a societal license to operate and grow (Bailey & 
Eggereide, 2020; Jensen et al., 2024; Krause et al., 2024; Misund et al., 2023; 
Schei Olsen et al., 2024).
Salmon lice are parasitic copepods that feed on the mucus, skin, and 
blood of salmonid fish and can harm the health and growth of farmed sal­
mon (Kristoffersen et al., 2014). Importantly, salmon lice can also spread 
from farmed to wild salmonid populations, posing a threat to the sustain­
ability of wild fish stocks (Krkosek et al., 2007; Torrissen et al., 2013). The 
density of farmed salmon has been shown to be a key driver of salmon lice 
abundance, with higher biomass concentrations leading to increased lice 
levels (Jansen et al., 2012; Kragesteen et al., 2019).
In response to these concerns, the Norwegian government has imple­
mented a series of regulations to manage the impacts of salmon lice. One 
such measure is the “traffic light system” (TLS), which divides the Norwegian 
coastline into 13 production zones and uses salmon lice-induced mortality 
rates on wild salmonids as the primary criterion for determining future pro­
duction growth in each zone (Jensen et al., 2024).1 Under the TLS, produc­
tion zones are classified as green, yellow, or red based on the estimated 
salmon lice-induced mortality rate for wild salmonids. Farms in green zones 
are allowed to increase their maximum allowable biomass (MAB), while 
farms in yellow zones must maintain their current MAB, and farms in red 
zones must reduce their MAB (Kristoffersen et al., 2018). This system incenti­
vizes farmers to control salmon lice levels, as the regulatory consequences can 
significantly impact their production and profitability.
However, the effectiveness of the TLS in managing the salmon lice chal­
lenge is debated (Jensen et al., 2024; van Nes et al., 2025). Farmers may have

This paper investigates the effects of farm location structure on the costs 
of salmon aquaculture firms. Translog cost models are estimated which 
account for the geographic distance between farms. We employ two indica­
tors of the spatial configuration of salmon production that are associated 
with the potential for transmission of diseases and salmon lice. The first 
indicator is the distance to the nearest farm, and the second indicator is 
the mean number of farms within a 20 km distance.

research literature. Section three analyzes the geographical distribution of 
salmon farms. Section four discusses the regulation of aquaculture in

five describes the data and empirical cost models. Section six presents the

Spatial externalities and productivity in salmon aquaculture
The economic and biological productivity in salmon aquaculture is deter­
mined by a complex interaction of external influences, e.g. water tempera­
ture, oxygen, fish diseases, salmon lice, and internal decisions and activities 
at farms, e.g. number of smolts released, feeding, monitoring of fish welfare 
and health, actions related to diseases and salmon lice, harvesting, fallowing 
of farm site (Asche & Bjørndal, 2011). It can be argued that the main mar­
ket failure in the salmon industry is externalities from salmon farms to 
other economic agents, in the form of e.g. diseases, salmon lice, salmon 
escapees, and organic emissions. Diseases, salmon lice, and salmon escapees 
are not pure externalities because the farm, which is the source of these 
externalities, will typically also incur costs in terms of own lost biomass 
and reduced profits. However, the costs incurred by neighboring farms and 
wild salmon fishers are not necessarily taken into account unless there are 
regulatory mechanisms that facilitate the internalization of these costs.

Disease and salmon lice externalities are spatial, where the transmission 
of disease agents and salmon lice from one farm to neighboring farms are 
determined by both biological and hydrodynamic factors. A strand of bio­
logical literature studies the epidemiology related to salmon diseases and 
salmon lice, employing knowledge about, e.g., hydrodynamic conditions 
and biological characteristics of salmon and disease agents. Epidemiological 
studies have estimated the relationships between the risk of infection for 
fish diseases and parasites and seaway distance, as shown in Figure 1 for 
the fish diseases HSMI (Heart and Skeletal Muscle Inflammation), PD 
(Pancreas Disease) and ISA (Infectious Salmon Anemia), and the salmon 
lice parasite (Aldrin et al., 2010, 2013, 2017, 2019). These studies show that 
increased farm density will contribute to an increased risk of infection. 
Although this is not analyzed by the cited papers related to Figure 1, these 
relationships could also imply a risk of reduced biological productivity and 
lower economic returns for aquaculture firms with increased farm density.

In general, the highest risk for negative spatial biological externalities from 
other farms related to fish diseases and salmon lice seems to be within 
20 km radius of the farm.
A strand of the economic literature has studied relationships between the 
geographic localization of salmon aquaculture production and productivity 
(Asche et al., 2016; Tveterås, 2002; Tveteras & Battese, 2006).3 The focus 
has been on agglomeration economies related to the emergence of special­
ized suppliers, more competitive input markets, and increased innovation. 
These mechanisms contribute to increased productivity, contributing to 
lower production costs and higher profits for firms. These studies have also 
analyzed the effects of regional farm density, measured as regional number 
of farms per square km. They generally find, with some exceptions that 
increased farms density is associated with lower productivity, measured by 
production volume, costs, and profits. Moreover, Asche et al. (2009) show 
that environmental conditions impact farm efficiency.
Our study has alternative measures of farm density, which measure the 
proximity of aquaculture farms to other farms for each individual firm in 
our dataset. Previous economic studies have used the same density value 
for all firms residing in the same region (Asche et al., 2016; Tveterås, 2002; 
Tveteras & Battese, 2006). Due to the consolidation of the aquaculture 
industry over time, some firms operate multiple farms in several regions, 
and consequently, a regional average farm density measure is not appropri­
ate for these. Furthermore, within a region, there may be significant differ­
ences between individual firms’ farm site structures, with some firms 
having higher farm proximity than others, as shown below in Figure 2.
Geographical distribution of salmon farms
The spatial distribution of salmon farming sites in Norway reflects the 
licensing system’s evolution and the industry’s geographical expansion. 
Salmon farming was initially concentrated in certain coastal regions and 
with fewer farm locations. Over time, the industry has expanded its foot­
print, with farming sites now distributed more widely along the Norwegian 
coastline (Andreassen et al., 2010; Hersoug et al., 2019).
This geographical expansion has been influenced by factors, such as 
water quality, proximity to infrastructure, and local industry acceptance.

We employ both these farm proximity measures later in our econometric

ernment regulation and the underlying mechanisms for density dependent 
biological externalities. The Norwegian government explicitly restricts the 
geographic distance to the nearest farms through regulation. On the other 
hand, spatial biological externalities to a farm may be more related to the 
number of other farms within a given radius. In this paper, we test the 
relationship between both measures and production costs.
This concentration of aquaculture activity has not been without conse­
quences. The density of aquaculture sites is a significant factor in the inci­
dence of diseases, such as salmon lice infections. We have discussed earlier 
that epidemiological studies indicate that the likelihood of infection 
increases as the distance between farms decreases, markedly affecting sal­
mon lice populations (Aldrin et al., 2013, 2017).

Regulatory framework and overall industry performance
Salmon aquaculture firms are regulated by government in several ways: (1) 
The total standing biomass of live salmon (in tons) of the salmon firm is 
regulated by the firm’s Maximum Allowable Biomass (MAB), and this 
MAB can be used at several farm sites. (2) The right to produce at a given 
farm site is regulated by a site license that specifies which firm is allowed 
to operate the site. (3) The standing biomass at an individual farm site is 
regulated by a location MAB, which is based on an assessment of the local 
biological carrying capacity of the farm site. (4) The salmon lice prevalence 
at a farm site is regulated by a constraint on the maximum number of 
adult female salmon lice per fish. The objective of the salmon firm is typic­
ally to maximize its profits (and thus minimize cost), which implies that it 
exploits its biological productivity potential, given all these restrictions 
embodied in government regulations.
The salmon aquaculture industry is subject to stringent regulations to 
ensure biosecurity on farms. The government oversees salmon farms 
through various measures, including the determination of Maximum 
Allowable Biomass (MAB) at farm sites. Each farm site’s production rights 
are controlled by a specific site license, while the standing biomass

However, there is a noticeable lack of rules concerning site distribution 
in the industry, which could impact biosecurity. To address disease trans­
mission concerns, the Norwegian Food Safety Authority enforces minimum 
distances between farms and processing plants. Initially set at 200 meters in 
the 1970s, the distance requirement increased to 1 km by the late 1980s 
and presently stands at 5 km for new facilities. In 2018, facilities with a 
Maximum Allowed Biomass (MTB) of up to 3,600 tons were required to 
maintain a minimum distance of 2.5 km to reduce disease spread risk, 
while those exceeding 3,600 tons MTB must maintain a 5-km distance.
Despite these regulations, debates persist regarding their effectiveness 
and sufficiency in ensuring biosecurity. One crucial regulatory aspect is the 
Traffic Light System (TLS), which categorizes production areas based on 
salmon lice-induced mortality rates for wild salmon. Green areas have mor­
tality rates below 10%, allowing for a 6% increase in MAB. Yellow areas 
have rates between 10 and 30%, maintaining MAB, while red areas with 
rates exceeding 30% face a 6% MAB reduction (Ministry of Trade, Industry 
and Fisheries, 2017).
Figure 3 visualized the TLS regulation application status in 2023 and the 
farmed Atlantic salmon mortality rates distribution over production zones. 
In 2023, Norway observed elevated mortality rates in salmon production, 
particularly in Southern Norway’s Production Zones (PO) 2, 3, and 4, attri­
buted to bacterial gill disease and parasitic infections (Fiskehelserapporten, 
2024). The concentration of production sites in certain zones raises con­
cerns about the efficacy of the TLS in governing salmon farming practices. 
Firms in problematic areas face regulatory penalties and higher mortality 
rates, impacting profitability.

Concluding remarks
In this paper, we have conducted a productivity analysis of Norwegian sal­
mon aquaculture farms where we have added terms to capture spatial 
externalities between farms, allowing us to present new empirical evidence 
of the effects of spatial proximity of salmon farms on aquaculture firms 
costs. Salmon aquaculture farms face externalities caused by various dis­
eases and salmon lice. The spatial transmission of these externalities is 
influenced by hydrodynamic forces and the spatial density of farms. 
Patterns of spatial concentration of production at farm sites and larger 
marine production areas may influence the spread of diseases and salmon 
lice to other farms, and their effects on biological productivity and cost 
productivity. Over time, as the production and number of farms have 
increased, we observe more treatment for salmon lice (which tend to have 
negative effects on farmed salmon welfare), increased losses of farmed sal­
mon (mostly due to increased mortality), and a rise in production costs.
In our analysis, we employed two indicators of the spatial configuration 
of aquaculture production that are associated with the potential for trans­
mission of diseases and salmon lice. The first indicator is the mean distance 
to the nearest farm for all the firm’s farms, and the second indicator is the 
mean number of farms within 20 km distance for all the firm’s farms. We 
find that increased proximity to other farms as measured by both these 
indicators is associated with lower cost productivity.
These empirical findings are important from a regulatory perspective as 
they imply that one should avoid further increased spatial density of open 
cage farms. Spatial biological externality mitigation requires the use of sev­
eral regulatory instruments. The policy toolbox may include a combination 
of farm location structure regulation, biosecurity regulation, emission quo­
tas, and innovation policies. Regulation of farm location structure that 
facilitates increasing production at fewer sites with sufficient biological car­
rying capacity will increase the distance between farms, and can

Biosecurity regulations and practices that change production practices in 
ways that provide lower externalities for a given locational structure are 
another option (Arriagada et al., 2017; Werkman et al., 2011). Examples of 
biosecurity measures are coordinated fallowing, disease treatment, and de- 
lousing across neighboring salmon farms. Such measures can also be 
undertaken voluntarily by aquaculture firms, but government regulations 
may be necessary to induce sufficient coordinated activities that can more 
efficiently reduce negative biological externalities.
Innovation policies that incentivize investments in production technolo­
gies with lower spatial externalities are a third option. An example is gov­
ernment incentives to reduce salmon lice externalities through increased 
investments in semi-closed containment systems (SCCS) or deep sea farm­
ing systems. For salmon lice, it is assessed that treatment technologies may 
not reduce salmon lice externalities to acceptable levels for wild salmon 
stocks and can have negative effects on the welfare of farmed salmon 
(Barrett et al., 2020). Studies have found that salmon lice barrier technolo­
gies in the form of SCCS lead to zero or very low reproduction of salmon 
lice and can provide high biological productivity (Kleppa Øvrebø et al., 
2022; Lazado et al., 2022; Nilsen, Nielsen, Biering, et al., 2017; Nilsen, 
Nielsen, Næss, et al., 2017; Nilsen et al., 2020). Deep farming systems, 
where farmed salmon is kept below the water levels where salmon lice 
thrive, also have demonstrated low lice reproduction rates and competitive 
biological productivity (Oppedal et al., 2020; Sievers et al., 2022; Warren- 
Myers et al., 2022). However, more innovation is required for SCCS in 
particular, as they currently have high investment costs and there are sig­
nificant technical, biological, and economic risks for farmers. Furthermore, 
there is a classical market failure since aquaculture firms do not internalize 
the benefits of reduced externalities of production with semi-closed con­
tainment systems to other firms and societal stakeholders in their invest­
ment decisions. Hence, the government should consider incentives for both 
innovation and internalization of externalities through mechanisms, such as 
subsidies, quotas, fees, or feed-in tariffs (Cojocaru et al., 2024).
In conclusion, the higher spatial density of salmon aquaculture farms 
and production is associated with lower biological productivity at farms 
caused by spatial disease and sea lice externalities. Higher spatial density 
can also increase negative externalities to wild salmonids, restricting the 
societal license to production growth. Both technological and regulatory 
innovation are required to facilitate further farmed salmon production 
growth, where investments in new technologies must partly be incentivized 
by appropriate regulations. In the future, we need new research-based
