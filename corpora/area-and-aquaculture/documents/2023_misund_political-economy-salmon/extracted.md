A B S T R A C T
Aquaculture production is typically located in rural coastal areas where the industry’s economic and environ-
mental impact is highly visible. Aquaculture industry is at times controversial and subject to criticism regarding
its externalities. For the industry to continue and expand, it needs support and acceptance from the public. We
empirically examine the social support yfor aquaculture production in Norway using survey data, investigating
the impact of economic as well as social factors. We ﬁnd that people living in municipalities with aquaculture
activities have a more favorable view of aquaculture production and become more positive with increasing
presence of the industry. However, people also report that the economic beneﬁts generated are not fairly
shared in the communities where the production occurs. Our results show that proximity to aquaculture activ-
ities improve social acceptance, while perceptions of how equitably the economic beneﬁts of aquaculture are
distributed within communities also play an important role.
Key words: Public expectations, salmon aquaculture, societal acceptance, survey data, value creation.
JEL codes: Q22, R11, R12.
INTRODUCTION
The economic, social, and environmental effects of aquaculture production make the pursuit of
sustainable development of the industry a complex balancing act. On the one hand, aquaculture
activities can create employment opportunities and economic activities at different stages in the
value chain, including ﬁsh feed production, supply of farming equipment and services, ﬁsh pro-
cessing, and distribution (Asche 2008; Cárdenas-Retamal, Dresdner-Cid, and Ceballos-Concha
2021). Employment and economic activities related to aquaculture are often found in rural re-
gions with limited alternatives, as many other sectors converge towards urban regions (Ceballos,
Dresdner-Cid, and Quiroga-Suazo 2018).1 On the other hand, aquaculture production may have

2019).
The public’s perception of an industry as beneﬁcial for the nation and individual communities
is crucial in maintaining societal support for its continued operation. Originally used in reference
to the mining industry, a “social license” is often conceived of as an unwritten social contract that
reﬂects the expectations and opinions of the broader community concerning the beneﬁts and im-
pacts of an industry (Moffat et al. 2016; Thomson and Boutilier 2011). Having a weak social li-
cense implies a lack of public support for industrial activities, often leading to a public call for
more stringent regulation or even a shutdown of operations. The implications may thus be severe
for the industry in question.
Different factors have been shown to inﬂuence perception of aquaculture production; these
factors include media reporting, level of knowledge, local presence of the industry in the com-
munity, food product preferences, and attributes related to the product as communicated by re-
tailers, product labels, and the like (Hynes et al. 2018; Mather and Fanning 2019; Olsen and
Osmundsen 2017). Studies also suggest that local and regional contexts inﬂuence perceptions,
with proximity to an industry in rural environments raising awareness of the industry’s negative
and positive effects along the three sustainability dimensions: economic, social, and environmen-
tal. Mazur and Curtis (2008) and Whitmarsh and Palmieri (2009) suggest that populations resid-
ing near aquaculture production sites tend to weigh the environmental, social, and economic as-
pects differently than do more urban populations. In other words, people residing in areas with
higher dependency on aquaculture production may be more positive towards the industry be-
cause of its contributions to their community in the form of employment and livelihoods (Krøvel
et al. 2019; Whitmarsh and Palmieri 2009; Whitmarsh and Wattage 2006). However, local res-
idents may also have more experience with the adverse environmental impacts of aquaculture,
which may inﬂuence their perceptions in a negative direction (Kraly, Weitzman, and Filgueira
2022).
To explore these issues of public perception and social license, we combine quantitative data
on aquaculture production and economic variables with results from an extensive survey (N p
1,183) on perceptions of aquaculture in coastal communities in Norway conducted in 2020
(Olsen, Amundsen, and Osmundsen 2023). Company-level data on aquaculture ﬁrms’ economic
performance and operations have been collected in Norway for many decades (Iversen et al.
2020), and the presence of aquaculture operations along a substantial proportion of the Norwe-
gian coast provides signiﬁcant regional variation in practices, from the location of suppliers to
processing plants and exporters (Asche 2008; Oglend, Asche, and Straume 2022). This in turn al-
lows for the exploration of heterogeneities in the public’s relationship to the aquaculture industry,
that are perceived to threaten jobs and economic activity within coastal communities—such as transferable ﬁshing quotas (Abbott,
Leonard, and Garber-Yonts 2022) or processing outside of the community (Asche, Yang, et al. 2022)—are typically regarded as
negative from a social perspective.

PUBLIC PERCEPTIONS AND THE DEVELOPMENT OF NORWEGIAN
AQUACULTURE’S ECONOMIC AND ENVIRONMENTAL PERFORMANCE
Over the last 50 years, salmon aquaculture in Norway has evolved from a small-scale, locally
owned farming sector to a more consolidated industry dominated by large and multinational
companies (Asche, Guttormsen, and Nielsen 2013; Asche et al. 2013; Sikveland, Tveterås, and
Zhang 2022; Afewerki et al. 2023; Asche, Roll, and Tveterås 2016; Misund and Nygård 2018).
Norwegian production has increased from a few thousand metric tons (mt) in 1970 to 1.6 mill
mt in 2021, with an accompanying increase in revenue and economic signiﬁcance for coastal
communities. As Norway is a small country with 5 million inhabitants, most of the production
is exported (Straume et al. 2020; Oglend, Asche, and Straume 2022). The industry’s success is due
to global demand growth, regulatory frameworks facilitating growth, and innovations that en-
hance competitiveness (Afewerki et al. 2023; Asche 2008; Garlock et al. 2020).
The aquaculture industry competes with other users of the same coastal areas, and lack of so-
cial legitimacy may be a signiﬁcant obstacle to growth in Norway (Sandersen and Kvalvik 2015)
and beyond (Anderson, Asche, and Garlock 2019; Asche, Eggert, et al. 2022). Aquaculture com-
panies use public resources such as production sites, and the public has become increasingly
aware of the industry’s negative impacts on the marine environment, especially through the me-
dia (Liu, Lien, and Asche 2016; Olsen and Osmundsen 2017; Osmundsen and Olsen 2017). En-
vironmental impacts from aquaculture have increased, and sea lice prevention on farmed and
wild salmonids is now an integral part of production growth regulation (Hersoug, Mikkelsen,
and Karlsen 2019; Misund 2019; Osmundsen, Olsen, and Thorvaldsen 2020; Hersoug 2021;
Larsen and Vormedal 2021). At the same time that aquaculture activities have adversely affected
the coastal environment, the owners of these operations have reaped extraordinary ﬁnancial re-
turns over the last decade. Consequently, coastal communities’ expectations of sharing of eco-
nomic beneﬁts from aquaculture have also increased (Sandersen and Kvalvik 2015; Bailey and
Eggereide 2020; Aanesen and Mikkelsen 2020).
For example, an asymmetric distribution of aquaculture proﬁts may contribute to less favor-
able perceptions of the industry in rural communities. The proportion of value added—calculated
as total ﬁrm revenues after purchases of goods and services—going to capital owners as opposed
to workers’ wages has increased signiﬁcantly over time (ﬁgure 2). This development is partly a
consequence of salmon farming becoming more capital intensive2 but is also due to increased out-
sourcing of operational activities to specialized companies, such as well boat companies (Iversen
et al. 2020). In addition, salmon prices are highly volatile (Asche, Misund, and Oglend 2019), caus-
ing value creation to vary from year to year. Over the last decade, extraordinary proﬁtability driven
by elevated market prices for salmon has contributed to the owners’ larger share of value added,
going from levels below or around 50% in earlier years to 70%–85% in the last ﬁve years. In sum,
local salmon farm employees have beneﬁted to a far lesser extent than have capital owners from
trends in sector proﬁtability.

As municipalities decide whether, when, and where salmon can be produced (Hersoug,
Mikkelsen, and Osmundsen 2021), the industry depends on having a good standing in the coastal
communities in which it seeks to operate, particularly since access to new locations is one of the
industry’s main challenges for increasing production (Hersoug 2021, 2022; Hersoug and Johnsen
2012; Mikkelsen, Karlsen, and Osmundsen 2019; Osmundsen et al. 2020; Young et al. 2019).
The industry could gain access to more and better production areas if there were stronger in-
centives for municipalities to prioritize aquaculture sites, which in turn can depend on public
perceptions that costs and beneﬁts are shared fairly between industry and society (Hersoug,
Mikkelsen, and Osmundsen 2021). Isaksen, Andreassen, and Robertsen (2012) show that a vast
majority of municipalities have previously viewed the industry positively but have, at the same
time, demanded economic compensation since the industry’s local economic contributions have
not met expectations.
The increasing disparities in the distribution of aquaculture production beneﬁts between in-
dustry and society have also received increased attention in the media (Olsen and Osmundsen
2017; Åm 2021) and sparked much recent debate, especially after proﬁts soared in 2016. Politi-
cians representing communities in which aquaculture operations are located have argued for a
larger share of the economic proﬁts, and, over the past decade, new measures to increase the com-
pensation of municipalities hosting aquaculture production have been introduced. For example,
income from public allocation of new and increased production licenses, sold by the government
to industry either for a ﬁxed price or by auction, is channeled into a new state-established Aqua-
culture Fund and distributed between the central government and counties/municipalities with
aquaculture production sites. In 2018, government sales of new licenses enabling production
growth generated an income of approximately NOK 4 billion (~USD 381 million), resulting in
a payment of NOK 2.7 billion (~USD 257 million) divided among 164 host municipalities and
10 counties (Directorate of Fisheries 2022). In addition, a production fee implemented in 2021
is estimated to provide aquaculture municipalities with yearly revenues of approximately NOK
500 million (~USD 48 million). However, these arrangements are still relatively new, and their
effect on public perceptions may not be easy to measure.

Importantly, the industry’s value and job creation are generated not only at the farming stage
in the sea, but also from the wider value chain (e.g., suppliers of equipment, materials, services,
and processing and distribution of ﬁsh). When examining the Norwegian aquaculture value
chain as a whole, value creation has increased signiﬁcantly over time, as shown in ﬁgure 2.3 Total
inﬂation-adjusted value added has increased from NOK 15 billion (~USD 1.4 billion) in 2004 to
around NOK 70 billion (~USD 6.7 billion) in 2019 (Richardsen et al. 2018, 2019), and value cre-
ation is actually higher in other stages of the value chain than in the farming stage. Figure 3 shows
that more than half of the value creation is in ﬁsh processing, export, distribution, and other sup-

Employment in the aquaculture value chain and other supplier sectors has ﬂuctuated between
35,000 and 39,000 full-time-equivalent jobs (ﬁgure 4). Employment in the farming stages is
around 20% of the total, while supplier sectors represent about 70% of employment and ﬁsh pro-
cessing accounts for 2%–3%. It should be noted that the employment effects of aquaculture pro-
duction on other supplier sectors may not be fully visible among the general public and politi-
cians, as this employment is often in manufacturing and service sectors companies that also
supply sectors other than aquaculture, and for which aquaculture is often not the main customer.
Salmon companies and their owners pay corporate income tax, wealth tax, employer tax on
wages, and property tax. In addition, companies have since 2017 paid fees in auctions or as a ﬁxed
price;5 for increasing the biomass prescribed by their licenses, as well as a production fee on salmon
biomass since 2021.6 As proﬁts grew substantially after 2010, corporate income tax revenues
followed suit and increased to around NOK 5 billion (~USD 476 million) out of a value added of
slightly above NOK 30 billion (~USD 2.9 billion) (ﬁgure 5). Since 2018, taxes other than corpo-
rate income taxes have become increasingly more important for capturing parts of the industry’s
proﬁts. Furthermore, the revenue from aquaculture fees to municipalities have become notewor-
thy since 2018, having reached above NOK 2 billion (~USD 190 million) in 2018 and 2020 (ﬁg-
ure 5). These revenues ﬂuctuate from year to year because of variations in available new bio-
mass license volumes sold by the government to companies. Increasing tax and aquaculture
income fees to the central and local governments may also have inﬂuenced the formation of
perceptions on the aquaculture industry among the general public.7

in Norway in 2020 by an independent survey company (Norfakta). The company used their ex-
isting panels to recruit respondents, who subsequently received an email invitation to an online
questionnaire. The research group developed the questionnaire and decided on some predeﬁned
targets: a minimum of 800 respondents in total, and a minimum number of respondents from
each Norwegian county.9

survey has a skewed distribution in favor of counties with aquaculture production, with approx-
imately 48% of the respondents living in a municipality with aquaculture production.10 The

added values for companies with production in the municipality. Since many companies will have
production in multiple municipalities, allocating company value-added values to speciﬁc munic-
ipalities using an allocation key is necessary. Company production in speciﬁc municipalities is
divided by the total company production. These ratios of municipality production to total produc-
tion are subsequently multiplied by company total value-added values and aggregated across all
municipalities, resulting in value added at the municipality level.

EMPIRICAL RESULTS
We estimate 11 empirical models for each dependent variable (general impression, equitable dis-
tribution of beneﬁts, and ﬁnancial signiﬁcance), where we test for the statistical signiﬁcance of dif-
ferent variables measured at the municipality level described above. The signs of the coefﬁcients
associated with individual- and household-level variables tend to be robust to changes in model
speciﬁcations. We provide detailed postestimation results in the online appendix.
Several municipality-level aquaculture-related variables are highly correlated (table 4).15 The
economic variables revenues, wages, or value added per capita are highly correlated. Several of the
other variables also have correlation coefﬁcients above typical threshold values (approximately
60%), implying that our regression models are prone to multicollinearity. Highly correlated var-
iables should not be included simultaneously, but prior studies do not provide theoretical or em-
pirical guidance as to which of our competing variables to include or exclude. Our approach is
one of statistically identifying/estimating relationships between municipality characteristics
and perceptions of the aquaculture industry. We believe separate model estimation and transpar-
ent presentation of estimates associated with each of the municipality level variables provide em-
pirical insights on relationships between these variables and perceptions.
DEPENDENT VARIABLE 1: GENERAL IMPRESSION OF AQUACULTURE
Table 5 presents the empirical results from models where the dependent variable is the respon-
dents’ general impression of the Norwegian salmon aquaculture industry. The focus here is on
the municipality-level variable, not the household-level variables. But for the household-level
variables, we note that the age group 30–39 years generally had a less favorable impression of
the industry than the base group of younger people. We ﬁnd less signiﬁcant differences for other
household characteristics.
People living in municipalities with a smaller population or with lower population densities
had a more favorable view of the aquaculture industry. People living in less central municipalities
as deﬁned by Statistics Norway’s index also have a more favorable view of aquaculture production
than do people in central municipalities. Respondents living in a municipality with commercial
salmon farm licenses had a signiﬁcantly more favorable impression of the industry. The percep-
tion of aquaculture production became signiﬁcantly more positive with increasing licensed farm
site capacity in the municipality (measured in tonnes MAB per inhabitant). Finally, the percep-
tion became signiﬁcantly more positive with increasing sales revenue and value added of aqua-
culture value chain companies with business addresses in the municipality.
The highest cut-point probabilities were for scores of 3 and 4 (see online appendix table A.1),
allowing us to predict that the inhabitants should have a rather average impression of the indus-
try. The probability of a negative impression (i.e., score of 1) is around 7%, while that of the most
positive impression (i.e., score of 5) is around 10%.

CONCLUSIONS
Previous research on public acceptance and social license shows different factors inﬂuencing
public support of the aquaculture industry. Even though there are variations from community
to community and from country to country, a combination of external conditions, attitudinal
factors, and individual characteristics inﬂuence public opinion on aquaculture (Weitzman,
Filgueira, and Grant 2022). Common issues in the literature on social license are which expecta-
tions and requirements apply to the industry and how the public perceives the fulﬁllment of these
expectations. For Norway, there is evidence of a fairly high level of public support for the aqua-
culture industry (Olsen, Amundsen, and Osmundsen 2023). However, there are voiced expecta-
tions, especially concerning the environmental impact and the distribution of beneﬁts, that the
industry does not fulﬁll (Hynes et al. 2018; Krøvel et al. 2019).
Our empirical model results show a more favorable impression of the industry in communi-
ties with aquaculture production. People are more positively disposed towards the industry as its
presence increases in terms of production capacity. Also, increased value creation is associated
with more favorable impressions of the industry. On the other hand, respondents in municipal-
ities with aquaculture did not have more favorable perceptions of the distribution of its economic
beneﬁts, signaling that the societal expectations from municipalities with aquaculture production
are not yet fulﬁlled by industry behavior and/or governmental regulation. Ongoing debates about
taxes, revenue distribution, and adherence to regulations16 could be contributing to this senti-
ment, which is consistent with previous research showing that communities with aquaculture pro-
duction expect more beneﬁts and revenues from facilitating industry production (Åm 2021).
Our results show that economic impacts and value creation are more visible to the public in mu-
nicipalities with aquaculture than in those that are larger or have no aquaculture, suggesting that
the sector’s social license is less established in urban communities. However, even respondents
from aquaculture municipalities rate the ﬁnancial signiﬁcance of the industry for their local com-
munity as being relatively low.
These results provide an important contribution to studies of social license, as they demon-
strate how the presence of the industry and adhering contextual factors offer a more nuanced
understanding of the many components constituting a social license. As discussed by Dare, Schir-
mer, and Vanclay (2014), it might be more fruitful to discuss the concept of a social license as
“multiple social licenses,” in contrast to one single social license as granted by “one community.”
For Norway, where aquaculture has such a broad geographical range, the industry can be seen to
have various social licenses dependent on how its impact is perceived in different local commu-
nities and society nationwide.
