### Background
This is the final report of FHF project MILJØREG (Akvaplan-niva 2023 63547.01, 15 May 2023, 502 pages). Akvaplan-niva, NIVA and Nofima compile research on environmental effects of aquaculture and of other industries in the same coastal areas, then ask how regulation could be more direct and differentiated. Project leaders are Anita Evenset and Gro Harlaug Refseth. NIVA led chapter 3, Akvaplan-niva chapter 4, Nofima chapters 5 and 6. The English cover title is Environmental impacts of aquaculture and coexisting industries (OCR on the cover reads coexcisting). The Norwegian title is Miljøkonsekvenser av akvakultur og sameksisterende næringer.

### Methods
Chapters 3 and 4 use a Quick Scoping Review: planned search strings, stated PICO questions, two-reviewer title and abstract screening, then one full-text pass. QSR does not critically appraise study quality. Search years are mainly 2010-2022. Finfish stressors are organic particles, dissolved nutrients, contaminants, escapes, disease and parasites, noise, light and artificial structure. Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout are the main species. Cod was added late and was not fully re-run in every search. Macroalgae uses a shorter literature review, not QSR. Chapter 4 adds other coastal industries, expert risk judgement and a GIS cumulative-impact feasibility case. Chapters 5 and 6 (Norwegian, with English summaries) review aquaculture, coastal-plan and water-management practice through documents, interviews and workshops.

### Main findings

**Organic waste (3.3.1).** The QSR kept 112 papers. Soft-bottom enrichment is well understood and monitored under NS 9410:2016 (B near-field, C far-field). Hard-bottom and mixed-substrate effects, and effects on vulnerable epifauna, lack standardised Norwegian methods and thresholds. Spatial extent is site-specific. Traditional non-dispersive sites show the strongest effects under cages and out to about 300 m. Dispersive or exposed sites can show a more diffuse footprint to 600-1000 m, and the knowledge-gap text cites up to 1.5 km with a risk of overlapping farm footprints. Higher-trophic and wave-driven processes are poorly known.

**Dissolved nutrients (3.3.2).** Forty-one papers (28 screened plus 13 added). Effects are site-specific and in most gradient studies limited to about 500 m. Husa et al. 2014 concluded that Hardangerfjord parameters showed little evidence of a regional impact from aquaculture. Other county studies often classified phytoplankton and littoral macroalgae as good or high. Rogaland stations in Økland et al. 2022 showed eutrophication signs (opportunistic algae, reduced kelp), which the authors treat as a mixed climate-and-nutrient story rather than a farm-only signal. ØKOKYST metrics match the literature but the programme is not designed for farm nutrient inputs. No nutrient QSR studies addressed wild fish or farmed cod. TEOTIL2 for 2021 attributes about 84 percent of anthropogenic coastal phosphorus and 60 percent of anthropogenic nitrogen to aquaculture. SINTEF estimates natural ocean input of dissolved inorganic nitrogen at 5-20 times the aquaculture input.

**Contaminants (3.3.3).** After full-text screening, 256 papers were kept. The authors assign 164 to delousing agents. In-feed compounds are mostly local but persistent (flubenzurons measured to 2 km). Bath treatments can remain toxic over several kilometres. One modelled deltamethrin case reached 32 km. A combined lab, field and model study expected lethal shrimp concentrations up to 4-5 km over 6.4 km2. Norwegian near- and far-field quality standards exist only for flubenzurones. Delousing agents are not in the traffic-light system. The authors note that heavier chemical use could help a farm gain growth via lower lice. Copper use was 1539 tonnes in 2020 and is falling. Impact is mostly local. Substitute biocides such as tralopyril lack robust marine risk assessments. Norwegian antibiotic use fell about 99 percent from 1987 to 2013. Plastics evidence is thin. Disinfectants lack discharge and marine-effect data.

**Escapes (3.3.4).** The QSR retained 237 items. Most concern Atlantic salmon. Cod and cleaner-fish research is thinner and covers mainly genetics. River monitoring of escaped salmon is in place (2021: 79 percent of rivers under 4 percent escapees, 8 percent over 10 percent). There is no equivalent programme for escaped farmed cod or cleaner fish. Escapees have been recorded across borders at scales of thousands of kilometres.

**Disease and parasites (3.3.5).** More than half of the extracted impact studies concern sea lice. Farm-driven environmental lice levels are strongly supported. Population-level mortality estimates from release-recapture work vary widely. Impact depends on more than lice counts. Farm lice pressure and wild-fish infestation do not always correlate in high-lice Norwegian production areas. Other viruses and bacteria show overall low prevalence in wild fish and few clear farm-outbreak links.

**Noise, light, structure (3.3.6-3.3.8).** Body counts are far below the chapter 3 executive-summary figure of about 20 papers each: 10 aquaculture-relevant noise papers, one environmental light paper in the QSR, 11 structure papers. Noise is judged unlikely to cause permanent injury except powerful acoustic deterrent devices on marine mammals. Light can attract invertebrates and fish. The spatial light footprint is almost unmeasured. Structures act as fish-aggregating devices. Long-term ecosystem effects and non-native stepping-stone risks remain uncertain.

**Macroalgae (3.4).** Small-scale production shows little to no negative benthic or pelagic effect in most studies. Norderhaug et al. 2021 score nutrient uptake, shading and organic matter as small (local and reversible) and alien species, genes and pathogens as large or unknown (regional and possibly irreversible). There is no routine environmental monitoring programme for kelp farms.

**Other industries and cumulative effects (ch 4).** The literature collection gave no results for direct impacts of other industries on aquaculture. The discussion adds that no peer-reviewed or grey published information on direct and indirect risks was found. Targeted yields were thin. Where shares could be compared (nutrients, pesticides, copper), aquaculture is the largest anthropogenic coastal source because of scale. Expert table 4.17 marks most external risks to farmed-fish and public health as low. Nanoplastics is the only pressure marked possible, and farms emit plastic too. Future external harm is expected mainly from climate change and ocean acidification. Combined-effects literature is limited or missing. The GIS case could not build a usable cumulative-impact model because raw data were often unavailable. GeoNorge and Marine grunnkart are named as possible bases.

**Management (ch 5-6).** Management is treated as weighing interests, not only a technical task. Discretion should remain. Municipal plans legally place farms in designated A-areas. The weakest shared gap is the chain from activity through stressor to environmental effect, including combined effects. Water-plan measures are implemented by sector authorities. Stressors named for fuller management are hard-bottom organics, escaped cleaner fish, copper, lice and delousing agents. Industry data are under-used. New species often face salmon-designed rules. New concepts that document lower impact than inshore net pens could allow growth without a larger total footprint.

### Uncertainty and limitations
QSR does not grade paper quality. Chapter 4 is a fast screening plus expert judgement. Nutrient, organic-waste and chemical footprints are site-specific. Lice population effects remain uncertain. CIA mapping is not feasible with current open data. Tables 6-1 and 6-2 are scored by judgement. Knowledge will stay incomplete. The authors say authorities must show the uncertainty they use.

### Relevance to this corpus
This is the large environment-and-coexistence text: what is known about farm effects by stressor, what other industries do to farms, and why area decisions cannot be reduced to one indicator. Inner chapters bound the executive summaries on evidence strength, spatial scale and species.
