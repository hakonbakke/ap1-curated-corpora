### Background
The Ministry of Fisheries and Coastal Affairs appointed this expert committee on 10 September 2009. The mandate was to propose new measures for enough coastal area and a new overarching area structure, so aquaculture uses space efficiently with least possible environmental impact and infection risk, and can coexist with other coastal interests. The unanimous report was delivered in Oslo on 4 February 2011, chaired by Peter Gullestad.

### Methods
The committee held 13 meetings, visited Hitra and Frøya, and met research groups and interest organisations. Part 1 reviews 40 years of industry development, coastal and fjord conditions, and likely trends over five to ten years. Part 2 discusses health, environmental effects, production losses, user conflicts, and planning and application processes. Part 3 lists measures 10.1 through 10.25. The committee says it did not have time or resources for a full consequence study of every proposal. It also left wellboat transport rules to a separate Food Safety Authority assignment, and did not re-open area-fee design after the government had chosen municipal property tax.

### Main findings
Aquaculture production is stated as rising from 531 tonnes in 1971 to about 1 million tonnes in 2010. At year-end 2010 the committee lists 966 salmon and rainbow trout licences, 785 643 tonnes MTB, 1 093 localities and about 40.2 km2 of calculated surface. Sold quantity per km2 of inshore sea is given as 12.3 tonnes nationally, 42.9 in Hordaland and 2.5 in Finnmark. Productive coastal area is called a main Norwegian advantage that is no longer a surplus. The sammendrag compares farm surface to about 80 000 km2 inside the baselines. Table 3.1 totals 76 410 km2 from Vest-Agder to Finnmark. The committee argues that area must include the water column, the bottom, environmental effects, infection between farms, and wellboat transport.

Over a ten-year horizon the area problem is said to concern mainly salmon and rainbow trout. Other species are not expected to create large area challenges. Growth potential is placed mainly in the north. The south has the highest intensity and the largest environmental problems. Short-term economic growth there is tied more to lower losses and more efficient use of existing sites than to new space.

Three linked challenges are sea lice, escapes, and production losses. Area tools can help but cannot solve them alone. A fourth challenge is weaker municipal anchoring as companies and slaughterhouses consolidate. A fifth is planning competence, rolling coastal-zone plans, and inter-municipal cooperation, because administrative borders rarely match ecology at sea.

Chapter 5 treats infection, production disease, welfare and spread. Current direction and speed are said to describe infection risk better than symmetric sea distance. Coordinated fallowing of a whole stocking zone for at least one month is proposed (each site still two months). Except a sufficient biomass cut or full infection isolation, no single measure solves lice. Wellboat transport is named as a major risk and then left aside.

Chapter 6 treats organics, chemicals, wild salmon and sea trout, marine stocks, birds and mammals. The committee adopts the Scientific Council line that lice and escaped farmed salmon are the only clearly unstabilized existential threats to wild salmon, and that measures so far are not enough. Lice and genetic effects must be acceptable before MAB increases are considered. National disagreement on coastal eutrophication is left to a Hardanger and Bokna expert group. Farm effects on seabirds are called poorly known. Cod can spawn in pens.

Chapter 7 treats production loss as a sustainability indicator. Official turnover-loss (mean 9.5 percent, 1986-2009) is called misleadingly low. Production loss averaged 23.0 percent and generation loss 25.2 percent. For 1998-2009, Trøndelag and Nordland averaged 16.2 percent, south and north about 22 percent. Dead fish plus slaughter reject are about 85 percent of loss. In 2008 the loss was 49.3 million fish, with potential first-hand income loss about 4.9 billion kroner against 16.9 billion first-hand value.

Chapter 8 maps conflicts with shipping and fairways, fisheries (including the Ryfylke saithe case), recreation and tourism, other aquaculture types, protection and biodiversity mapping, plus wind, cables, defence areas and reindeer swim routes. Physical surface conflicts in the south are described as more modest after farms moved seaward. Ecological and biological conflicts are expected to grow, especially in the north. Many municipal plans use multi-use FFFNA designations and give little real allocation.

Chapter 9 keeps municipal plans under the Planning and Building Act as the local arena. Municipal interest in hosting is described as declining. Knowledge of currents, topography and other uses is incomplete. Sámi rights and consultation can affect northward relocation.

The structural proposal has three pillars: the coast is divided into separate production areas with stocking zones, mitigating measures are steered by indicators and action rules, and industry in each area is given stronger joint responsibility. A permit should belong to one production area. Zones should fallow together for at least one month. Starting geography is the 23 areas used when assessing new licences in 2009. Natural barriers are preferred over municipal or county lines. Ideal four-zone cycling is called hard under the then locality structure, with Hardanger 2010 as an example.

Until that system exists, the committee recommends no new salmon licences and no general MAB increase. It also recommends freer closed sea farms and land-based grow-out, repeal of the 250 g smolt cap, a national current catalogue, coastal mapping, crisis localities, repeal of the 4-6 locality rule, generation-loss statistics, smolt-origin reporting, stronger monitoring of large sites, faster hatchery applications, a permanent joint industry organisation per area, state planning expectations, pilot coastal plans, an aquaculture planning guide, and evaluation of municipal property tax after production areas exist. A production-loss action rule would cut or raise regional MAB and allow environmental relocation. A future lice rule is sketched as a traffic-light principle. An escape rule would put a collective duty on the industry in a production area to remove first-generation farmed fish from important rivers when a genetic-effect indicator is exceeded.

### Uncertainty and limitations
Ideal four-zone cycling is called hard to implement quickly under the then locality structure. Zoning is important against lice but far from sufficient where currents mix water over large distances. How large a coordinated-fallowing zone must be to cut lice is called unknown. Current models need field validation. Eutrophication effects remain disputed. Farm-to-wild infection mortality is poorly documented except for lice and a Namdal furunculosis example. Coastal-cod spawning around salmon farms is still unclear. Loss-rule trigger numbers are left to politics. Lice and escape indicators are not operational. The legal basis for collective river removal is not fully investigated. Consequence notes under each measure are principle-level. Many maps and figures in the extract are images and were not visually interpreted.

### Relevance to this corpus
This is the origin text for production areas, coordinated fallowing, growth tied to area and environment, and a lice rule built on a traffic-light principle. It is also the origin text for the user-conflict and knowledge-gap claims that later coastal-planning papers keep returning to. Usable, spaced and accepted sea, not empty sea, is treated as the constraint.

### Relation to other documents
Hersoug, Mikkelsen and Osmundsen (2020) treat this report as a foundation for the later area challenge. SALT 1075 and later production-area practice sit on the same architecture.
