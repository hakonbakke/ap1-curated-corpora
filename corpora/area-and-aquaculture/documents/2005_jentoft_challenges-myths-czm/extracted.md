Coastal Zone Management must address situations that are inherently complex, di-
verse, dynamic, and risky. What management systems are best able to handle such
challenges? What ambitions are realistic for coastal zone managers? This article
reflects on Norway’s coastal zone management experience. In particular, we dwell
with the tendency to postpone, or even neglect, making the hard decisions and to
rationalize non-decision by referring to some beliefs or myths regarding the situa-
tion in the coastal zone, variously that (a) space is no limitation, (b) spatial delimi-
tation is all it takes, and (c) coastal culture will always self-heal. Finally, we argue
that risk-management may be a more fruitful/realistic management ambition than
aiming for some positively defined goals.
Keywords
coastal zone management, complexity, culture, hard choices, Norway

Introduction
In all coastal states, coastal zone management is an issue of growing importance. The
reasons for this are several: the density of people residing in the coastal zone is high and
increasing. Those who live by the sea experience a growing encroachment on both ter-
restrial and marine territories and resources, as people seek to make use of the shore and
adjacent waters for development and leisure activities. Conflicts among user-groups competing
for space are becoming more and more common. Last but not least, it is becoming
increasingly clear that we cannot continue to use the ocean as both dumpsite and pantry.

Norway is perhaps not the latest country to discover this reality, but because we
have historically been blessed with more free space per inhabitant than most countries,
these issues have not been felt to be as urgent as they have elsewhere. Aquaculture, for
instance, never met such fierce resistance in Norway as in many other countries. For the
last 20 years or so it has become one of the most prominent growth industries in Nor-
way. Affluence of sea-space is perhaps not the only reason, but it is certainly one of the
more important ones, why conflicts over space have been relatively few. When the government,
as a condition for obtaining new fish farming licenses, required municipal authorities to
develop a coastal zone management plan, a lot of municipalities discovered that they
really had to get their act together.1 For many municipalities, it did not take long to
produce a document of this kind. The quality, however, was such that many of them
soon had to be revised.
Drawing on the Norwegian example, this article reflects on some major challenges
in coastal zone management. Norway has some particular characteristics due to her northern
location, natural conditions, and cultural landscape, but also some that are experienced
in other countries as well. In this article, we contend that coastal zone managers in
Norway and elsewhere must relate to four major environmental ecological and social
characteristics: diversity, complexity, dynamics, and risk. These are inherent features
that complicate planning and decision making but, nevertheless, require a management
response. We discuss what managers can do to meet these challenges. In particular, we
describe what we see as a tendency to rationalize management non-decision by invoking
some widespread assumptions or myths regarding the situation at hand in Norway’s
coastal zone. One is that space is unlimited and hence, that CZM is not very urgent.
Another is that spatial demarcation is all there is to coastal zone planning and manage-
ment. Third is the idea that the coastal culture always adapts and therefore not some-
thing that CZ managers need to worry about. Finally in this article we discuss what
ambitions managers might realistically have. Here we argue that managers should con-
centrate on avoiding worst-case scenarios rather than planning for an ideal state of af-
fairs in the coastal zone.
Challenges
(a) Diversity relates to differentiation and variability in natural, social, and cultural con-
ditions (cf. Vallega, 2003, 18f): coastal zone managers must relate to a large number of
situations that are very different from one coastal area to the next and that require very
different responses (Ngoile & Linden, 1998). The number and type of stakeholders also
vary accordingly. For instance, in some of Norway’s coastal districts, commercial fisheries
dominate; in other districts fishing plays much less of a role. In some districts the aquac-
ulture industry is well established, whereas in other districts this industry has not yet
started. Some coastal zone areas, such as the Lofoten islands, are extremely attractive to
tourists and are now experiencing heavy pressure, whereas this affects other areas less.
The problem structure that managers must address is quite different in the north
compared to the south. In the north, for instance, the coastal zone managers must secure
grazing opportunities for reindeer herds, whereas this is something that managers in the
south, for example, in the Oslo fjord area, do not have to think of at all. In the Oslo
fjord, a central challenge is to secure the public’s rights of access to the waterfront,
which is becoming increasingly populated by urban cottage owners who buy up the
land. In the north—in the Finnmark region, for instance—the ratio between people and
coastline is such that public access to the shoreline in general is not much of an issue.2

(b) Complexity refers to the fact that in the coastal zone concerns, goals, needs, and
interests are numerous, and they are variously dense, overlapping, and interdependent.
Things interact in ways that are not always fully understood or capable of being pre-
dicted. For instance, we know that species interact, when one species feeds on another
species, and together they comprise a food chain. Also, stock composition is influenced
by natural phenomena such as large-scale oceanographic currents, sea temperatures, sa-
linity, and so on. Add human predation and other human influences, and we have an
intricate, multiscale interactive system that is not only difficult to comprehend but also
decidedly difficult to manage in any controlled fashion.
The fact that many of these resources are common pool resources makes the man-
agement process no less complicated. Collective action is hard to accomplish under such
circumstances. Resource users and other stakeholders may, therefore, not be able to sort
out their differences themselves without some external help. As John Dryzek (1990, 65)
also points out: “A contributing factor to the complexity of environmental problems is
the variety of value positions brought to them by human stakeholders.” In some situa-
tions user-groups and stakeholders, despite different value positions, may be more fortu-
nate, if their activities support one another. For instance, small-scale fisheries are largely
complementary to small-scale coastal tourism, provided that their relation is adequately
organized. The local anchoring of small-scale businesses also points to the relevance of
analyzing the formation and stock of social capital within different communities. People
may have things in common; a history and an identity they share, relationships based on
co-operation and mutual trust. If such qualities of communities and social relationships
are absent, coastal zone managers may have a more complex task at hand.3 Indeed, the
European Commission points to the “weakening of the social fabric” and “marginalization”
as important challenges for the coastal regions and CZM, and states that these are im-
portant problems to be solved (COM 2000–547, 2).
(c) Dynamics refers to fluctuation and change over time. Both eco-systems and
social systems alter, sometimes rapidly, unpredictably, irreversibly and nonlinearly, and
coastal areas are among the most dynamic natural and social environments “and one of
the most important contexts in which human activity, economy, ecology and geomor-
phology interact” (Fabbri, 1998, 52). Incidents in one part of the system may trigger
processes that spread and magnify. If one cannot plan from the premise that conditions
can be controlled and stabilized, management systems must be made to adapt to ever-
changing circumstances and moving targets. This calls for short feedback loops and
reflexivity among the parties involved, based on an ability to learn from experience.
Learning is also inherently dynamic, because knowledge must be continuously updated
as an integrated part of coastal zone management. In addition, it is not sufficient that
this kind of learning takes place at the level of the individual. New knowledge has to be
continually assimilated and acted on in organizational contexts, thus necessitating a ca-
pacity for institutional reflexivity (Cohen & Sproull, 1996; Giddens, 1990, 38ff).
Norway has experienced many crises in the fishery that paralyzed communities, and
in some instances a whole region. And every time, the crisis seems to come out of the
blue—suddenly and unexpectedly. Therefore, managers have learned that they cannot
plan on the assumption that conditions can be predicted, controlled, and stabilized. The
booming aquaculture industry has resulted in an acute demand for coastal zone planning
at a local level, but in many instances municipal authorities have not been able to pro-
duce management plans fast enough to have any impact.4 Thus, the challenge is to avoid
management systems working at full stretch because they are unable to prepare for eventualities
or respond to events quickly enough. This near-ubiquitous planning dilemma between

(d) Risk refers to the fact that coastal zone actors—including managers—have a
less than complete knowledge of alternatives and the possible outcomes of the decisions
they make. Consequently, things may go wrong, thus harming both nature and society,
and affecting both current and future generations. Coastal ecosystems are fragile, biodiversity
may be lost forever, and coastal communities could be damaged beyond repair. The
same would largely apply to the public’s access to the coastal zone. It is obviously less
difficult to go from a public to private property rights based management system, rather
than the opposite way. Once lost, the public’s access to the shoreline would for many
practical reasons be difficult to restore.
Thus if things go wrong in the coastal zone, they can go really wrong. For instance,
in Norway we now fear a similar catastrophe to what happened recently in Spain with
the “Prestige” oil tanker, as the Russians now have huge plans for shipping oil out of
the Kola Peninsula. If an oil spill of a similar magnitude should ever occur, and inci-
dents suggest that we have had several close shaves, our coastal zone would be in a
totally different situation from one day to the next. These risks are real, despite the
measures that have been introduced to reduce them.5 There are also numerous other
risks at different geographical levels that are worrisome. Science cannot always be trusted.
As Ulrich Beck contends, the origin of risk consciousness in highly industrialized soci-
eties, “came into being against a continuing barrage of scientific denial, and is still
suppressed by it” (Beck, 1992, 70).
Responses
What, then, would be an appropriate way to deal with diversity, complexity, dynamics
and risk? What should a coastal zone management regime look like? Simple, standard
fixes fit for all situations are not available. Instead, perhaps, management systems should
be appropriately diverse, complex, and dynamic, and thus tailor-made for the particular
context within which it is to operate (cf. Kooiman, 2003). Cicin-Sain and Knecht (1999,
9) stress the dynamic feature of planning: “Integrated coastal zone management can be
defined as a continuous and dynamic process by which decisions are made for sustain-
able use, development and protection of coastal and marine areas and resources.” It is
worth reflecting on what this may imply in practical terms.
We suggest that management regimes for the coastal zone should address three key
issues: (a) What knowledge is essential for planners and decision makers? (b) What
institutional designs are needed? c) How should management responsibilities be divided
among the parties involved?
Knowledge
The four challenges mentioned earlier place heavy demands on managers’ knowledge
and the kinds of information they need. Because hardly any problem in the coastal zone
is delimited by academic disciplines, coastal zone managers must tap on insights of
multiple disciplines, as well as from informal, local sources of knowledge (Hegarty,
1997). No discipline has all the analytic tools that are needed, and science does not have
all the answers. Science and practical ecological, social, and cultural knowledge may,
combined, be able to fill in most of the holes. Coastal zone managers must therefore
know how to draw on this broad expertise.

As Poul Degnbol (2003) argues, there is a need for “averaged” knowledge of low
resolution, for example, learning about how fish stocks fluctuate. Managers also need
spatially and temporally specific knowledge—“high-resolution knowledge,” in Degnbol’s
terms—regarding, for example, particular habitats (e.g., spawning grounds and biotopes).
There is a similar need regarding social data. Coastal zone management requires both
analytical and methodological skills. Managers must know the identity of the user-groups
and stakeholders, their situation and their ambitions. Coastal zone managers need data at
an aggregate level regarding, for instance, developments and trends within relevant in-
dustries, but they also need to know about concrete localities, and their particular char-
acteristics and processes. They must know which localities are suitable and available for
what purpose; which are already taken by other activities; and what plans and projects
are underway among local or external entrepreneurs.
There is also the question of how much knowledge is needed. A management sys-
tem can suffer from information overload. More information is therefore not always
better, if it is neither processed nor put to use. Obviously, managers need to understand
how ecosystems and social systems work and interact, but they can never know it all:
their rationality will always be bounded (March & Simon, 1958). Providing more knowledge
also has a cost implication. Degnbol (2003) contends that the marginal cost curve of
ecosystem knowledge acquisition is becoming increasingly steep.
Dealing with diversity, complexity, dynamics, and risk when knowledge is uncer-
tain and incomplete is certainly a challenge in itself. As Bennett argues, “one of the
challenges in coastal management today is not only more science, but how to use the
knowledge that we already have to solve recognizable problems, and how to remove
bottlenecks that hinder management problems from being solved” (Bennett, 2001, 9).
One of these hindrances is that knowledge is integral to the political process, so that
stakeholders may draw on knowledge that supports their interests, while suppressing
knowledge that goes against them. The means by which knowledge is acquired, devel-
oped, validated, and filtered in the coastal zone management process is certainly an
issue that deserves attention. Thus, knowledge is rarely neutral, but instead often a tool
of power. Who decides whose knowledge is valid? Someone may be in a position to
impose their perception of reality on others. Foucault (1977) pointed out that “reality” is
not so much represented as constructed. The question is not so much who is right but
who gets it right in the management discourse. It is not always the best argument that
wins, but the one that can be phrased most forcefully, backed up by authority and muscle.
Incentives
The second question is basically one of institutional tools, incentives, and mechanisms.
For instance, how should rules and regulations be phrased and presented, and how should
they be implemented and enforced? What incentives are the most effective? How many
levels of jurisdiction should there be, and which management functions are better car-
ried out at a local level, rather than a central level—and vice versa. The issue here is
partly one of effectiveness: how close to or distant from the scene where impacts are
felt should managers and decision makers be located? Being close to the scene is some-
times necessary, but in other instances—as when general administrative principles are
involved—distance may be more of an advantage.
Here, the principle of “subsidiarity” is relevant, stating that management tasks should
be handled by the organization whose level most closely corresponds to the matter con-
cerned (cf. McCay & Jentoft, 1996). Determining what this means in real situations is

no easy task. The issue is complicated by the fact that the “lowest possible organization”
is not stated once and for all, but depends, among other things, on which institutions
exist or may be created at particular levels. In many instances, mismanagement is caused
by the absence of an institution or institutions at a level that is best suited to handle
management responsibilities. This is both a matter of sensitivity to spatial diversity, and
of responsiveness to complexity, both of which may be encountered at different levels
of administration. Sometimes a rapid response is essential, at other times things can
wait. Again, the question is, how far is a particular decision located from the problems it
needs to solve?
Coastal zone management should not, of course, take the existing institutional order
as a given. Institutional formation and improvement is partly what coastal zone manage-
ment is about. Thus, when Cicin-Sain and Knecht (1999) appeal for a more integrated
and holistic approach to coastal zone management, it requires an institutional response.
Then, institutional fragmentation becomes a handicap; rather, institutions must have broad
and multiple functions. They must also be built on partnerships and organizational net-
works. This is certainly needed in the case of Norway, where coastal zone planning
authority is primarily vested with the municipal authorities, but in reality their powers
are severely restricted by sector authorities in areas such as the environment, fisheries,
farming, harbor maintenance, defense, veterinary, and similar matters, who may veto
municipal management plans if they find that these conflict with their own particular
mandates. Unless the authorities involved can reach some agreement, a process of arbi-
tration may be triggered, which can be time-consuming and antagonizing, and which
may disrupt the local motivation for coastal zone management (Bennett, 2000).
In Norway, sector agencies operate on the basis of laws and regulations that cover
their functionally defined mandates. In principle, this legislation6 takes precedence over
the Planning and Building Act, which forms the basis for municipal government’s coastal
zone management activities. This may also be perceived as a clash between two management
approaches, influenced by functional and territorial responsibilities (cf. Friedmann &
Weaver, 1979). In Norway, the lex specialis principle generally causes the latter to yield
when the two collide. In practical terms, this means that the local authorities must ca-
pitulate, although municipalities have, in some instances, successfully appealed to the
Ministry of the Environment for an issue to be decided in their favor.
Responsibilities
The third question concerning the response to challenges in the coastal zone also relates
to institutions. Coastal zone management involves a range of more or less well-orga-
nized public and private interests, which interact in a structured fashion. We may think
of society as comprised of three basic institutions: the state (central and local), the mar-
ket, and civil society. The three institutions represent various domains, they work ac-
cording to dissimilar logics and they are guided by different values. The question, then,
is which of these institutions should be mobilized in the management of the coastal
zone. Jan Kooiman (1993) argues that modern society is too complex, too diverse, and
too dynamic to be governed by only one of these institutions. Rather, governance must
invoke all of them, in a collaborative and interactive process. In coastal zone manage-
ment we need partnership arrangements that draw on the resources, capabilities, and
capacities of all three institutions (cf. Pierre, 1998). In particular, there is a need to
involve coastal communities. As Clark emphasizes, “empowering communities always
works better than commanding them” (Clark, 1998, 76).

One challenge facing partnerships is how to bridge their respective working prin-
ciples and cultures. Partnerships raise tricky issues of participatory democracy, represen-
tation, and communication (cf. McCreary et al., 2001; Gallagher et al., 2003). They
prompt questions of transparency and accountability. What is sometimes standard proce-
dure for government agencies often goes against what is common practice in the private
sector—and vice versa. According to Rhodes, “There is an obvious conflict between the
tenets of accountability in a representative democracy and participation in networks which
can be open without being formally accountable.” (Rhodes, 1997, 58). Involving stake-
holders in public–private coastal zone management partnerships unquestionably activates
such a conflict. Thus, when the state, market, and civil society have different working
principles, a modus vivendi may be hard to attain.
One should not be oblivious to the eventuality that some of the most urgent issues
in the coastal zone are, overall, simply too diverse, complex, dynamic, and risky to
sustain truly collaborative public–private partnerships. Instead, they require the correc-
tive hand of the state or of a regional or municipal authority. Participatory democracy
is sometimes too cumbersome, and at other times too hampered by conflicts and
political logrolling to be effective. This is the classic dilemma between “internal de-
mocracy and external effectiveness,” as stated by Dahl and Tufte (1973). Perhaps, there-
fore, hopes are too high for a truly participatory process in all cases. Stakeholders may
be too numerous and conflicts too intricate for participation and bottom-up decision
making.
Ingenuity in crafting partnership arrangements needs to be mobilized, which might
compensate for some of the inherent obstacles to participatory democracy. Good gover-
nance is not only about efficiency but also about legitimacy. Participatory democracy is
one way to ensure legitimacy—more so than efficiency. Both are necessary in coastal
zone management, which is therefore bound to be an ongoing, time-consuming process,
where the ideal is not always feasible. After all, as Cicin-Sain and Knecht note, concern-
ing stakeholders:
Including their input early in the program development can, of course, com-
plicate and delay the process, but this a risk that generally must be taken.
Failure to involve (. . . ) user-groups early in the process carries a much
greater risk—the risk that when brought into the process later, they will
oppose the entire procedure and the ICM [Integrated Coastal Management—
our addition] that has been produced. (Cicin-Sain & Knecht, 1999, 211)
It may well be necessary to make partnerships formal, with specified roles, responsibili-
ties, and procedures (Karlsen, 2001). But given our comments on the diversity, com-
plexity, dynamics, and risks of the coastal zone, such responsibilities and procedures
would also need to be reworked on a continual basis. These factors require institutional
flexibility and experimentation (Clark, 1997), as well as the ability to reflect and to
learn interactively from mutual experience (Jentoft et al., 1999; see also Fletcher, 2003).
Myths
The diversity, complexity, dynamics, and risk that coastal zone managers must address
may seem overwhelming. Accounting for all of them in an informed, consistent, and
comprehensive manner is more than many managers feel they can do. It is therefore
quite understandable that they would try to make things more manageable for them-

selves. In the following we describe one ”technique” frequently used in Norwegian coastal
zone management. CZ managers, and the agencies they work for, often invoke three
common “images” (Kooiman, 2003), which are basically myths. As myths they convey
a message—something myths always do (cf. Barthes, 1957)—legitimizing particular ac-
tions, sometimes inaction, which is rarely politically neutral but, deliberately or not, in
someone’s special interest. They tend to be taken for granted by managers and stake-
holders alike, because the social “realities” they allude to appear as natural.7 The first
myth says that “space is unlimited,” the second that “space is the key,” and the third
that “coastal culture takes care of itself.”
Space Is Unlimited
There are those who walk Norway from north to south, just to prove themselves. How-
ever, they never walk on the beach—for reasons that are easy to appreciate. Not many
coastal states have a longer coastline than Norway. The Norwegian State Mapping Au-
thority (Statens Kartverk) has estimated the coastline—islands and fjords included—to
be 83,000 kilometers: one and a half times the length of the equator (Sandbæk, 2003).8
Divided per capita, Norwegians have more coastlines to play on than most other people.
Thus, if you look down from a plane on a clear day on the land- and seascape, you
cannot avoid thinking that space is almost limitless and that there should be enough
room for all kinds of activities, both industrial and recreational. Not only is the distance
between communities and houses extensive, it is also hard to spot any activity at sea.
For instance, it is hard to see any traces of the booming aquaculture industry in terms of
fish pens in the ocean: there are just a few dots here and there. However, the way in
which the coastal zone is used and valued cannot be discerned from high up. It can only
be detected on the ground, out at sea, and below the surface of the water. Any rational
approach to coastal zone management would require a detailed mapping of resources,
activities, and use. Without it, one might easily get the impression that Norway’s coastal
zone is nothing more than a tabula rasa. When this is the image, there is little urgency,
coastal zone management can wait, and the hard decisions can be postponed. However,
if we look more closely, from the ground rather than from the air, the coastal zone is
quite crowded as it is, and almost any conceivable new development will interfere with
other uses—human or non-human. For instance, with the demands for security zones
and alternative locations for aquaculture installations, this industry has become quite
space consuming.9
The coastal zone is now regarded as an area where the nation’s future economic
growth will be located. The potential of various forms of aquaculture and marine bio-
technology seems huge. How these new activities can be integrated into a coastal zone
that is already occupied is, however, not much of an issue in the public debate. It is
generally assumed that new activities can simply be added to existing ones. When the
areas are already taken, however, development could easily become a zero-sum game.
Construction would pre-suppose destruction. Coastal zone managers may choose to turn
a blind eye to this dilemma, but only as long as those who have to bear the conse-
quences do not voice their protests. User conflicts cannot be deposited under the carpet
for long. A detailed mapping of all existing activities and ongoing (but often sporadic or
seasonal) usage might be a first step in addressing potential conflicts in a non-confronta-
tional, preemptive manner. It would be one way of discerning who the users and stake-
holders are, what stakes they hold, and what risks are involved.10

Space Is All
Coastal zone management can easily become focused on spatial delimitation alone. Ac-
cording to the Planning and Building Act of 1985, this is indeed also its primary instru-
ment. The expectation is that if we can only avoid simultaneously overlapping usages,
by separating them using clear boundaries, conflicts will be eliminated and peaceful co-
existence will reign. For this reason, the demarcation of space becomes a central concern
of coastal zone management (Visser, 2004).
Although the spatial delimitation of coastal activities is important, a territorial ap-
proach is hardly sufficient. Institutions and other mechanisms that facilitate conflict reso-
lution, where stakeholders have an opportunity to communicate and negotiate, are also
essential. But, even more importantly, coastal zone management must aim to build cooperative
relations, networks, and trust among stakeholders. As with countries, clear borders help to
reduce conflicts between them, but integration, collaboration, and mutual exchange also
help. It is a known fact that countries that are interdependent are less likely to go to war.
The war metaphor is not all that far-fetched because the coastal zone in Norway has
frequently been referred to as a battle zone.
A conflict between two parties easily develops when the relation between them is as
follows: if A does X, B cannot do Y. The politics in this situation would be that one who
sides with B would seek to oppose A. A symbiotic relationship looks different: if A does
X, B can do Y. In this situation, one who sides with B would also support A, and an
alliance between the two parties would become relatively easy to establish. Coastal zone
management will be less of an exercise in conflict resolution if it is directed at creating
symbioses, for instance between capture fisheries, aquaculture, and tourism. Although
the potential clearly exists, Norwegian fisheries and aquaculture, managements are not
at all targeted at creating such symbioses; rather, they are framed within a sector, rather
than a territorial, perspective. The same is the case for all other industries that operate in
the coastal zone. For coastal zone management, this calls for greater horizontal integra-
tion of institutional structures.11
Similarly, coastal zone management seems to have only peaceful co-existence as its
main objective: the ambition to create constructive links of mutual benefit between vari-
ous activities and usages is rare. For this situation to change, one would first of all need
to regard coastal zone activities as a socioeconomic system—rather than an uncoordi-
nated aggregate of individual parts—with the untapped potential of building networks,
joint ventures, cooperative relations, and interactive learning. As to the latter, James
Wilson maintains:
The complexity of these systems [i.e., marine fisheries—our addition]—their
size, spatial distributions, multiple scales, large number of components, con-
tinuous change, and other factors—create circumstances in which no one
individual or group can hope to adequately address the learning problem.
The problem is a collective problem and, as such, is dependent on social
organization and processes. (Wilson, 2002, 340–341)
All this would require an effort in the field of inter-organizational innovation, with coastal
zone managers assuming a facilitating and more proactive, entrepreneurial role, rather
than the bureaucratic, reactive, and regulative role that they have in Norway today. This
would require not only the skills of a geographer, but also those of a sociologist and
social psychologist, both of whom have a trained eye for interpersonal and organizational
relationships.

Microbiologists are currently exploring the coastal zone for marine organisms, with
interesting genes and enzymes, which they believe could represent an enormous economic
potential. They call what they do “bio-prospecting.” We believe there is a social equiva-
lent to such an exercise—“socio-prospecting.” Organizational and institutional mecha-
nisms, which may help bring relations among user-groups from a conflictive to a coop-
erative and symbiotic relationship, are probably already out there, and only need to be
discovered—or rediscovered. For example, co-operatives may well experience a renais-
sance in the new area of marine biotech industries, where it would be important to find
ways of building bridges and bonds between new and existing activities. Symbioses are
often better handled if they are internalized into one organization or within a formalized
partnership. This is expressed, for instance, in Oliver Williamson’s now classic argu-
ment concerning markets, hierarchies, and transaction costs (Williamson, 1975).
Culture Self-Heals
A standard perception is as follows: as long as people live on the coast, they will always
have a coastal culture. Coastal culture is an expression of people’s lives, life forms,
socioeconomic activities, worldviews, and aspirations, and will change as material con-
ditions change. The coastal culture is nothing more than the sediment of coastal indus-
tries. Thus, fisheries and maritime communities will constantly produce a kind of culture
that is distinct from the kind of culture that one would find in, for instance, agrarian
communities. Consequently, culture is something coastal zone managers do not have to
worry about. They can simply leave it alone, expecting that it will take care of itself. As
long as fish stocks and marine eco-systems are sustained, the rest will fall into place. If,
for instance, management authorities make sure that the cod and the herring stay healthy,
then the fishing industry will survive, the communities will continue to exist, and coastal
culture will blossom accordingly. Coastal culture is here the final and most dependent
variable in the causal chain.
Obviously, this way of reasoning cannot easily be dismissed. It goes without saying
that if marine resources are decimated, many coastal communities will be almost unin-
habitable as people will not be able to sustain themselves there. Culture, as a way of
life, will not have a material basis, and will not survive other than as a museum relic.
But what if the causal chain works the opposite way? What if coastal culture is the first
and not the last element in the chain, that culture is the basic condition for it all? Then,
coastal zone managers could not assume that coastal culture would take care of itself,
and therefore ignore it. Instead, culture would become a premise, not an outcome; it is
where coastal zone management would start, not where it ends. This way of looking at
culture is, in a way, turning Karl Marx on his head, but others have done that before.
Therefore, this alternative perception, of culture as a “basis” rather than a “super-structure,”
is not all that original. But can it be substantiated with regard to the coastal zone? Let us
try to do so.
Small-scale fisheries employing traditional gear are generally less of a threat to
coastal ecosystems than the highly powered, large-scale industrial fishery. For this and
other reasons, trawlers in Norway are banned inside the twelve-mile limit. The small-
scale fleet certainly needs fish in the ocean but it also depends on the communities it
comes from, the support it gets from its home base, and the skills and knowledge har-
bored by these communities.12 Vessels need experienced crew, which means a crew that
knows more than the handiwork. To be an experienced fisher also means to carry cer-
tain standards for proper conduct at sea and on land. Recruiting a broadly experienced

crew would scarcely be possible unless fishing communities were maintained with what
they possess in the way of knowledge, expertise, values, attitudes, reason, morality,
memory, meaning, identity, and the like—in short, culture.
A hot issue in Norwegian fisheries today is the fishers’ under-reporting of catches;
in addition, the number of violations of regulations that are detected and brought to
court is increasing. The government’s response is to intensify inspections onboard and
ashore, and increase the level of sanctions (cf. Jentoft & Mikalsen, 2004). However, rule
violation on the part of fishers is also an ethical concern. Cheating is, by any standards,
morally wrong. If fishers do not have a sense of committing a crime when they know-
ingly breaking the rules, any management system will be in trouble.
If norms of morality, such as integrity and honesty, are necessary conditions for
fisheries management adherence, the question is: Where do people originally acquire
their moral standards? It is certainly not something that an individual adopts in the
market place or acquires from the state. Rather, such cultural norms are something he or
she “uploads” within civil society: within the family, the community, in church, in school,
and so on—and then ”downloads” when appearing in the market and in public settings,
as well as when responding to regulatory regimes (cf. Wolfe, 1989). If these civil society
institutions fail, the management system will be in jeopardy. In that case enforcement
will be a very costly affair, as fishers have to be threatened and forced to respect the
rules. For coastal zone management it is therefore essential that these institutions should
not be allowed to degenerate. On the contrary, they should be supported. However, this
is currently not within the armory of Norwegian fisheries authorities when they try to
promote lawful behavior among fishers. As Howard and Pinder (2003, 61) comment:
“Governments seem unable to devise systems to protect the heritage of ways of life,”
and we would add that the development of “policy handles” to enhance coastal culture
is indeed a difficult task. But at the same time one can hardly expect that these institu-
tions, and the important functions they have, should be self-sustained. Consequently,
they need to exist inside and not outside the realm of coastal management. This form of
reasoning, which puts culture at the start and not at the end of the causal chain, is also
in accordance with the so-called embeddedness thesis in economic sociology. This term
was originally coined by Polanyi (1944) and later developed by Granovetter (1985) and
Barber (1995)—the latter arguing that all economies are culturally embedded, and that
culture should therefore have an independent position in the analysis.
Ambitions
One can hardly avoid concluding that coastal zone management must be among the
more complicated things in life, particularly if the objective is to pursue a holistic and
integrated approach, and if one is determined that developments should go precisely
according to plan. We may also conclude that coastal zone management is a highly
complex and dynamic process. Managers must have an eye for many things at the same
time: they are constantly confronted with dilemmas and hard choices, and they must
always consider the possibility of hazardous outcomes. It is an illusion to expect that
management can be a strictly rational exercise. It is unrealistic to think that managers
could have a full overview and control of what goes on in the coastal zone at all times.
They can hardly avoid being reactive, rather than proactive—at least for some of the
time. And they will not try to make the job any more complicated than it has to be:
rationalizing their actions, for instance, by relying on simplistic assumptions, such as the
three myths sketched herein.

From the manager’s perspective, one way of handling diversity, complexity, dynamics,
and risk is to reduce the ambition to control them. Maybe it is wise, after all, to concen-
trate less on what, in an ideal world, we should like to see taking place in the coastal
zone, and more on what we would not want to happen under any circumstances. The
best-case scenario is most likely the one we shall never experience, as is the case with
most Utopias. The worst-case scenario, then, is perhaps more realistic. Thus, as Ottar
Brox (1988) has urged in the case of Norway, management goals should be defined in
negative rather than positive terms. For instance, negative goals could be: avoidance of
major oil spills; preventing further marginalization of coastal communities; not turning
the coastal zone into a stakeholders’ battle zone; or not ruining the democratic decision-
making process. Maybe this would also make it easier for user-groups, stakeholders, and
decision-makers to agree on what coastal zone management should aim for—or at least
what they should aim to avoid. Considering the sheer number of interested parties con-
cerned with different and conflicting claims and ambitions, this in itself is not going to
be an easy process.
However, as Cyert and March (1963) argued in a now classic treatise, ambitions are
not stable, but tend to vary with actual performance. Disappointments often reduce am-
bitions, whereas successes cause them to rise. A sensible strategy would therefore be to
begin in a modest fashion, making it possible for ambitions to be adapted as results
emerge. Again, this calls for a negative goal approach, at least to begin with. After all,
coastal zone management in Norway is in its initial phase. It is a recent phenomenon,
no more than 20 years old, and still a task that many coastal municipalities have just
commenced. Thus, as in other countries, coastal zone management in Norway is in an
ongoing learning phase. In some municipalities, it has taken many years to develop a
management plan, as agreement among the affected stakeholders has been impossible to
attain. We know of municipalities where setbacks seem to have caused them to give up.
They may have decided that the best strategy was not to have a plan; that is, to let
things evolve and address them on a case-by-case basis, as necessary.
Given the complicated challenges confronted by coastal zone management, this is
not surprising. There is, however, a considerable urgency involved. The coastal zone is
a growth zone. This is where Norway’s largest economic potential is supposedly lo-
cated, what we shall need to rely on when the oil era is over. Non-decision in coastal
zone management can hardly be a viable strategy in the short term, and certainly not in
the long run. There are also risks involved in such a strategy. When managers attempt to
make their situation more simple, for the purposes of manageability, they tend to forget
the complexity of the issue involved, which, according to Mol and Law, “may mean

Thus, coastal municipalities find themselves in a dilemma: they can afford to be

able to create the opportunities needed to avoid their coastal space turning into a battle-
zone where the winner, whoever this proves to be, takes all, once and for all. The stakes
are simply too numerous and too high.
