### Background
Jentoft and Buanes reflect on Norwegian coastal-zone management. The question is which management systems can handle situations that are diverse, complex, dynamic and risky, and which ambitions are realistic. They argue that managers often postpone hard choices and rationalise non-decision with three myths: space is unlimited, spatial delimitation is all it takes, and coastal culture will always self-heal. They prefer risk-management (avoiding worst cases) over aiming for an ideal coastal state.

### Methods
This is a conceptual essay on Norway's coastal-zone experience, not a new empirical survey. It draws on planning law (Planning and Building Act of 1985), earlier work by the authors, and international CZM literature. An earlier version was given at a 2003 Stockholm conference. Received 28 October 2003, accepted 26 October 2004. Coastal Management 33:151-165.

### Main findings
Norway has historically had more free space per inhabitant than most countries, and aquaculture met less fierce resistance than elsewhere. Affluence of sea-space is named as one reason conflicts over space were relatively few. When government required a coastal-zone plan as a condition for new fish-farming licences, many municipalities produced documents quickly and then had to revise them.

Four inherent features complicate planning: diversity (north versus south, fisheries versus tourism versus aquaculture, reindeer in the north, public access in the Oslofjord), complexity (overlapping goals and common-pool resources), dynamics (systems change, aquaculture demand outran municipal plans), and risk (incomplete knowledge, irreversible harm, oil-shipping examples).

Myth 1, space is unlimited: the coastline is given as 83 000 km. From the air the zone looks empty and pens are hard to see. On the ground it is crowded. Security zones and alternative locations make aquaculture space-consuming. New activity is often assumed to be addable. When areas are taken, development can become a zero-sum game.

Myth 2, space is all: the 1985 Act makes spatial delimitation the primary instrument. Clear boundaries do not replace institutions, negotiation and cooperative links. Sector management of fisheries and aquaculture is not aimed at creating symbioses with tourism.

Myth 3, coastal culture takes care of itself: small-scale fisheries depend on community knowledge and moral standards. Culture is not self-sustaining and sits outside the usual fisheries-authority toolbox.

The authors argue that managers should concentrate on avoiding worst-case scenarios rather than planning an ideal state.

### Uncertainty and limitations
Essay, not a measurement of how much space is occupied or how often the three myths appear in named municipal plans. Figures in the extract were not read as data. DOI on the Taylor and Francis landing page is truncated in one header line. The full DOI on the article page is 10.1080/08920750590917567.

### Relevance to this corpus
This is the named argument that empty-looking sea is a management myth, and that pens plus security zones occupy more than they appear to. It is earlier than Gullestad 2011 and Hersoug 2020 on the same visual-versus-usable gap.

### Relation to other documents
Sandersen and Kvalvik (2014) cite this paper on municipal plans and sector objections. Read with Hersoug 2020 and Sand 2025 on physical versus sanitary occupation.
