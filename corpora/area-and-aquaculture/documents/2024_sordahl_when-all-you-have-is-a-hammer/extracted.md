Abstract
This paper critically examines the evolution of integration as a concept in coastal zone planning through an analysis of 
Norwegian municipal coastal zone plans spanning a 35-year period. By drawing on the framework of governmentality, 
we show how the coastal zone and the activities taking place there are being subject to problematizations, rationaliza­
tions and technologies for promoting integration. This illustrates how previous spatial dispositions themselves become 
problematized at later points, giving rise to new rationalizations and new technologies of governing, revealing a dynamic 
redefinition of integration in response to complex coastal governance challenges. What integration entails is not a given. 
Instead, it’s a fluid concept, evolving over time in meaning and intent. The paper also shows how ambitions for integrated 
or comprehensive planning can obscure the purpose of planning and increase complexity. With integration being framed 
as an undisputed answer to conflicts in the coastal zone, the difficulty of managing the multitude of coastal activities does 
not lead to a critical discussion on whether ‘integration’ is a fruitful ambition. Instead, it becomes an argument for yet 
more integrative efforts.

Analytical and methodological approach
Approaches to integration
There is a diversity of approaches to analysing integration 
in marine and coastal settings. Although the approaches dif­
fer, the integration dimensions are mainly the same. The dif­
ferent understandings and conceptualizations of integration 
in coastal and marine environments hints at the multiplicity 
of what integration is. There is confusion and uncertainty 
about what integration means, as well as how we go about 
implementing it (Saunders et al. 2019). Integrative mecha­
nisms, such as MSP, are dynamic and malleable concepts 
with content that are subject to different framings– framings 
that can further particular interests and ultimately reduce 
their integrative potential (Flannery and McAteer 2020; 
Sørdahl 2023). This is despite integrative efforts having 
been made in a marine and coastal setting since the 1970s 
(Kenchington and Crawford 1993). Integration is therefore 
not necessarily something new. Instead, it is our assumption 
that governance concepts such as ‘integration’ are dynamic 
concepts that are created and acted out in particular con­
texts, and recreated over time, taking on new forms (Sørdahl 
2023).
In our study we have categorised the different integration 
tools and practices based on earlier studies, where we dis­
tinguish between four dimensions of integration (Table 1): 
political/administrative (integration horizontally/vertically 
between administrative boundaries and of processes), geo­
graphic (integration of land-sea, and larger coastal areas), 
participation (integration of relevant parties), and knowl­
edge integration (integration of different knowledge types) 
(Douvere and Ehler 2009; Kidd 2013; Portman 2016; Port­
man et al. 2012; Saunders et al. 2019). The dimensions are 
not mutually exclusive and may overlap in practice, but they 
address different concerns in coastal zone planning that we 
expect to find in the empirical analysis of Norwegian coastal 
zone planning framing and practice.

nalities these practices contain and the ways in which they 
can shape the conduct of individuals (Barnett et al. 2008; 
Dean 2010; Stenson 2008). These ‘regimes of practices’ are 
manifestations of routinised actions that involve “the pro­
duction of truth and knowledge, comprise multiple forms 
of practical, technical and calculative rationality, and are 
subject to programmes for their reform” (Dean 2010, p. 28). 
The aim of governmentality however, is not to uncover to 
what degree these regimes and their purported ‘truths’ are 
valid, but rather it is to uncover how they are constructed 
and to ‘denaturalize’ them (Bevir 2010; McKee 2009). To 
understand these regimes, it is necessary to analyse the 
mentalities associated with them and the organised practices 
they contain. This requires us to distinguish between three 
separate but interwoven processes: problematisation, ratio­
nalisation, and the use of technologies for the operationali­
sation of identified solutions (Dean 2010).
Governance is a problematising activity, whereby an 
observed situation is measured against an ideal (Rose 
and Miller 1992). This act of problematisation defines the 
objects of governing, the ways in which those objects are to 
be governed, and by whom (Dean 2010; Flannery and McA­
teer 2020). In our case, the lack of integration in coastal 
zone planning is one such problematisation. For these prob­
lematisations to be accepted, they need to be rationalised 
through appeals to moral, scientific or ethical notions, and 
therefore justify the need for governance interventions 
based on the problematisations (Flannery and McAteer 
2020; Rose and Miller 1992). The rationalities substantiate 
the need for action prescribed in the problematisation by 
“rendering certain states of affairs problematic and provid­
ing solution to such problems” (Dean 2009). Through this 
process, not only are solutions provided, but other alterna­
tives are closed off, rendering the proposed solutions not 
just an option, but the option. In our case, the rationalisation 
could, for example, include the need to address the demo­
cratic challenges of limited stakeholder participation. Prob­
lematisation and rationalisation are discursive practices. To 
have an impact on practical governance they must be opera­
tionalised by prescribing governance technologies, such as 
programmes, calculations, techniques and procedures (Rose 
and Miller 1992). In the context of planning, examples of 
technologies for integration include the use of maps, impact 
assessments and public hearing procedures. The processes 
of problematisation, rationalisation and technologies must 
not be seen as linear processes, but rather as circular pro­
cesses where technologies created for a given purpose can 
later be problematised themselves (Flannery and McAteer 
2020). Further, they must be studied in unison as it is not 
possible to study these technologies without attention to the 
political rationality surrounding them (Lemke 2002).
The governmentality framework contributes to showing 
how certain ends and means become embedded as truths, 
and how certain actions come to be viewed as rational solu­
tions and actions to an observed challenge (Flannery and 
McAteer 2020). Importantly, it allows for seeing these 
rationalities as historically contingent, representing “partic­
ular responses, to particular problems, at particular times” 
(McKee 2009, p. 468). In this view, governance becomes 
a practical activity that can be “studied, historicized and 
specified at the level of rationalities, programmes, tech­
niques and subjectivities which underpin it and give it form 
an effects” (Walters 2012, p. 2).
We apply the governmentality framework to analyse the 
stated need for a more holistic or integrated coastal zone 
planning. By studying how integration in Norwegian coastal 
zone planning is problematised, rationalised and operation­
alised through technologies, we reveal how different men­
talities of governing are created and changed. This approach 
makes it possible to understand how objectives for more 
integrated planning are translated downwards through hier­
archical management levels, how they change over time and 
across different plans, and how new regimes of practices are 
established, building on existing ones. It also allows for a 
discussion of the discursive practice towards an integrated 
governance of the coastal zone. This approach places the 
paper within the growing ‘critical turn’ in research on gover­
nance of marine and coastal areas (Flannery et al. 2020), and 
adds to other scholars who have utilized related approaches 
to the study of issues such as framing, power and ontologi­
cal politics in the marine context (Boucquey et al. 2016; 
Kelly et al. 2018; Smith 2015; Tafon 2018, 2019; Tafon et 
al. 2019). A governmentality-influenced lens has also been 
used to study practices and mentalities in other areas related 
to environmental governance, such as climate change, con­
ceptualized as “environmentality” or “green governmental­
ity” (Death 2013; Lovell and MacKenzie 2011; Luke 1995; 
Rutherford 2007, 2017; Rutland and Aylett 2008; Stripple 
and Bulkeley 2013), to which this study relates and con­
tributes to.

Norwegian policy on coastal zone planning
Land use planning is regulated by the Planning and Build­
ing Act (PBA), where the main planning authority is the 
local municipality. The planning part of the Planning and 
Building Act is a procedural act, where the purpose of the 
act is to facilitate information sharing, coordination and dia­
logue between societal interests and levels of government 
(Oslo Economics 2021). Hence, integration is the object, or 
answer to governing challenges.
Although there is a long history of planning for the ter­
restrial area in Norway, coastal zone planning is more 
recent. It was first introduced with a new PBA in 1985, ini­
tially just for harbour areas, but was extended in 1989 to 
the entire coastal area out as far as the baseline(Plan- og 
bygningslov, 1985)3. In the current PBA of 2008, it was 
extended to one nautical mile beyond the baseline (Hersoug 
and Johnsen 2012). This was done to harmonize the plan­
ning jurisdiction with the jurisdiction of the European water 
framework directive. (Hauge and Stokke 2021). This can in 
itself be seen as an integration mechanism. The authority 
of the municipality is limited by national sector agencies, 
which have the power to object to proposed dispositions in

Integration in municipal planning practice– 
a study of Tromsø
The above overview provides an example of how ‘integra­
tive concepts’ have been present in planning regulations and 
legislation for decades. This is consistent with the papers’ 
conceptual starting point that ‘integration’ is not new, nor 
static, but rather a malleable concept whose content changes 
with the political and administrative climate. In this section 
we analyse how the framings are translated into coastal zone 
planning practice, analysing spatial plans in Tromsø munic­
ipality. The NVIVO mapping and the interviews reveal how 
the need for integration in spatial planning of coastal areas 
are problematised and rationalised in five plans in Tromsø, 
along with the technologies used. The first three plans 
(1991, 2000 and 2008) were made for Tromsø municipality 
only, although the first and third also included land areas, 
while the second only pertained to the sea area. The lat­
ter two (2015 and 2022) were inter-municipal coastal zone 
plans, including five and three municipalities respectively, 
and only pertaining to the sea area.

Tromsø municipality over a 35-year period reveals a mix

strongly mobilised participation, with several consultation 
meetings being held in both the municipal centres and rural 
areas (Informant 9). Participation was framed as being vital 
for gathering knowledge as well as ensuring local legiti­
macy. The use of knowledge was expanded through a more 
in-depth impact assessment and risk and vulnerability anal­
ysis methodology, including both scientific knowledge and 
local knowledge that had been gathered and mapped. This 
plan was the first planning process after the adoption of the 
2008 PBA, an act which stressed sustainable development 
as the overarching goal of planning, the need for an ecosys­
tem-based approach, and the importance of inter-municipal 
cooperation. The 2015 plan thus clearly follows up on these 
new signals from the national level.
The latest inter-municipal coastal zone plan was approved 
in 2022, this time involving three municipalities5. The plan 
largely carried over many of the same problematisations and 
rationalisations that had been expressed in the 2015 plan 
(Tromsø kommune 2022). The need for inter-municipal 
planning was rationalised through appeals for ‘good gover­
nance’, where planning across municipal borders provides 
a regional perspective allowing for ecosystem-based man­
agement and ensuring predictability for users of the coastal 
zone. There was also a focus on the time frame and having 
an efficient planning process. The land-sea interface was 
explicitly left to the individual municipality and was not 
part of the plan. Unlike the 2015 plan, the 2022 planning 
process was run by the municipalities themselves instead of 
a contracted consultancy firm. In the plan, it is emphasised 
that it is “crucial for the project’s success that the municipal 
councils have ownership of the planning work and partici­
pate in the public meetings that are organised”. According 
to interviews, this ‘municipal led’ process improved local 
participation and understanding of local issues, visible 
through the increased amount of feedback received dur­

2022 plan emphasised the ever-increasing complexity and 
challenges of managing the coastal zone, and stressed the

The above points towards of a central issue that fol­
lows from attempting to achieve more integrated planning. 
Planning as a technology of government is future-oriented, 
aimed at shaping our world. But, with the world becom­
ing ever-more complex, planning must respond. A world 
increasingly consisting of multiple functionally differen­
tiated subsystems (Axtmann 2004; van Assche and Ver­
schraegen 2008) necessitates planning approaches that are 
more fluid, non-linear and adaptive to the complexity at 
hand (de Roo et al. 2012; Jay 2018; Skrimizea et al. 2019; 
Sørdahl 2023; Zellner and Campbell 2015). In the face of 
this change, practical planning has evolved in both scope 
and nature. Planning, regardless of whether we are talk­
ing about coastal or terrestrial planning, is a ‘coordinating 
arena’ and has, over time, grown to incorporate a vast array 
of different issues and interests. Along with this expansion, 
it has also evolved from being a tool primarily for making 
spatial dispositions towards being a tool for broader societal 
development. Subsequently, the overarching ambition of 
planning then becomes to ensure a ‘comprehensive’ societal 
development. However, ‘comprehensiveness’ (or ‘integra­
tion’) is an intuitively positive, yet hollow, term (Chircop 
and Hildebrand 2006; Sørdahl 2023). It gives no clear guid­
ance as to what planning is to accomplish or how. The use 
of the term ‘comprehensiveness’ might actually disguise 
what is expected from planning, whereas ‘coordination’ and 
‘cooperation’ are more precise. This points at a potential 
‘paradox of integration’: the efforts to handle the increasing 
complexity of the world by seeing more and more things 
in unison, in fact do not necessarily reduce complexity. 
Instead, they bring with them different layers of complexity 
through the introduction of new terms and goals which due 
to their scope are vague in nature, like ‘comprehensiveness’ 
and ‘sustainability’.

The development shown here is an indication of the 
concept of integration persisting through time but being 
reframed from one plan to another. Two significant changes 
stand out: first, there has been a shift in the terms used when 
talking about integrative planning. In the plans adopted in 
2000 and 2008, the terms comprehensiveness or integra­
tion were not used. Rather, these plans talked about the 
need for coordination. In 2015, however, comprehensive­
ness was introduced along with integration, and in 2022, 
comprehensive almost became the sole term used. Second, 
the municipal coastal zone plans themselves have become 
more ‘comprehensive’. This is the case regarding the top­
ics addressed, the documentation and knowledge gathered, 
and the level of participation in the process. The documents 
themselves have become ‘thicker’, where the plan descrip­
tion of 2015 was twice the size of that of the 2000 plan, and 
that of the 2022 plan was triple its size. A consequence of 
the claim for comprehensiveness is an increase in the time 
taken to develop the plan, and at a certain point this became 
part of the rationalisation for improved planning. The most 
recent generations of the plan are also more standardised, 
where coastal zone planning seems to have become profes­
sionalised, more closely follows the national guidelines that 
are being developed and applies a standardised setup.

Regardless of the issues planning is faced with, the response

to achieve it ((Saunders et al. 2019). According to Deacon

the technologies we use. Or, in the words of Scott (2008,

are needed to handle the tasks we have set out to accom­

planning systems’ difficulty of hindering spatial conflicts in 
the coastal zone becomes not a basis for a discussion on 
whether ‘comprehensiveness’ is a fruitful ideal, nor whether 
the planning system can handle all these conflicts. Rather, 
it becomes a foundation for yet more ‘comprehensiveness’ 
and for more topics to be included in the planning systems’

and the fact that it can be regarded as intuitively positive,

interests, using it as a mean to an end that furthers certain 
vested interests. It is perhaps worth being reminded of the 
point made by Foucault, that while everything isn’t neces­
sarily bad, “everything is dangerous” (Foucault 1982). It is 
also important to keep in mind that ‘integration’ does not 
have an endpoint. In planning practice, we cannot envisage 
a situation where we are ‘fully integrated’ (Sørdahl 2023).

chasing the horizon: regardless of how far you go, it is for­
ever out of reach. The planning practices shown here illus­

granted’ truths with which we surround ourselves. Without 
a conscious review of the tools at our disposal and solutions 
we are prescribing, we risk being stuck in a loop, where 
yesterday’s solutions continue to be the de facto answer to 
todays’ problems.
