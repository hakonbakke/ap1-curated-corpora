### Background
FHF asked SALT for a knowledge compilation of seafood industries' sea-area use. Part 1 covered fisheries. This part 2 (SALT 1075, 13 October 2023, project lead Tale Skrove) covers aquaculture today and possible future need, as other sea uses (offshore wind, new species, seabed minerals) compete for space.

### Methods
Legal method is used for the rules. Current footprints come from Fisheries Directorate map data in Yggdrasil. Natural conditions use NorKyst-800 (2018-2022, 25 m depth, three seasons). Change over time compares sites cleared 1991-2010 and still active, sites cleared 2011-2023 and still active, and sites cleared by 2010 then deleted. Three salmonid growth scenarios run to 2050. New-species sales paths run to 2032. Interviews cover submersible, closed, semi-closed and offshore technology: four industry informants (notes from 45-minute digital meetings) and one person each from NFD, the Fisheries Directorate and the Food Safety Authority (recorded and transcribed). The authors exclude land-based hatcheries, on-growing, slaughter and land-based salmon grow-out, and they do not assess environmental impacts other than area. Five locality decisions from 2019-2022 illustrate licensing practice.

### Main findings
Aquaculture is the inverse of fisheries: production is forbidden unless permitted. Municipal coastal plans under the Planning and Building Act, to one nautical mile beyond the baselines, decide which sea can be used. Practice splits between single-use aquaculture zones and larger multi-use zones (the reverse planning principle). The Lurøy plan from Kystplan Helgeland (3 April 2019) is the worked example. A locality also needs county permission after sector consents. Company licences and locality licences are separate. Locality MTB is absolute. As of 31 December 2022 there were 1135 salmonid sea company licences and 989 grow-out sea localities. Figures from 2017 showed about 3.9 times as much locality biomass cleared as company biomass. Since 2017 the traffic-light system steers capacity in 13 production areas, with lice on wild salmonids as the only environmental indicator at the time of writing. The department's powers are "may" rules. Red areas were not cut in 2017. Later rounds did cut them, so far by 6 percent.

Combined salmon, trout and rainbow-trout locality area is given as 94 km2, or 123 km2 and 261 km2 with 20 m and 100 m buffers. Anchors are omitted. The largest blocks are in production area 6 and areas 3 and 4. With illustrative safety radii, occupied share of sea is highest in areas 2 and 3 (prose says about 30 and 50 percent) and generally falls northward. The authors warn that sites in the same infection zone may sit closer.

Other stated uses include algae about 8 km2, blue mussels about 5 km2, and lobster seabed ranching 23 km2. Seabed ranching is not allowed north of Vestfjorden. Salmon localities have become larger and deeper, with a tendency farther from land and a northward shift in new clearances. Chapter 4.2.1 first repeats a 50 to 100 m median-depth band for all production areas, then lists 125 m or 150 m medians in several areas.

If historical area growth continues, occupied area roughly doubles by 2050. All-green 6 percent capacity steps would imply about 126 percent area growth if volume still needs area (doubling by 2046). Past traffic-light colours would imply about 44 percent from 2022 to 2050, with shrinkage in areas 3 to 5 and that path reaching 64 percent of the all-green area. Those two scenarios ignore exception growth under section 12 and shared biomass across production areas.

Twenty-four development-licence awards are cited. Industry and administration expect new technology to complement open pens. Submersible pens were already in commercial use. Closed systems typically need power near land. No offshore farm was yet on its intended offshore site. Two of the three investigation areas overlap wind-power investigations. Administration informants name shrimp fields, wild-fish quality near farms, recreation, shipping, defence, cables and corals as coexistence issues.

Six new sea species are modelled to 2032 from an Akvaplan-niva 2019 score. Cod growth in open pens is quoted from HI 2022 as bottlenecked by missing knowledge on escape, cage spawning and spawning fields. Kelp is treated as the most horizontally area-demanding.

### Uncertainty and limitations
Scenarios two and three assume extra volume needs extra area and ignore site scarcity, feed, other biology and new rules. Safety-zone maps are a pointer, not exclusive legal occupation. NorKyst-800 drops many sites, especially for current. Depth uses 50 m isolines. Deleted-site species are inferred from capacity unit. 517 Trøndelag deletions in 2018 look like a cleanup. Table 6 colour cells are unreadable. Blue-mussel 2022 tonnes differ between Table 9 (2612) and Table 10 (1967). Climate effects (warming, a stated 8 to 14 °C comfort band, combined stressors) are summarised from ClimeFish and related papers, not modelled into the area scenarios. High-resolution coastal climate projections and continuous farm time series are named as missing. Administration interview views are personal.

### Relevance to this corpus
It is the national footprint and scenario text that later regional reports, including Sand 2025 on Trøndelag, cite. Chapter 2 is also a planning-and-licence primer (A1, A2). Chapter 7 interviews add coexistence and technology language (A3, A5). Chapter 9 names postponed climate knowledge (A4).
