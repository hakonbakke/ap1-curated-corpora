### Background
This file is the full digital IPBES Methodological Assessment Report on the Diverse Values and Valuation of Nature (ISBN 978-3-947851-29-4, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.6522522), edited by Balvanera, Pascual, Christie, Baptiste and González-Jiménez. The IPBES Plenary accepted the chapters and approved the summary for policymakers at IPBES 9 in Bonn, 3-9 July 2022. The volume contains the SPM plus six chapters (role of values, conceptualising values, potential of valuation, value expression in decision-making, diverse values in visioning just futures, and policy options and capacity). It is not the same object as the standalone SPM file 2022_ipbes_spm-values.

### Methods
The assessment used about 100 selected experts, about 200 contributing authors, more than 13,000 publications, and a body of indigenous and local knowledge. It synthesises existing knowledge. It does not produce new global value numbers. Chapter 3 inventoried methods in 1,163 in-depth studies drawn from a much larger English-indexed corpus (the SPM text refers to about 79,000 identified studies and about 48,000 with geo-references). Chapter 2 combined scoping, systematic and critical reviews with case studies. Chapter 5 reviewed futures literature and four pathways. Chapter 6 assessed policy instruments, initiatives and capacities. Curation of this extract walked the table of contents and front matter, the SPM key and background messages, and each numbered chapter’s executive findings, confidence language, limitations and conclusions, plus targeted searches and a re-read of Box 2.8 and Box 4.3.

### Main findings
The SPM key messages still hold, and the chapters specify them. KM1 and KM2 tie the biodiversity crisis to political and economic decisions that privilege a narrow, often market, set of nature values, and that have often ignored indigenous peoples’ and local communities’ world-views. A9 (well established) states that market-based instrumental values have been prioritised at the expense of non-market instrumental, relational and intrinsic values. The typology is layered: world-views and knowledge systems, broad values, specific values (instrumental, relational, intrinsic), indicators, and life frames of living from, with, in and as nature. Living from nature has been privileged in research and policy.

KM5 and chapter 3 describe more than 50 methods in four families (nature-based, statement-based, behaviour-based, integrated). Choice among them is a trade-off among relevance, robustness and resources. In the chapter 3 review, nature-based methods are 68 percent of the literature, instrumental values are elicited in 74 percent of valuations, justice is a stated goal in 4 percent, and 77 percent of studies use one main method. High plurality is rare. Aggregation of individual values to a social value has no agreed best practice and can mask minorities and future generations. Method choice itself shapes whose values appear.

KM6 and chapter 4 finding 10 state that less than 5 percent of published valuation studies report uptake in decisions, and that documented uptake has not risen with the literature. A majority of countries had not met Aichi Target 2 by 2020. Chapter 4 adds what the SPM softens: valuation is relevant but insufficient once power, sociality and greenwashing are admitted, and the role of power in selective uptake is a research blindspot. Progress was weaker on Aichi Goals A and B (drivers and cross-sector power) than on targets mainly inside environment ministries. Cursory participation can be harmful.

Whose values are typically ignored or treated as externalities include indigenous and local knowledge holders, relational and intrinsic values that do not travel well in markets, local people affected by conservation or infrastructure when rights are weak, and future generations under discounting. Chapter 3 finds that more than half of studies are not explicit about whose values they represent, and only 1 percent document how power asymmetry was handled. IPLC valuation traditions exist but academic understanding of them is incomplete.

Policy options in chapter 6 are institutional (rights of nature, co-management, alternative economic measures, marine spatial planning, locally managed marine areas). Socio-cultural and customary-rights instruments show higher potential to operationalise diverse values in the chapter’s reading, yet they are used less than legal and economic tools. International initiatives more often claim a diverse-values approach than place-based projects deliver. Four contested pathways in chapter 5 (green economy, degrowth, earth stewardship, nature protection) all call for a more balanced valuation of nature. Constructive dialogue among them is marked unresolved.

Coastal and aquaculture wording in this volume is sparse and must stay bounded. Box 2.8 is a United Kingdom coastal and marine case (144 Community Voice interviews) on fish stocks, marine protected areas, community regeneration and access. Box 4.3 is a Norway legal-hierarchy case (Winge 2017) on implicit valuation in planning and sector acts, with the Aquaculture Act named in the figure. The prose conclusion ranks nature below local development and energy, not farm siting. Figure SPM.3 and Figure 3.11 list aquaculture as one habitat or ecological-context category. Chapter 3 cites Custódio et al. (2020) on cost-based valuation of ecosystem services provided by aquaculture. Chapter 6 notes that monetization such as shrimp aquaculture in mangroves can erode social value.

### Uncertainty and limitations
Confidence language is IPBES four-box language, not a single certainty score. Chapter 1 records that about 96 percent of cited sources are in English and about 73 percent of first authors are affiliated in the Western Europe and Others Group. Grey literature is about 8 percent. The assessment remains primarily framed by a western academic process even after ILK dialogues. Chapter 3 could not generalise IPLC valuation beyond the dialogues and 26 essays it used. Chapter 4.7 flags missing public databases of consultancy valuations and environmental impact assessments, and a gap between studies of uptake and studies of outcomes. Chapter 5 finds marine futures coverage very weak and trade-offs, winners and losers poorly reported. Partner statements (UNESCO, FAO, UNEP, UNDP, CBD) are front matter. This volume does not analyse Norwegian municipal coastal plans or aquaculture site access.

### Relevance to this corpus
Peripheral for A3 (values that enter trade-offs), not a siting, licensing or density paper. The usable Norway residue is Box 4.3 on how planning and sector law silently rank nature against development and energy. The usable coastal residue is Box 2.8 (UK) plus chapter 6’s marine-instrument list. Aquaculture is a study category and a handful of examples, not an object of assessment.

### Relation to other documents
Read with 2022_ipbes_spm-values for the short approved text. The two files are one assessment family, not two independent empirical studies of coastal area. Do not treat this volume as a substitute for Norwegian planning, licensing or coexistence reports in the same room.
