Adaptive co-management in Norwegian aquaculture: insights 
from horizontal and vertical local networks
Hilde Bjørnå1 
Received: 13 June 2025 / Accepted: 10 March 2026 / Published online: 24 March 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
Norwegian salmon aquaculture, a cornerstone of the nation’s economy, faces significant socio-ecological challenges, includ-
ing fish escapes, lice infestations, and environmental conflicts. This study explores how informal, self-organized networks 
contribute to sustainability within the sector, focusing on horizontal collaborations among salmon entrepreneurs and verti-
cal collaborations between companies and municipalities. Drawing on 20 semi-structured interviews and policy document 
analysis from two regions in Northern Norway, the research highlights the role of adaptive co-management in fostering mutual 
learning, sustainability, and adaptability. Horizontal networks excel in knowledge sharing, trust-building, and collective 
action, enabling stakeholders to address biological risks, regulatory pressures, and economic challenges. Vertical networks, 
while more transactional, emphasize public–private collaboration, driving community and economic development through 
projects like property development and place-branding initiatives. However, vertical networks show limited engagement 
with environmental sustainability, often driven by external policies rather than shared long-term goals. The findings align 
with adaptive co-management principles, demonstrating the importance of trust, local knowledge, and iterative adaptation. 
Yet, regulatory constraints and relational dependencies pose challenges, particularly in fostering proactive environmental 
collaboration. This study underscores the complementary strengths of horizontal and vertical networks, offering insights 
into how informal governance systems can enhance resilience and sustainability in aquaculture. These findings have broader 
implications for resource-dependent industries navigating complex socio-ecological landscapes.

Introduction
Norway’s salmon aquaculture industry is the nation’s sec-
ond-largest export sector (Nyrud et al. 2023) and a corner-
stone of economic activity in rural coastal regions. Since its 
emergence in the  1970 s, the industry has expanded rapidly 
and transformed coastal livelihoods, particularly in North-
ern Norway, where aquaculture has partly replaced declin-
ing fisheries and fish-processing industries as a source of 
employment and income (Iversen et al. 2020). At the same 
time, the industry has become increasingly consolidated, 
automated, and capital-intensive, raising questions about 
local value creation, community relations, and long-term 
sustainability (Eriksen and Mikkelsen 2024).
Despite its economic importance, Norwegian salmon 
aquaculture faces persistent socio-ecological challenges. 
Environmental impacts such as fish escapes, salmon lice, 
pollution, and high mortality rates are well documented 
and remain central to public debate and regulatory scru-
tiny (Young et al. 2019; Hersoug et al. 2021; Grefsrud et al. 
2024). These challenges have contributed to conflicts with 
other coastal resource users and have affected the industry’s 
social legitimacy (Bailey and Eggereide 2020; Osmundsen 
and Olsen 2017). As a result, Norwegian aquaculture can be 
understood as a turbulent socio-ecological system in which 
economic growth, environmental risk, and social acceptance 
are tightly intertwined (Mahon et al. 2008).

Northern coastal regions are particularly exposed to these 
dynamics. Alongside aquaculture expansion, these regions 
face broader pressures related to climate change, ecosystem 
degradation, intensified resource extraction, and population 
decline (Rybråten et al. 2018). While aquaculture companies 
vary in size and ownership, individual municipalities often 
depend heavily on a limited number of sites and employers, 
increasing both economic vulnerability and the importance 
of stable industry–community relations. Residents with 
close ties to aquaculture tend to emphasize employment and 
economic activity, even as concerns persist regarding envi-
ronmental impacts and competition with other coastal users 
(Misund et al. 2023; Isaksen and Mikkelsen 2012). Several 
studies describe local attitudes as pragmatic: negative con-
sequences are acknowledged, but community survival and 
regional development are often prioritized (Krøvel et al. 
2019; Lindland et al. 2019; Bjørkan and Eilertsen 2020).
Governance of Norwegian aquaculture is character-
ized by a strong regulatory framework, including licensing 
systems, spatial planning, and environmental monitoring. 
While extensive research has examined formal governance 
structures in Norwegian aquaculture, particularly regulatory 
constraints and planning challenges related to space and site 
allocation, this literature has primarily addressed governance 
at national and sectoral levels, with comparatively less atten-
tion paid to how local actors coordinate and collaborate out-
side formal governance arenas (Hersoug et al. 2021; Young 
et al. 2019). Yet, in practice, aquaculture production depends 
heavily on informal interaction, repeated collaboration, and 
trust-based relationships among companies, municipalities, 
and other local stakeholders.
To address such complex and dynamic challenges, schol-
ars and practitioners have increasingly turned to adaptive 
co-management, a framework that emphasizes collabora-
tion, mutual learning, and resilience in socio-ecological 
systems (Folke et al. 2005; Armitage et al. 2009). Adap-
tive co-management integrates ecological, economic, and 
social dimensions and often combines formal governance 
arrangements with informal, self-organized coordination. In 
this study, formal governance refers to mandated and pro-
cedurally anchored coordination (e.g., licensing procedures, 
hearings, and formal plans), while informal governance 
refers to voluntary, trust-based coordination among actors 
outside formal arenas. Networks are understood as patterns 
of relationships among actors, while collaboration refers to 
coordinated action enabled through these relationships.
This study addresses a key gap in the literature by exam-
ining how informal, self-organized networks operate as 
adaptive co-management systems within Norwegian aqua-
culture. By focusing on horizontal networks among salmon 
farming companies and vertical networks between compa-
nies and municipalities in two regions of Northern Norway, 
the study highlights how local actors navigate environmental 
risk, regulatory pressure, and community expectations 
through informal collaboration. Empirically, the northern 
Norwegian context, characterized by long-term interaction, 
local embeddedness, and strong dependence on aquaculture, 
provides a particularly suitable setting for examining how 
adaptive co-management unfolds beyond formal govern-
ance structures. Figure 1 shows the geographical location 
of the study area in Northern Norway. The highlighted area 
indicates the approximate spatial span of the two case study 
regions, covering parts of Vesterålen and southern Troms, 
including adjacent marine areas.
Specifically, this study asks: How do self-organized 
systems in Norwegian aquaculture foster mutual learning, 
sustainability, and adaptability among local stakeholders? 
Drawing on 20 semi-structured interviews and policy docu-
ment analysis, the study contributes to broader debates on 
adaptive co-management, informal governance, and sustain-
ability in resource-dependent industries.

ingly emphasized collaboration, mutual learning, and 
multi-stakeholder arrangements for addressing complex

environmental challenges. Several influential frameworks 
highlight formal and structured collaborative governance 
processes in which stakeholders engage in joint decision-
making and shared responsibility (Ansell and Gash 2008; 
Davies and White 2012). Adaptive co-management builds on 
these approaches by combining collaboration with learning-
oriented and flexible adjustment under uncertainty (Folke 
et al. 2005; Armitage et al. 2009). It is often described as 
a hybrid governance form, because co-management can 
involve both formal institutions and informal practices 
through which actors coordinate and respond to change 
(Carlsson and Berkes 2005; Berkes 2009).
In this study, formal governance refers to coordination 
that is mandated and procedurally anchored (e.g., licensing 
processes, hearings, formal plans, and compliance routines). 
Informal governance refers to voluntary and self-organized 
coordination that occurs outside formal arenas and is often 
sustained through trust, repeated interaction, and practi-
cal problem-solving. Informal governance does not imply 
the absence of structure; rather, informal coordination may 
become routinized over time and can be central to how gov-
ernance works in practice, especially in contexts character-
ized by uncertainty and rapid change.
A key concept in adaptive co-management is self-organi-
zation, meaning that governance practices and coordination 
may emerge through interaction among stakeholders rather 
than being designed and controlled by a single authority 
(Ostrom 1990; Olsson et al. 2004). Self-organized govern-
ance can be understood as a form of complex adaptive sys-
tem, where actors adjust strategies and relationships over 
time in response to feedback and changing conditions (Lewis 
2017). In this paper, networks refer to relatively durable 
patterns of relationships among actors, while collaboration 
refers to coordinated action that occurs through these rela-
tionships (Berkes 2009).
Networks can take different forms in adaptive co-man-
agement settings. This study focuses on two types that are 
analytically useful for understanding local governance 
dynamics in Norwegian aquaculture: horizontal networks, 
which connect actors operating at the same level (e.g., coop-
eration among salmon companies), and vertical networks, 
which connect actors across levels and institutional roles 
(e.g., cooperation between companies and municipalities) 
(Carlsson and Berkes 2005; Berkes 2009). Both horizontal 
and vertical networks can enable learning and adaptation, 
but they may differ in their goals, incentives, and sustain-
ability priorities depending on actor roles and institutional 
responsibilities.
A core ambition of adaptive co-management is to pro-
mote sustainability in a broad sense by addressing socio-
ecological challenges through collaborative processes and 
learning across knowledge systems (Berkes 2004; Folke 
et al. 2005). Adaptive co-management emphasizes “learning 
to live with change and uncertainty” through iterative inter-
action between management interventions and environmen-
tal feedback (Cleaver and Whaley 2018). By incorporating 
diverse forms of knowledge, including experience-based 
and local knowledge, adaptive co-management may support 
innovation and context-sensitive decision-making (Armitage 
et al. 2009; Berkes 2009).
Trust, familiarity, and goodwill among participants are 
widely identified as crucial conditions for effective collab-
oration (Sharma-Wallace et al. 2018). Trust can facilitate 
information sharing and coordination, particularly when 
actors face uncertainty and risk. Because trust develops over 
time, the effectiveness of co-management collaboration is 
often shaped by historical relationships and prior experi-
ences of cooperation or conflict (Chuenpagdee and Jentoft 
2007).
Key features of adaptive co‑management used 
in this study
To operationalize adaptive co-management in the empirical 
analysis, this study focuses on three key features commonly 
associated with learning-oriented collaboration in socio-
ecological governance: mutual learning, sustainability, and 
adaptability. These features serve as analytical lenses for 
examining how local networks contribute to adaptive co-
management in Norwegian aquaculture.
Mutual learning
Mutual learning refers to interactive learning processes 
through which stakeholders develop shared understandings 
and adjust practices based on experience and exchange. 
In this study, mutual learning is understood as a form of 
social learning, occurring through repeated interaction, joint 
problem-solving, and the circulation of experience-based 
knowledge among stakeholders (Berkes 2009). In aquacul-
ture, mutual learning may involve sharing experiences about 
disease prevention, lice mitigation strategies, crisis response 
routines, or how to navigate regulatory requirements.
Sustainability
Sustainability is understood as the long-term balancing 
of ecological, economic, and social/community goals. In 
aquaculture, sustainability challenges often involve trade-
offs across these dimensions. For example, investments 
in new technology to reduce biological risks and improve 
fish welfare may strengthen environmental and reputational 
sustainability while creating significant economic burdens, 
particularly for smaller companies. In this study, sustain-
ability is examined as a practical and negotiated outcome

Adaptability
Adaptability refers to the capacity of actors and networks to 
respond effectively to change and uncertainty. This includes 
adjusting practices in response to shifting biological con-
ditions (e.g., lice outbreaks, algae events), policy changes 
(e.g., new regulatory requirements), and social expecta-
tions (e.g., community legitimacy concerns). Adaptability 
is closely related to resilience perspectives that emphasize 
adaptive capacity as a key condition for responding effec-
tively to change (Arctic Council 2016). In this study, adapt-
ability is examined not only at the level of individual actors, 
but also as a network-level property enabled by collaboration 
and shared learning.
Together, mutual learning, sustainability, and adaptability 
provide a structured framework for analyzing how horizontal 
and vertical informal networks contribute to adaptive co-
management in Norwegian aquaculture.

Literature review
A substantial body of research has examined adaptive co-
management in fisheries and coastal systems, demonstrating 
both its potential and its limitations. Gutiérrez et al. (2011), 
based on a global analysis of co-managed fisheries, high-
light leadership and social capital as key factors associated 
with successful governance outcomes. Sandström and Rova 
(2010), drawing on cases from Sweden, show how network 
structures can support shared understandings of ecosystem 
conditions and conservation outcomes. Together, these 
studies suggest that adaptive co-management can enhance 
learning, coordination, and responsiveness when social rela-
tionships and governance networks are well aligned.
At the same time, the literature emphasizes that co-
management does not automatically enhance resilience or 
sustainability. Gelcich et al. (2006), in a study of tradition-
ally managed marine ecosystems in Chile, demonstrate that 
the introduction of co-management policies can in some 
cases reduce social–ecological resilience by undermining 
local institutions and customary practices. This work high-
lights how externally driven co-management arrangements 
may constrain local adaptive capacity if they fail to align 
with existing governance systems or ecological dynamics. 
Similarly, Trimble and Berkes (2015), based on case studies 
from Uruguay and Brazil, identify long-standing conflicts, 
mistrust, and tensions between stakeholder groups and gov-
ernment agencies as obstacles to adaptive collaboration. A 
systematic review by d’Armengol et al. (2018) finds that co-
management in small-scale fisheries often generates positive 
social and ecological outcomes, while also noting persistent 
challenges related to conflict resolution, power asymmetries, 
and benefit distribution.
Recent studies continue to refine understandings of adap-
tive co-management by focusing on cooperation dynamics 
and strategic behavior within governance systems. Hidalgo 
et al. (2025) examine shifting cooperation strategies in 
fisheries co-management and show how actors adapt their 
collaborative behavior over time in response to ecological 
change, institutional incentives, and trust relationships. Their 
findings suggest that cooperation in co-management is not 
static, but contingent on evolving social and ecological con-
ditions, and may fluctuate between collaborative and more 
self-interested strategies. This perspective complements 
resilience-oriented approaches that emphasize adaptive 
capacity and the ability of communities and organizations 
to respond effectively to change (Arctic Council 2016).
Empirical research across diverse contexts further illus-
trates these dynamics. For example, Kura et al. (2023)

Methodology
This study employs a qualitative methodology, with its pri-
mary data derived from twenty semi-structured stakeholder 
interviews conducted between August 2023 and November 
2024 across two regions in Northern Norway. A qualitative 
methodology was chosen because it allows for an in-depth 
exploration of stakeholder perspectives, relationships, and 
adaptive practices, which are central to understanding the 
dynamics of adaptive co-management in aquaculture. This 
approach is particularly suited to capturing the nuances of 
informal networks that may not be evident through quantita-
tive methods.
The two regions were selected because they represent 
locally embedded aquaculture systems operating in small 
coastal municipalities, where long-term interaction among 
actors has created strong conditions for informal coordina-
tion. The cases include both smaller owner-operated firms 
and a large locally owned salmon company, allowing for 
examination of adaptive co-management across different 
organizational scales.
To complement the interview material, the study included 
a focused review of secondary data, including fish farm 
sustainability reports, municipal policy documents, official 
hearing documents, and articles from local newspapers in 
the two regions. These sources were used to cross-check and 
contextualize interview findings and to support the interpre-
tation of key themes.
The interviews were conducted by the author, either in 
person or digitally, depending on respondent availability and 
preference. Respondents were identified through purposive 
sampling to ensure representation from key stakeholder 
groups, and snowball sampling was used to identify addi-
tional relevant participants. In total, the study included 
representatives from salmon companies (n = 6), municipal 
political and administrative bodies (n = 7), the supply indus-
try (n = 2), traditional fisheries (n = 2), and local community 
and civil society actors (n = 3). An overview of respondents 
and their roles is provided in the Appendix.
While interviews were adapted to each respondent’s role 
and experience, they followed a common interview guide 
structured around four themes: (1) perceptions of relation-
ships and cooperation, (2) environmental, economic, and 
community sustainability initiatives, (3) economic depend-
ence on aquaculture, and (4) challenges and adaptations. The 
guide also included questions about change over time and 
experiences with multi-stakeholder collaboration.
Interview data were analyzed using a thematic analysis 
approach. Transcripts were coded to identify recurring pat-
terns and themes, which were then grouped into broader 
analytical categories aligned with the study’s conceptual 
framework: mutual learning, sustainability, and adaptabil-
ity. A spreadsheet was used to organize coded material and 
illustrative quotations, supporting systematic comparison 
across respondents and cases.
The study was conducted in accordance with Norwegian 
data protection regulations. The project was approved by 
Sikt, the Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Educa-
tion and Research, which oversees GDPR compliance and 
data storage procedures for research projects. All interview-
ees provided informed consent prior to participation. The 
author was responsible for the design of interview questions, 
data collection, analysis, and interpretation.
Findings
This study identifies two self-organized adaptive co-manage-
ment systems in Norwegian aquaculture: one characterized 
by horizontal interactions and the other by vertical interac-
tions. Both systems contribute to sustainability across envi-
ronmental, economic, and community dimensions.
While some collaborations among salmon companies 
involve formalized agreements or contractual arrangements, 
these collaborations remain informal in governance terms, 
as they are voluntary, self-organized, and operate outside 
mandated public decision-making arenas.

six companies operating in Region A. These companies are 
relatively small compared to the largest aquaculture firms in 
Norway. Some have contractual forms of cooperation that 
include cooperation on management and services, produc-
tion and safety, and development of new ideas and technol-
ogy. Others, involving some of these companies, cooperate 
formalized on supply boats, and a third group cooperates 
more deeply in one part of the region. These groups overlap. 
Moreover, an additional company is loosely knit to this net-
work and is significantly larger and owns substantial parts 
of the supply chain. However, most of these companies are 
relatively small and locally attached to the region through 
local ownership.
The salmon companies share many common interests. 
A key unifying factor is their identity as “locally owned” 
businesses, which sets them apart from many other aqua-
culture enterprises that are shareholder-driven. According to 
these entrepreneurs, being locally owned provides them with 
a unique perspective, as they prioritize the local develop-
ment and prosperity associated with the industry. This focus 
translates into a strong commitment to using local suppliers, 
further reinforcing their ties to the region. This distinct per-
spective in their horizontal collaboration is also recognized 
and confirmed by newspaper articles, regional entities, and 
municipalities in official hearings related to the industry.
Environmental sustainability in horizontal networks
The salmon companies place a strong emphasis on collabo-
ration for sustainability. They must address a range of chal-
lenges, with biological challenges being the most critical. 
Issues such as fish diseases, lice infestations, and escapes 
have long been persistent threats. In recent years, they have 
also faced new challenges, including attacks from algae. 
They do not use antibiotics and have had to develop alterna-
tive solutions, relying heavily on advanced technology and 
vaccines to mitigate these risks.
The network frequently discusses and shares information 
regarding lice infestations, exchanging insights on effective 
and less effective strategies reflecting a strong pattern of 
mutual learning across firms. Companies openly share their 
findings with others, which reflect a strong pattern of mutual 
learning across firms (Informant 9). As one entrepreneur 
explained, “We are interested in the well-being of the fish 
and a good reputation for the industry; of course, we have to 
share such information” (Informant 2).
Members also update one another on their experiences 
with new industry innovations, such as regionally developed 
methods like using burnt limestone in the water, new shield-
ing skirts on cages, and laser technology to combat lice 
infestations (Informant 19). Notably, no one uses medica-
tion treatments due to their broader environmental impacts.
Burnt limestone is an innovative solution currently being 
tested by a company to enhance aquaculture practices. The 
process involves temporarily removing fish from their cages, 
treating the water with burnt limestone, and then returning 
the fish to a cleaner and healthier environment. This method 
is reported to be completely harmless to the fish and ensures 
they thrive in pristine water conditions (Informant 12).
Additionally, newly designed cage skirts have been intro-
duced. These skirts are fastened in a way that enhances their 
durability against harsh climatic conditions while simulta-
neously ensuring adequate oxygen levels in the water. This 
dual functionality promotes both resilience and optimal liv-
ing conditions for the fish.
The salmon industry is a technologically advanced sec-
tor that continuously develops new methods to combat lice. 
Shielding skirts remain the industry standard. However, 
most companies in Region A use laser technology, such as 
the Thermolicer®, and are quite satisfied with this technol-
ogy. Lice removal remains one of the leading causes of fish 
mortality. As one owner admitted, some of the equipment 
used in the past was too harsh on the fish (Informant 13).
The network in Region A is also part of several broader 
networks in the northern area that focus on sustainability. 
For instance, one overarching voluntary network has devel-
oped an emergency response plan to facilitate mutual learn-
ing and resource sharing during crises. Another network col-
laborates on biosafety prevention plans, while a third focuses 
on algae surveillance (Informant 19).
Several companies have collaborated by pooling their 
resources to make significant investments in the develop-
ment of massive, innovative salmon cages designed for 
deeper sea environments (Informant 2). These large-scale 
technological advancements require government-issued 
development licenses. Such licenses aim to address criti-
cal environmental sustainability challenges, including 
issues like lice, fish escapes, and fish waste. Additionally, 
by situating these cages further out at sea, they help miti-
gate the growing scarcity of suitable aquaculture sites. The 
development license has been approved, and construction 
is already underway. This process leverages the expertise 
and knowledge of companies with extensive experience in 
the oil industry. However, the final decision regarding the 
placement of this new innovation is yet to be made (official 
reports).
Another notable example from the environmental sphere 
is the collaboration on risk measures. Companies work 
together to ensure they have the necessary equipment to han-
dle crises effectively. This collaboration extends to assisting 
one another during emergencies, such as algae attacks. In 
such situations, they are allowed to relocate fish cages and 
have, on occasion, lent farming localities to each other. How-
ever, the availability of free localities is limited, and, as one

informant noted, “we most often do not know if we move

The strength of this collaboration became particularly 
evident during an algae attack in Region A in 2019, which 
resulted in the loss of over 12,000 tons of fish. During this 
crisis, the companies coordinated closely to address the 
challenges. One company identified that mature fish were 
dying due to the algae, and there was a significant risk of fish 
cages sinking. This posed a dual threat: the potential escape 
of farmed fish into wild salmon rivers and ocean pollution 
caused by decomposing fish.
While several companies suffered substantial fish losses, 
these were primarily smaller fish, which reduced the risk 
of sinking cages. The 2019 incident was a critical situation 
that demanded an urgent response. Companies collaborated 
to keep the cages afloat, providing assistance to one another 
without charging for their efforts. This collective action 
highlighted the solidarity within the network during a time 
of crisis.
Currently, the region is facing another algae attack. How-
ever, it has been observed that cages equipped with the new 
type of lice skirt—a regional innovation—have been largely 
unaffected. This development is seen as a promising solution 
for the future.
Another example of their commitment to sustainable 
practices is the cleaning and reuse of equipment. While the 
salmon companies do not lend equipment to one another due 
to biosafety concerns, they agree on the importance of clean-
ing and reusing it whenever possible. This approach helps 
minimize waste and promotes greater resource efficiency.
Economic sustainability in horizontal networks
According to the informants, one additional significant chal-
lenge they face is the ever-changing regulatory framework 
combined with slow bureaucratic processes, both of which 
result in high costs. The following quotes illustrate the depth 
of their frustrations:
•	 “This is the most thoroughly regulated industry in Nor-
way.” (Informant 2)
•	 “Some of the regulations are hopeless.” (Informant 2)
•	 “The County Governor’s office (in this particular county) 
obstructs our plans because of unclear rules about cor-
als—it’s never enough.” (Informant 2)
•	 “The reporting and statistics we have to deliver every 
year are insanely massive.” (Informant 17)
•	 “23 different institutions are involved in an aquaculture 
application.” (Informant 2)
•	 “We need a full permit procedure for even minor adjust-
ments, like moving the cages 10 m. The time and exper-
tise this requires is costly for us.” (Informant 13)
•	 “They constantly add new regulations. We don’t mind the 
regulations themselves, but the bureaucracy doesn’t keep 
up.” (Informant 13)
•	 “We are just barely finished with one fight before a new 
one starts. It wears out small participants.” (Informant 
19)
To adapt to these challenges, five of the companies col-
laborate in formalized private cooperation on administrative 
tasks. This approach allows them to achieve economies of 
scale and pool their expertise, enabling them to better navi-
gate the complex regulatory framework.
In the economic sphere, their collaboration also focuses 
on discussing and sharing information about upcoming 
taxes, which they collectively work to oppose through their 
interest organizations and local politicians (Informants 13, 
17, and 19).
The entrepreneurs express significant frustration with a 
new tax (grunnrenteskatten), citing several reasons for their 
discontent. They argue that it drains money from rural dis-
tricts, adds excessive bureaucracy (requiring the submission 
of three full accounts), reduces their profits, increases their 
financial risk, and hinders large-scale investments. Addition-
ally, they believe the method for calculating the tax rate is 
unfair: “They just think of a number and do not consider our 
investments” (Informant 2). Furthermore, it is claimed that 
the tax disrupts the conditions for the supply chain (Inform-
ant 8).
This tax has prompted several adaptations. Naturally, they 
discuss how to respond to it. They believe that the govern-
ment set the profit margin for the biomass (fish in the cages) 
too high. To adjust, they have increased the value in other 
parts of the production chain, such as smolt production and 
fish slaughter. These areas are no longer reported as zero-
sum. Instead, the costs and revenues are distributed across 
different parts of the value chain. “Slaughter is no longer 
reported as zero-sum; it now reflects the actual expenses.” 
(Informant 19). This adjustment has led to an increase in 
prices for items like smolt, which, in turn, has positively 
impacted the overall profitability of the companies.
Notably, they avoid aggressive competition over new 
areas for fish cages. Instead, they may lend areas to each 
other and operate under a mutual understanding: if one 
salmon entrepreneur within this informal horizontal network 
expresses interest in a specific area, the others voluntarily 
refrain from competing for it. This cooperative approach 
is viewed as balancing out over time. In this respect, it is 
important to highlight that the salmon companies do not cur-
rently compete in a traditional market environment. Global 
demand for their products far exceeds supply, and most of 
what they produce is sold on the international market. This 
dynamic further minimizes direct competition among them.

Community sustainability in horizontal networks
In the community sphere, the salmon companies share infor-
mation about their initiatives to support the local populations 
in the areas where they operate fish cages (Informants 13 and 
19). Over time, these initiatives have become increasingly 
similar, and are typically involving activities such as devel-
oping small-scale infrastructure, co-financing local shops 
and festivals, and supporting youth sports programs. Some 
companies in the network have a shared 4-year sponsorship 
agreement with a local cross-country skiing talent. These 
efforts are seen as a way to give back to the community 
while strengthening their ties to the region.
Overall, the horizontal network supports environmental 
sustainability through collaboration on biological risk, eco-
nomic sustainability through shared adaptation to regulatory 
and fiscal pressures, and community sustainability through 
locally oriented sponsorship and development initiatives.
Vertical collaboration
Context of vertical collaboration
The vertical network case involves a collaboration between a 
municipality and a salmon company in Region B. Although 
no formal public–private governance arrangement exists, the 
collaboration operates through informal, trust-based interac-
tion, repeated contact, and mutual accommodation outside 
formal decision-making arenas. At the level of formal roles 
and procedures, the two parties maintain an arm’s-length 
relationship. In practice, however, close informal ties have 
developed between the salmon company’s management/
owner and the municipality’s politicians and administrative 
staff. It is worth noting that these relations were strained in 
the past under a different political majority.
The municipality is moderately sized, with a population 
above the median for Norwegian municipalities, though it 
is not considered large. Historically, it has struggled with 
fiscal challenges. The salmon company plays a pivotal role 
in the local economy, contributing directly to municipal tax 
revenues and indirectly through the tax income generated 
by new residents—often highly skilled professionals in this 
technologically advanced trade—who have moved to the 
area (Informants 3, 5, 6, and 7). Furthermore, the company’s 
reliance on local suppliers has created positive ripple effects, 
enhancing the municipality’s appeal as a place to live and 
work by fostering a dynamic economic environment (ibid).
The salmon company is a large, locally owned busi-
ness that has maintained its ownership structure for several 
decades. It holds a strong position across various parts of 
the supply chain and is highly profitable. According to 
multiple interviews, the collaboration between the munici-
pality and the company is driven by a shared goal: foster-
ing local growth and prosperity.
For the salmon entrepreneur, this goal aligns with their 
philosophy that “this is what local owners want” (Inform-
ant 2) and that “local prosperity is also good for the com-
pany, as it makes us an attractive workplace” (ibid). For 
the municipality, supporting local growth and prosperity 
is a core part of its mandate (Informant 6).
Environmental sustainability in vertical networks
The municipality and salmon company collaborate across 
various spheres of sustainability. In the environmental 
sphere, their collaboration has focused on establishing a 
green transport corridor connecting a municipality with 
a railroad link to Europe (Informant 3). The destination 
municipality also served as the owner of this pilot project, 
which aimed to eliminate the CO2 footprint associated 
with transporting fish to markets. Informants described 
this as an unusual initiative in the Norwegian salmon sec-
tor, as, so far, no other Norwegian salmon companies are 
involved in similar projects. This might be because com-
mercial adoption of new electrified trucks often depends 
on individual transport companies, as salmon companies 
in the Arctic region typically subcontract their transporta-
tion needs (Informants 16 and 13).
Launched in 2022, the project requires significant 
technological innovations, including the development of 
advanced electrified trucks and the establishment of power 
banks, and has therefore engaged a wide range of private 
and scientific partners. The first lightweight electric truck 
successfully completed the route in 2024. However, chal-
lenges remain, such as the difficulty of establishing a 
power bank along the distance between the municipality 
and the railroad link. As a result, this innovation is still 
in its early stages (official documents and news articles).
Another initiative is a common project on waste man-
agement and recycling. This project is however disclosed 
from publicity at this early stage, but the municipal admin-
istration acknowledges that it has gained insights and 
learned from the company’s recycling initiatives (Inform-
ant 3). Mutual learning is also something the chief com-
municator of the salmon company believes is important, 
stating that there is much to learn from the municipality’s 
openness on environmental initiatives (Informant 1).
While both municipal and company representatives 
emphasized learning from each other’s environmental 
initiatives (Informants 1 and 3), municipal informants 
nevertheless tended to frame environmental responsibil-
ity primarily as the company’s obligation.

Economic sustainability in vertical networks
In the economic sphere, there is significant cooperation. 
For example, the municipality and the salmon company 
have engaged in an informal collaboration to develop a new 
municipal harbor they have named “The Environmental Har-
bor.” This initiative was spearheaded by the salmon entre-
preneur, motivated not only by the need to improve infra-
structure but also by a desire to facilitate a land exchange, 
enabling the company to rebuild its aquaculture plant 
(Informants 2 and 5).
The municipality owns the new deepwater harbor, which 
was constructed by blasting rock masses from a nearby 
mountain and using the material to fill the shoreline over a 
large area. The blasting has left a big scar on the mountain, 
which is planned to be restored. The entrepreneur still retains 
ownership of some of the land adjacent to the harbor and 
makes effective use of the new facility. For the municipal-
ity, the harbor has improved access to goods in the area and 
generates revenue, particularly from its use by the salmon 
and fish feed companies.
The municipality is very clear that the salmon company 
plays a significant role in its property development efforts 
due to its status as a cornerstone of the local economy. It 
currently cooperates on the development of the local hospital 
and business development in a remote smaller settlement 
in the municipality (Informant 6). The municipality owns 
a substantial amount of land and has benefited from col-
laborating with the company on property development pro-
jects. As one informant stated: “We have had projects that 
we developed together— they got something out of it, and 
the municipality got something else” (Informant 5).
The municipality’s willingness to accommodate the 
entrepreneur’s needs is further exemplified by its decision 
to allow the salmon company to purchase land designated as 
“open space.” This was permitted despite the municipality 
anticipating that the entrepreneur would likely apply for a 
change in land use to “business purposes” at a later stage. 
However, as one informant explained, changing the status of 
land from “designated open space” to “business purposes” 
requires a formal dispensation process that ensures predict-
ability and equal treatment (Informant 5). As the informant 
noted: “At the same time, we do what we want with munici-
pal property, and we can—and do—take into account the 
ability to follow plans through. We have become increas-
ingly aware that this is important” (Informant 5). It was 
emphasized that the municipality is confident in the entre-
preneur’s ability to follow through on their plans (Informants 
3, 4, and 5).
The strength of their collaboration is particularly evident 
in the mutual trust and respect they have developed. Both 
parties regard one another as reliable partners. This trust has 
become a cornerstone of their relationship, enabling open 
communication and effective collaboration (Informants 1, 
2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7).
Community sustainability in vertical networks
The salmon company and the municipality also collaborate 
in the community sphere. The municipality has spearheaded 
several projects, including a place-branding initiative, the 
creation of a scenic mountain path, and the construction 
of a sports hall. The salmon company has been invited to 
participate in these projects and has taken on an increas-
ingly significant role over time (e.g., Informants 1, 2, and 
3). The company is also the main contributor to local sports 
(Informants 1 and 9) and, as mentioned in the horizontal col-
laboration network, is involved in activities such as develop-
ing small-scale infrastructure, co-financing local shops and 
festivals, and supporting youth sports programs. According 
to the entrepreneurs, these efforts are essential, not only as 
a way to give back to the community, but also as a means of 
strengthening their social license to operate and fostering 
long-term connections with the region.
These practices illustrate that vertical network enhances 
economic sustainability through collaborative infrastructure 
projects, contributions to environmental sustainability, and 
to the local community.
Horizontal and vertical collaboration
These findings illustrate the diverse ways in which hori-
zontal and vertical networks contribute to sustainability in 
Norwegian aquaculture. While horizontal networks excel in 
fostering collaboration and innovation among peers, vertical 
networks highlight the importance of informal collabora-
tions between salmon companies and municipalities. The 
following discussion explores how these findings align with 
the principles of adaptive co-management and contribute to 
the broader understanding of sustainability in socio-ecolog-
ical systems.
Discussion
Key findings
This study set out to explore how self-organized systems in 
Norwegian aquaculture foster mutual learning, sustainabil-
ity, and adaptability among local stakeholders. The findings 
demonstrate that horizontal and vertical networks contribute 
to these goals in distinct but complementary ways.
The horizontal network is particularly notable for its 
emphasis on knowledge sharing and mutual learning. Par-
ticipants in this network prioritize collaboration over com-
petition, fostering trust and mutual support. Over the years,

and pooling resources to address natural and regulatory 
challenges. This cooperation is especially active within the 
environmental and economic dimensions of sustainability, 
serving as a critical safety net during natural crises. By lev-

effectively to changing economic conditions and external

In contrast, the vertical network operates in a more trans-
actional manner. According to informants, there is limited 
dependency or active learning between the parties involved. 
However, trust and a deep understanding of each other’s 
needs are evident. This mutual adaptation is reflected in the 
way municipalities and companies respond to each other’s 
requests: when the municipality requires something, the 
company complies, and vice versa. This form of cooperation 
is most prominent in the economic and community sphere 
of sustainability. For example, the company’s strong com-
munity engagement in municipal initiatives, such as place-
branding and scenic road projects, underscores the impor-
tance of this relationship.
Alignment with adaptive co‑management principles
These findings support previous research on adaptive co-
management across several dimensions. They reveal the 
presence of both formal and informal, as well as horizontal 
and vertical, forms of local cooperation that significantly 
impact sustainability. Moreover, the actors within these net-
works actively share knowledge and resources. As noted by 
Ostrom (1990), these networks lack a central director and 
often appear “invisible” to the public, yet they contribute to 
the system by enabling dynamic adaptation to challenges.
The findings from these cases demonstrate that aqua-
culture collaborations have developed the capacity to live 
with change and continuously adapt. This is evident in their 
innovation of new technologies and their ability to respond 
to new policies, such as the introduction of a new tax regime 
and efforts to reduce CO2 emissions. These observations 
align with the findings of Armitage et al. (2009), who 
emphasize the importance of adaptability in collaborative 
systems.
The role of local knowledge in adaptive co-management 
has been widely recognized as a critical factor for foster-
ing resilience and sustainability (Berkes 2009). In the cases 
studied, local knowledge is present but appears to be utilized 
indirectly rather than explicitly. This indirect use of local 
knowledge manifests in the way actors rely on their accumu-
lated experiences and tacit understanding of local conditions 
to navigate challenges and adapt to change. For instance, 
entrepreneurs in the horizontal network are acutely aware of 
their dependence on mutual assistance during crises, such 
as algae attacks. This awareness stems from years of shared 
experiences and collaborative problem-solving, which have 
cultivated an implicit understanding of the value of collec-
tive action. Similarly, in the vertical network, aquaculture 
entrepreneurs demonstrate an understanding of the expecta-
tions of the local population and municipality. This knowl-
edge, while not formally codified or explicitly discussed, 
has been shaped by past interactions and experiences. For 
example, entrepreneurs have learned over time what types 
of community engagement are valued and how to align their 
actions with municipal priorities. This suggests that local 
knowledge is embedded in the relationships and practices 
of the network, even if it is not formally acknowledged or 
systematically documented.
The findings also support the argument made by Folke 
et al. (2005) regarding the importance of leveraging diverse 
knowledge systems and experiences to develop shared 
understandings and policies. In the horizontal network, for 
instance, participants share their experiences with different 
approaches to combating lice problems, fostering a collec-
tive understanding that such information should be openly 
exchanged. This openness to sharing innovations, such as the 
use of new lice skirts or laser-based lice removal systems, 
demonstrates how mutual learning can enhance environmen-
tal sustainability. In the vertical network, the collaboration 
between public and private actors is similarly attuned to the 
local economy and the well-being of the community, dem-
onstrating an alignment of goals across sectors.
Sharma-Wallace et al. (2018) argue that trust, familiarity, 
and goodwill among participants are critical for successful 
collaboration. The findings here strongly support this asser-
tion, as the relationships studied are characterized by high 
levels of trust and are perceived as highly effective. These 
collaborations have developed over many years, further rein-
forcing the idea that the success of collaboration is path-
dependent and deeply influenced by the historical context of 
the cases, as noted by Chuenpagdee and Jentoft (2007). For 
example, the trust and mutual adaptation evident in the verti-
cal network reflect years of consistent interaction between 
the municipality and the salmon company, enabling them 
to work together on community and economic initiatives.
Additionally, the findings highlight the iterative nature 
of adaptive co-management, where stakeholders continu-
ously adjust their practices in response to changing condi-
tions. This is particularly evident in the horizontal network, 
where companies collaborate to address biological risks and 
regulatory challenges. The ability to experiment with new 
technologies, share lessons learned, and adapt to external 
pressures underscores the dynamic and flexible nature of 
these networks.
In summary, the findings align closely with the principles 
of adaptive co-management by demonstrating the impor-
tance of collaboration, trust, mutual learning, and adapt-
ability. Both horizontal and vertical networks contribute to

Divergences from the co‑management perspective
While the two cases align with many findings in the litera-
ture, some aspects diverge or challenge established perspec-
tives on adaptive co-management. Most notably, the vertical 
network demonstrates a limited and sporadic focus on envi-
ronmental sustainability. Adaptive co-management funda-
mentally aims to address resource challenges through col-
laborative processes that promote ecologically sustainable 
livelihoods (Berkes 2004; Carlsson and Berkes 2005; Folke 
et al. 2005). However, in the vertical network, environmental 
initiatives, such as the electrification of transport systems, 
appear to be driven more by external climate policies than 
by a shared, long-term environmental vision. This suggests 
that environmental sustainability has yet to become a central 
focus of the vertical collaboration.
One explanation for this limited engagement with envi-
ronmental sustainability lies in the highly regulated nature 
of the Norwegian aquaculture industry, as highlighted in the 
empirical findings. Municipal actors perceive environmental 
concerns, such as improving biodiversity in marine ecosys-
tems, to be addressed through formal hearing procedures 
related to the allocation of new sites. This reliance on formal 
processes may limit opportunities for proactive, collabora-
tive environmental initiatives within the vertical network. 
Instead of fostering innovation, the regulatory environment 
may inadvertently reinforce a compliance-driven approach, 
where actors focus on meeting minimum standards rather 
than exceeding them.
The transactional nature of the vertical network further 
complicates its ability to address long-term environmen-
tal goals. The collaboration is largely focused on meeting 
immediate needs, with municipalities prioritizing economic 
development and community well-being, while private com-
panies concentrate on regulatory compliance. While this 
dynamic fosters trust and mutual understanding, it may not 
provide the momentum required for ambitious, long-term 
environmental initiatives. This contrasts with the horizontal 
network, where mutual learning and shared goals drive col-
laboration and innovation.
Power dynamics also play a significant role in shaping 
the vertical network. The municipality wields considerable 
authority in allocating new sites for fish farming, a resource 
that is becoming increasingly scarce (Hersoug et al. 2021). 
Conversely, the salmon entrepreneur holds substantial influ-
ence as a key player in the local economy, upon which the 
municipality heavily depends for economic ripple effects. 
This interdependence fosters trust but also limits the will-
ingness of either party to address contentious issues, such as 
stricter environmental standards. As Plummer and Armitage 
(2007) suggest, such power imbalances can lead to co-opta-
tion, where dominant actors absorb each other’s ideas and 
avoid addressing issues that might disturb the relationship.
These divergences highlight the challenges of integrat-
ing environmental sustainability into collaborations that 
are primarily focused on economic and community goals. 
Without a shared environmental vision, vertical networks 
may struggle to address long-term ecological challenges, 
such as biodiversity loss or climate change. Additionally, 
the reliance on formal regulatory processes underscores a 
tension between compliance and innovation. While regula-
tions are essential for ensuring accountability, they may also 
constrain the flexibility and creativity needed for adaptive 
co-management to thrive.
In summary, while the vertical network demonstrates 
strengths in fostering trust and mutual adaptation, its limited 
focus on environmental sustainability and its transactional 
nature highlights key divergences from the ideals of adap-
tive co-management. Addressing these gaps will require a 
shift toward more proactive and integrated approaches to 
environmental collaboration.
Potentials of horizontal and vertical collaborations
These findings suggest that horizontal and vertical net-
works offer distinct but complementary contributions to 
sustainability in Norwegian aquaculture. Horizontal net-
works excel in fostering mutual learning, trust, and collec-
tive action. Through knowledge sharing and collaboration, 
these networks have demonstrated their ability to address 
shared challenges, such as biological risks and regulatory 
pressures. Some have formalized certain aspects of their 
collaboration—for example by pooling resources for large-
scale innovations—and by this expanded their contributions 
to sustainability. Government incentives, such as grants for 
collaborative environmental projects, could further encour-
age these efforts.
Vertical networks, on the other hand, are particularly 
effective in aligning local economic priorities with commu-
nity needs. Collaborative initiatives, such as the develop-
ment of “The Environmental Harbor” and place-branding 
projects highlight the potential of public–private collabora-
tion to drive local growth and prosperity. However, their 
contributions to environmental sustainability remain limited, 
and are primarily driven by external policies rather than a 
shared vision. To address this, municipalities and compa-
nies could co-develop long-term environmental plans that 
align local priorities with broader climate goals. Agreements

their complementary strengths and fostering deeper collabo-

to drive transformative change in Norwegian aquaculture, 
ensuring its sustainability for future generations.
Conclusion
This study has explored local adaptive responses in Nor-
wegian aquaculture through the lens of adaptive co-man-
agement, shedding light on how flexible, locally embedded 
resource management systems can drive sustainability. By 
examining both horizontal and vertical networks, the find-
ings underscore the pivotal role of informal collaborations in 
fostering mutual learning, adaptability, and resilience.
A key contribution of this research is its demonstration 
that informal networks serve as effective buffers against 
external shocks, particularly in the environmental and eco-
nomic dimensions of sustainability. Horizontal networks 
excel in building trust, facilitating knowledge exchange, and 
enabling collective action to address shared challenges such 
as biological risks and regulatory pressures. Meanwhile, 
local vertical networks emphasize mutual adaptation and 
community well-being, highlighting the value of trust-based 
public–private relationships. Together, these networks illus-
trate how integrating environmental, economic, and social 
dimensions can lead to more holistic and resilient aquacul-
ture practices.
Nevertheless, the study has its limitations. Its focus on 
two regions in Arctic Norway may constrain the applicabil-
ity of the findings to other contexts, especially larger, more 
industrialized aquaculture systems. Future research should 
investigate how similar networks operate in diverse regula-
tory and cultural settings, as well as how they adapt over 
time to long-term challenges such as climate change and 
shifting market dynamics.
This research underscores the significant potential of 
self-organized networks in advancing sustainability within 
the aquaculture sector. By fostering trust, leveraging local 
knowledge, and embracing adaptive, flexible approaches, 
these networks contribute to resilience in ways that formal 
governance structures alone often cannot achieve. These 
insights offer a valuable analytical framework not only for 
aquaculture but also for other resource-dependent industries 
striving to balance environmental, economic, and social pri-
orities in an era of rapid change.
For policymakers and practitioners, the implications are 
clear: the value of these networks must be recognized and 
supported. This could involve creating platforms for col-
laboration, incentivizing innovation, and aligning regulatory 
frameworks with the needs and capacities of local stake-
holders. By doing so, they can help unlock the full potential 
of adaptive co-management to build more sustainable and 
resilient resource systems.
