### Background
Schütz and Slater review marine governance of aquaculture facilities and offshore wind farms in Norway and Scotland. The legal-values question is whether the systems can keep predictability (stability for investment and established rights) while remaining adaptable to natural change, new technology and multi-use. Aquaculture is treated as a mature inshore sector. Offshore wind is treated as an emerging sector. The study is offered as a lesson for Arctic and northern Atlantic management.

### Methods
Functional comparison of structures and rules that fulfil the same tasks in the two jurisdictions: strategic marine plans, marine spatial planning (MSP) and licensing. Norway is a civil-law system. Scotland is mixed common and civil law. No wind farms were under construction in Norway at the time of writing. Beatrice Wind Farm in Scotland (latitude 58) is named as under construction. Marine Policy 110 (2019) 103556. Received 10 May 2019, accepted 21 May 2019. Funded by the Research Council of Norway, grant 255586.

### Main findings
Both countries run a patchwork of national, regional and local competences, land-sea interface and licences, plus a vertical hierarchy of national strategic guidance. One-size-fits-all policies are judged ineffective because the two sectors grew inside different policy frames. Even so, Norway and Scotland took parallel policy approaches to aquaculture and to offshore energy respectively.

Norwegian aquaculture is described as inshore until now. Production areas are said to increase predictability for fish farmers, while what will be decisive in a given case can still be unpredictable. Municipal land-use planning under the Planning and Building Act coordinates aquaculture with other uses. Regional thematic county plans exist but are not a full substitute. Integrated management plans for large marine ecosystems have no legal binding force in Norway in the same way as some Scottish instruments.

Licensing in both systems can promote predictability by clear wording and also create unpredictability where tests such as 'significant' effects are open. Changes in mature aquaculture management are described as slow and grounded in democratically adopted laws, which the authors treat as predictability in a broader sense.

### Uncertainty and limitations
Brief comparative review. Nuances are stated as lost. No new occupancy or licence-outcome dataset. Supplementary data are not in this extract.

### Relevance to this corpus
This is the legal text that places aquaculture next to offshore wind as competing and coexisting marine uses, and that names predictability versus adaptability as the live tension in plans and licences.
